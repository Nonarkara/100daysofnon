## Tradition-boundary honesty note — first-class

**This is a Thai Buddhist teaching, not a Daoist one.** The tradition is Theravada Buddhism (South Asian roots via Sri Lanka), with distinctive concepts: *anatta* (non-self), *tanha* (craving), *dukkha* (suffering), *anicca* (impermanence). The specific "ชีวิตจะกัดเจ้าของ" framing sounds like a modern Thai lay-teacher coinage — possibly in the lineage of Buddhadasa Bhikkhu, Ajahn Chah's Western students, or contemporary authors like Wor Wachiramethi (ว. วชิรเมธี).

**Filing this to the Dao De Jing project needs a careful move:** *there is real cross-traditional convergence on practical wisdom (non-clinging, working with the flow, ego-reduction), but the metaphysics differ.* Chinese Chan/Zen Buddhism historically absorbed heavy Daoist influence, so the boundary is porous — but not erased.

**What this input contributes to the Dao project is not "Laozi said this too" — it is "another tradition converged on practical wisdom Laozi articulates from its own metaphysics."** The e-book should treat this convergence honestly, not collapse the traditions.

## The chapter-by-chapter mapping where the convergence is legitimate

### Chapter 44 (名與身孰親,身與貨孰多,得與亡孰病) — the direct convergence

> *"Which is dearer, name or self? Which is worth more, self or goods? Which is worse, gain or loss?"*

**Points 1, 3, 4, 6, 7 all map directly here.** Laozi is asking exactly the question the Buddhist points answer: attachment to name / goods / gain produces suffering. **Chapter 44 is the strongest Dao De Jing anchor for this input.**

### Chapter 33 (勝人者有力,自勝者強) — for point 9

> *"Overcoming others requires force; overcoming oneself requires strength."*

The book's point 9 (train the mind to be empty of ego) is the Buddhist framing of Laozi's self-conquest teaching. Different metaphysics, same practical direction.

### Chapter 22 (曲則全) — for point 8

> *"Yielding preserves."*

The book's point 8 (see life as it actually is, stop fighting what you can't control) maps to Laozi's yielding-as-preservation. Both traditions arrive at the same practical instruction from different starting points.

### Chapter 11 (三十輻共一轂,當其無,有車之用) — for points 6, 7, 9

> *"Thirty spokes converge on a single hub; the hub's usefulness comes from its emptiness."*

The book's emphasis on reducing / emptying / letting go rather than adding — Chapter 11's emptiness-as-usefulness. **This is the pattern [[Dao Input 09 IC 555]] already established** for the modern-object case; the same Chapter-11 anchor now applies to the individual-practice case.

### Chapter 76 (人之生也柔弱,其死也堅強) — for point 2

> *"When alive, humans are soft and yielding; when dead, they are hard and stiff. The soft and yielding is the companion of life; the hard and stiff is the companion of death."*

The book's point 2 (don't get attached to age) resonates with Laozi's soft-vs-stiff framing — resisting the natural softening of age is holding stiffness that is closer to death than to life.

### Where the traditions diverge, not converge

**Points 4 and 9 ("me" and "mine" as illusion, ego as emptiness)** are Buddhist *anatta* — the doctrine that there is no permanent self. **Daoism does not go this far.** Laozi's teaching preserves a *ziran* (自然) natural self that flows with the Dao; it does not dissolve the self entirely. **The Buddhist points 4 and 9 are stronger metaphysical claims than the Dao De Jing makes.** The e-book should note this — Buddhism goes further than Daoism on the self question.
