## CH14 — The Convenience

At 4:55 on the morning of Thursday the twenty-sixth of November I checked the forecast the way another man might check a pulse. Zero percent, the app said, a small cartoon sun already smug about it, no cloud logged anywhere on the radar between the Gulf and the hills, dry season locked in for five more months without exception, as it does every year at this latitude. Ninety-seven days since the photograph. I had stopped, sometime in the last two weeks, being the kind of man who says that sentence out loud. I still knew the number. Some men keep a phone number for a house they no longer live in.

I left the apartment with nothing. This took some deciding. Other nights I had carried instruments — the notebook, the taped kit, a strip of receipt paper folded for the jamb, the bent glasses corrected with pliers to eleven degrees instead of fifteen. Tonight I patted my pockets at the door out of pure animal habit and found phone, wallet, keys, and stood there a moment with the fourth pocket empty, Direk's ledger still on the kitchen table where I had left it four days running, the dog-eared page still dog-eared, still blank. I did not go back for it. I am aware of what that refusal cost me to perform and what it will look like on the page: a man finally travelling light. I only know that when I reached for the ledger my hand changed its mind on the way, which had not happened to my hand before.

Direk had been cremated the Sunday before, at Wat Hua Lamphong, three nights of chanting compressed by nobody's request into the single evening his niece could manage, and I had gone on the third night with a hundred and twenty baht of mandarins from Sam Yan, the same stall, the same price, which had stopped surprising me by then. The abbot who spoke read from a sheet, reading glasses pushed onto his forehead, a man who had already memorized the passage and was only using the page for the family's comfort. Nine items, the niece had said, numbered, in his own hand, found in the top drawer. The Go board went into the coffin sealed shut, stones separated first into their two bowls, the argument closed before it was filed. The photograph of two men in front of a banner in Osaka stayed in the room, face down, per instruction, on a table that would be someone else's table within the month. I had not opened the ledger since. I had thought about it every day and not opened it, which is its own kind of arithmetic.

Charoen Krung at five in the morning belongs to trucks. I walked the last stretch rather than pay a driver to ask me, again, whether my business with an abandoned building was legal, and the gap behind the chained noodle cart took me without argument at 5:07, the same two sheets of hoarding overlapping the same negligent centimeter they had overlapped in November's first week, and would go on overlapping, I assumed, until somebody either finished the tower or tore it down — two outcomes the building itself seemed entirely indifferent between. The dog was there. Ginger, medium, unsurprised, exactly as before, and this time it came further — past the second floor, where it had stopped in November, on up through a stretch of landings where the paint numbers ran two floors optimistic, keeping four steps behind me instead of walking me, until somewhere around the twenty-sixth it lost interest, the specific administrative disinterest of an animal that has satisfied itself a stranger's business does not concern it, and turned and went back down at its own pace, unhurried.

I reached forty-four at 5:51. Forty-four minutes, three fewer than the last time, which I noted and then, deliberately, did not write down.

The building teaches you, if you climb it twice, that the first ascent is the only one you experience as vertical. The second time you understand it as what it actually is, which is horizontal — the same floor, repeated, forty-four times, each one a decision the builder made about how a life on that elevation ought to be organized, and each one abandoned in the middle of being made. I know this building's grammar because it is my old grammar; I spent the better part of a decade teaching students to read exactly this kind of sentence, and this one has a detail I had not let myself look at closely in November, in the dark, in a hurry to reach whatever was waiting above me. On the raw concrete of the stair walls, where the finish coat was never applied and never will be, the grain of the wooden formwork is still legible — the plywood boards that held the wet concrete in its shape while it set, forty years ago, stripped away the moment the concrete no longer needed them, leaving their pattern pressed permanently into the thing they had made. You can run a hand down it and feel the knots. Every building in this city has that pattern hidden somewhere behind a skim coat and a coat of paint and a coat of marketing; this one alone kept it on the surface, because nobody ever came back to finish the surface, which means nobody ever came back to lie about how the thing was actually made. A rendering wants you to forget the scaffolding. This building forgot to hide it, and in forgetting became, of every tower on this river, the only one that told the truth about what held it up while it was still soft. I put my palm flat against a stretch of it on the thirty-ninth floor landing, felt the grain of boards that had been kindling for four decades, and kept climbing.

The garlands were still on the rebar where I had found them in November, five of them, and — I saw this only now, at closer range, with the phone's light steady on it rather than shaking from a climb — they had been refreshed. Not the same flowers. New string, new dok rak, white and firm, the old ones presumably gone into the bucket of two-fingers water to compost, quietly, on somebody's schedule, without being asked why. I did not ask why. In August I would have taken the light apart photon by photon looking for the seam where the impossible one got in. Tonight I put my fingers on a petal, felt the same coolness I remembered from November, and took my hand back, and understood nothing, and let the not-understanding sit in me without turning it into a project. This may be the only thing I have learned this year that I would call, without irony, growth. A man can go a long way on not needing to know who keeps the flowers fresh.

The chair was where it had been. The wooden one stood at its twelve centimeters, angled toward the headrest, patient as furniture. Mor Nok was sweeping — I use his name now, though I want to record honestly that I still do not remember the exact hour I learned it; it arrived in my head sometime between November and tonight, the unremarkable way names arrive once you have stopped needing an occasion for them — the same stretch of slab toward the open edge, in the trousers and undershirt he seemed to own in some quantity, and he did not startle at my light this time either, which told me he had heard the dog leave two floors below and had known, for that long, that I was coming.

"You came back with nothing," he said, before I had said anything.

"I noticed that myself on the stairs."

He leaned the broom against the rebar, next to the garlands. "Last time you asked if the chair was connected to anything." He looked at it, and then at me. "Wrong verb, I told you."

"I've been working on the verb."

"Good." He sat on the edge of the wooden chair himself, which I had not expected — I had thought of it as furniture reserved for someone else, and instead here he was, an old man taking the visitor's seat in his own operating theater, hands on his knees. "What is it, then. The chair."

I had not actually asked. He answered the question I would have gotten around to eventually, the way he answered most things up here — ahead of schedule, on his own terms.

"People come up here to have something extracted," he said. "Most of the time it isn't what they thought it was."

"What was it for the people it was?"

"Different for each of them. That's the part nobody wants to hear. They want a menu." He tapped the arm of his own chair once, a dry sound. "I pulled teeth for thirty-one years in a shophouse near the flower market, before the license, and then without it, and I can tell you the same thing about teeth. A man comes in swearing the pain is in the back left. Half the time it's the front right, referred, and you learn to distrust where a patient points before you learn anything else in the trade."

"And this. Up here. What is it, exactly, if it isn't teeth."

He considered the question the way he'd considered the river before, unhurried, a man for whom hurry had stopped paying. "A convenience," he said. "That's the honest word for it." He nodded at my empty hands. "You brought less than most."

I looked at the chair a while. The vinyl held the cracks and the crosshatched tape I remembered, and the pump handle sat at the same angle it had in November. Nobody had reclined it since, most likely.

"Did she ever sit in it," I said. I did not qualify who.

Mor Nok did not ask either. "Once," he said. "Two years before the end. She came up here on her own, no note, found the stairs off somebody's ledger, I assume, though not the same somebody — mine's been dead longer than yours." He looked at the river a moment. "She sat in the chair for perhaps ten minutes and did not ask me to do anything to her. She came up asking your verb." He turned back to me. "She left asking a better one."

"What did she look like. Up here. That night."

He was quiet long enough that I thought he had decided, after all, not to answer. "Like a woman doing arithmetic she'd already finished and was only checking," he said. "She thanked me on the way out. Nobody thanks the sweeper. I've thought about that more than I've thought about the rest of it."

"What was hers."

"That's not for me to hand you," he said, without unkindness, flat and procedural, a form he had clearly read out many times before. "You'll get your own, or you won't, and either way it won't be hers. That's the part people also don't want to hear. The chair doesn't issue the same question to two people. It isn't that kind of instrument."

I sat down in it.

I had not decided to. My legs decided that, a forty-four-minute stair climb and a year like this one having their say before I did, and I found myself reclined at some angle I did not measure, looking up past the exposed rebar and the ranks of Corinthian columns holding up nothing, at a sky that had gone from black to the specific charcoal it goes before it commits to anything. The headrest was cold, and then, briefly and without ceremony, it was not cold anymore, an object that had simply stopped being strange. Below the open bay the river ran black and businesslike, same barge traffic, same unhurried freight moving upstream at the pace of a thing with nowhere urgent to be and every reason to keep moving anyway. Somewhere past Klong Toey the port burned its low sodium orange over a building I now knew the inside of down to its floor plan and its shift schedule and the smell of its air conditioning, still set for fish. I could not see Cognita's roofline from there. I found myself looking for it anyway, and then, deliberately, stopped.

Mor Nok took up the broom again and went back to his stretch of slab, not watching me, giving me the specific privacy of a man doing his own work three meters away.

I lay there and did not ask if the world was real. I had asked some version of that question, in one register or another, for ninety-seven days, to Direk, to Golf, to a woman crying into a shoulder that was not offering comfort so much as absorbing it, to a photograph, to a closet with a fan in it, and every answer I had gotten back — the timezone, the peripheral, the golden set, the queued message I chose not to open — had closed clean and left a flower on a nightstand nobody could account for. I understood, finally, lying in a stranger's dental chair on the forty-fourth floor of an unfinished building at six in the morning, that I had been asking the wrong thing the entire time, exactly as Mor Nok kept telling me, and that the right question had been sitting under the wrong one the whole while, patient, waiting for me to climb enough stairs to find it.

"If everything is already written," I said, to the river, to Mor Nok, to whoever was going to answer, because by then I had stopped expecting the asking and the answering to come from the same direction, "why show me anything at all."

Nobody answered from the direction I was facing.

It came from the stairwell behind me — the same stairwell I had just climbed, footsteps I had not heard arrive, unhurried, the tread of a man who had all the time this building had ever had. I turned my head against the cold vinyl. A green jacket stood in the stairwell doorway with a helmet held under one arm, bare-headed for the first time in ninety-seven days of green jackets, and the landing light — what little of it the sky was starting to give — did not reach his face. I want that on the record precisely: the helmet came off, and it changed nothing. I still cannot tell you what he looks like. What I can tell you is that he arrived at the exact second the question finished leaving my mouth, not a beat before, not a beat after — the same instinct for the turning frame that put Pui three chairs ahead of every other annotator on that floor, the frame nobody else could find until the model had already found it for them. I did not think that thought all the way through at the time. I am thinking it now, writing this down. I can tell you what he said, because he said it once, in a voice with no particular accent and no particular hurry, the only seven words I would ever hear from him in a year that had given me a great many words from a great many other sources.

"The script still needs one honest reader."

Then his right glove went to the delivery box that was not on his back this time — he had come up forty-four floors on foot, apparently, carrying nothing that would explain what came out of his hand next — and produced a slip of thermal paper, warm as it was always warm from him, printed by no printer I have ever located, and held it out.

I sat up to take it. Mor Nok had stopped sweeping.

One decision. No charge. The paper gave its heat to my fingers over about the same ninety seconds it always takes, and by the time it had gone cool the stairwell doorway held nothing but the doorway.

I stood up out of the chair.

I want to be exact about what that was and was not. It was not defiance; there was nothing in the room demanding defiance. It was not refusal, because nobody had asked me to stay reclined, and Mor Nok had never once, in either visit, told me the chair required anything of the person in it beyond the sitting. It was the smallest possible motion — weight forward onto the feet, hands off the cracked armrests, the hydraulic pump giving one small mechanical sigh as it took the released weight, the same sigh, I imagine, it has given for every patient in thirty-one years who ever got up under their own power instead of being wheeled — and it did not feel, from the inside, like anything larger than standing up. I have stood up out of chairs my entire life. I did not expect this one to be different, and from the inside it was not.

Mor Nok watched me do it with the broom idle in both hands, and something in his face — I could see his face; it was only the Rider's I could never see — arranged itself into an expression I would call, if I had to file it, respect withheld pending confirmation.

"That one," he said, "was not in the game records."

I did not ask him what game he meant. I think by then I had finally learned the lesson the receipts had been billing me for since August, one non-transaction at a time: some sentences are complete the moment they are said, and asking them to explain themselves is a tax you no longer have to pay.

I put the receipt in my shirt pocket, folded once, the same pocket that had carried a hundred-baht lottery ticket in November and, before that, nothing worth mentioning for forty-three years. Somewhere in the apartment, in a drawer, sealed in their zip-lock bags with their masking-tape dates, were the others — a longan drink I never bought, priced at twenty-five baht for the considering; a question about Pui the printer had misspelled as fertilizer; a door nobody had knocked on; a lesson with no branch number. Three of those had run their total to zero exactly, the longan drink the sole holdout with a real price on it. This one made a fourth zero and a fifth receipt altogether, and I understood, folding it in with the rest, that I had stopped needing to know who kept that particular ledger either.

The garlands moved once in a draft off the river, all five together, strung on the same length of line and answering to it. Mor Nok picked his broom back up.

"Go home," he said. "You climbed for nothing tonight. That's usually how you know it worked."

The descent took twenty-two minutes, which I only know because I checked the time once at the top and once stepping through the gap behind the noodle cart, and did not check it in between — a way of measuring a duration I had never previously permitted myself. Going down, the building's grammar reverses; you read the same forty-four repeated floors in the other order, each one a decision walked away from instead of walked into, and the Corinthian columns holding up nothing went past me at a pace that made them look, for one flight, almost busy. The vendor was two hours from setting up, her stools still stacked and cabled to the cart's frame. A street sweeper three doors down worked a section of Charoen Krung with a broom identical to Mor Nok's, twigs bound to a long handle, the same unhurried stroke, and I did not point out the coincidence to either of them, because by then I had a policy about that.

The street held the particular grey Bangkok gets in the last half hour before commitment, buildings all outline and no color, and I had gone maybe forty meters toward Sathorn when the sky, having promised zero percent at 4:55 and meant it for every hour since, opened without any transition I could locate — no gathering, no distant thunder walking closer, no darkening in advance of itself. It simply rained, suddenly and completely, every argument that might have led up to it apparently already settled somewhere out of sight.

It rained hard, straight down, no wind behind it to give it an angle, drops fat enough to sound individually against the corrugated hoarding before the individual sounds ran together into one continuous roar that filled the whole width of the soi. I stood in it. I had a phone in one pocket and a receipt for a decision in the other and I did not move toward either awning on the block. Charoen Krung's shophouses ran dark and shuttered on both sides, water already finding the low points in the road, streetlight breaking into long amber threads on the wet asphalt, and somewhere behind me forty-four floors of unfinished sentence stood getting rained on for the first time in five months of a season that had sworn off rain entirely, its rebar catching the water and letting it go, its raw concrete drinking what it could and running the rest off in sheets down formwork grain that had been dry since June. I let it soak through my shirt and did not check whether it was the kind of rain a man could reasonably have predicted. Somewhere in the apartment, on the kitchen table, a notebook with a dog-eared blank page sat exactly as I had left it four days running, and I understood that I could go home and open it and see whether an old man's shaking hand had already known this hour was coming. Standing in the water, my shirt gone through, my hair flat against my scalp, I understood I was never again going to need to check.

I took my phone out anyway, sheltering it under one arm, and I wrote to Fon. Not a template. Not a scheduled hour. Twenty past six in the morning, the rain loud enough on the screen's glass that I had to read my own message back twice to be sure autocorrect hadn't done anything unkind to it.

I watched the three dots appear under it almost at once, which at that hour, for her, was its own small violation of a seventeen-month pattern, and then they stopped, and I stood in the rain on Charoen Krung holding a phone with nothing on the screen but my own sent line, and then the dots came back, and stopped again. She was choosing, I understood, between two or three answers, none of them false, not all of them the one she meant.

I did not wait to see which one she sent. I put the phone away wet and walked home the long way, past a 7-Eleven with its doors already open and its fluorescent light throwing a clean rectangle out onto the flooding street, past a clerk I did not recognize wiping down a counter nobody had used yet, past a green jacket parked at a soi mouth with its engine off and its visor down, unremarkable now, one more man waiting out a downpour the ordinary way, and I did not cross the street to check the plate. Somewhere over Sathorn the sky was starting, unconvincingly, to consider morning. By the time I reached my own soi the rain had thinned to something a man could walk through without deciding to, and the strip of receipt paper was still in the door jamb where I had left it, undisturbed, and I went inside and did not check the notebook on the kitchen table, and did not open the folder marked with two initials in the closet, and did not, for the first morning in ninety-seven days, check anything at all before I slept. The next draw was five days out, the first Sunday of December, and somewhere in the building a television would already be tuned to it by six that evening, the way it always was on draw days, and for the first time since Direk had shown me his ledger I understood I was not going to be in the room for it.

The 7:47 alarm — Radar, the one I had set seventeen months ago and never once edited, toggled, or silenced — did not ring.
