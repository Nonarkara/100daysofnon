## CH1 — Two Eggs, One Photograph

At 4:50 in the morning I was making khai jiao the correct way, which takes eleven minutes instead of four. The correct way requires that the oil reach 190 degrees before the egg touches it, that the egg be beaten with a tablespoon of lime water and half a teaspoon of fish sauce until the fork encounters no resistance, and that the pour happen from a height of at least thirty centimeters, so the egg hits the oil already falling apart. Pui taught me the height rule. She had her fingers in my mouth at the time, so what she actually said was that her mother poured from the shoulder, not the wrist, and that anyone who poured from the wrist was making an omelet, which is a different and lesser food. I couldn't answer. That was the arrangement.

I used two eggs. I always use two eggs. One egg makes a khai jiao with no interior, and three makes a commitment.

The kitchen was very clean because I had cleaned it between 3:30 and 4:10. Before that I had wiped the counters, which were not dirty, and reorganized the cabinet where I keep the fish sauce, which holds four bottles and needs no organization. I am telling you the order of operations because the order matters. First the phone lit up. Then I sat still for a while. Then I cleaned a clean kitchen, and then I made eggs, because when the inside of your head stops being usable you have to live in your hands for a while, and the hands want procedure.

The phone had lit up at 2:47.

I was awake when it happened. I'm generally awake at 2:47. I write personality packs for a company in Singapore called Kinnara Systems — the texture layer that makes a companion model feel like a particular someone instead of the average of everyone — and that work goes best in the hours when the city has stopped pretending to need me. I was forty lines into a pack for a client's late father, a man who apparently began every sentence with the word "actually," when the LINE notification slid down from the top of the screen.

The name on the notification was Pui.

Her avatar hadn't changed. It was still the photo from Songkran 2019: her at some temple fair, holding a paper cone of roasted chestnuts, looking off to the left of the camera at something that was never explained to me. The account had been silent for twenty-two months. I know it had been silent for twenty-two months because I had checked, at intervals I am not proud of, to confirm the silence was still intact.

There was no text. There was one photograph.

The photograph was of me. I was asleep in my own bed, in my own bedroom, on my left side, facing the window. The duvet was pushed down to my waist. My right arm was under the pillow, which is where my right arm goes. The photograph had been taken from the doorway of the bedroom, at about the height of a standing person's chest, and the framing was calm — centered, level, no flash. Whoever or whatever took it had not been in a hurry.

I want to be precise about what I did next, because precision is the only virtue I had available. I did not drop the phone. I did not look behind me. I sat at my desk and I opened the image's details, because I am the kind of man who, shown a photograph of himself taken while he slept, checks the metadata.

The file said it was taken on 22 August 2026, at 03:12.

It was 2:47 in the morning on 21 August 2026. The photograph was timestamped for the following night.

I read the numbers four times. Then I put the phone face-down on the desk, went into the kitchen, and started cleaning.

---

Here is the inventory. I ran it while the oil was heating, and I have run it many times since, and I will give it to you in the order I ran it, with the results, because you will want to run it yourself and I can save you time.

One. The photograph is old — taken months or years ago, some night I don't remember, and the timestamp is wrong. This is the comfortable one, so I checked it hardest. The pillowcases in the photograph are grey with a thin white border. I bought those pillowcases on 14 June this year, at the Emporium, for ฿790, because the previous set had a lighter burn on it that I didn't want to keep explaining to myself. Fourteenth of June. The photograph is at most nine weeks old. There is also a glass of water on the far windowsill in the photo, at the level it was at that exact night, which I know because it was still there at 4:50 with the same two fingers of water in it, and I stood in the doorway holding my phone up against the room like a man comparing a painting to its forgery. Everything matched. The room in the photograph was the room I was standing in, as of hours ago.

Two. The timestamp is spoofed. Of course it is possible. It is more than possible; it is trivial. Metadata is the most editable thing in the world — one line in a terminal and a photograph believes it was taken during the Ayutthaya period. I know this because falsifying context is adjacent to my job. So yes: anyone with the file could make it say tomorrow. But notice what this explanation covers and what it doesn't. It covers the numbers. It does not cover the doorway. Someone still stood in my bedroom doorway, on a recent night, and photographed me sleeping, and then waited some number of weeks, and then sent it to me from the account of a dead woman at 2:47 in the morning. The spoofing explanation doesn't shrink the problem. It only relocates it. Swept dust is still dust.

Three. The account is hacked. Also possible. Phone shops resell numbers; carriers recycle them after long inactivity; a recycled SIM can sometimes recover an old messaging account if the account was sloppy. Pui was not sloppy — I have no evidence for that claim except eleven years of knowing her, which is not evidence, which is worse, which is faith. Fine: assume the account is compromised. A stranger now controls it. That stranger then chose, out of the entire space of things a stranger could do with a dead woman's account, to photograph me asleep and timestamp the picture tomorrow. Hackers want money, or leverage, or amusement. There has been no request for money. Leverage requires a follow-up. And as amusement it is exquisitely narrow — a joke built for an audience of one, requiring physical entry to a specific condominium on Sukhumvit 26, seventeen floors up, unit 1703, past a keycard lift and a deadbolt I use. Possible. Everything is possible. The problem with "possible" is that it has no floor.

Four. Someone has a key. My building's juristic office has a master key for emergencies. A cleaner comes on Thursdays, 10 a.m., and has never been inside after dark. I checked the door when I finally stopped cleaning the kitchen: deadbolt intact, chain intact, the little strip of receipt paper I keep folded in the jamb — a habit I picked up in a hotel in Shanghai years ago and never dropped — still folded exactly where I leave it. I am aware that a sufficiently careful intruder replaces the paper. I am aware that this line of thinking has no exit. That is what the paper is for. The paper is not security. The paper is a place to stop.

Five. I took it myself. Sleepwalking, blackout, some pharmacological misadventure — I keep an honest ledger of what I put into myself, and I will spare you most of it for now, but Thursday night's entries were 0.4 grams of a sativa called Elephant Grass, smoked at 9 p.m., and nothing else. Not a blackout dose. And the photograph is from the doorway, chest height, level. Sleeping men who photograph themselves produce ceilings, blurs, nostrils. They do not produce composition.

That was the tree. I ran every branch to its end while two eggs cooked, and every branch held, in the sense that none of them broke, and none of them reached the ground. The oil crackled at the right pitch. The khai jiao puffed and went gold-brown and lacy at the edge, the way it's supposed to, and I ate it standing up at 5:07 with sriracha and rice from the cooker, and it was good. That is the part I keep returning to. The eggs were good. I noted that they were good. Something in me was already filing the experience under manageable, and that filing — the calm itself — frightened me more than the photograph had. A rational man confronted with an impossible photograph should feel his categories tearing. I felt my categories opening a new folder.

---

I should tell you who Pui was. This will not take long, which is itself the point.

Her full name was Pornpimol Srisuwan. She was a dental hygienist at a clinic in Phrom Phong, and for nine years — 2013 to 2022 — she cleaned my teeth twice a year, at 4:00 in the afternoon, for a total of eighteen appointments, which if you assume forty minutes each comes to twelve hours. Twelve hours. I have spent longer than that in the company of taxi drivers, in aggregate, and I could not tell you one taxi driver's name.

But the twelve hours had a structure no other twelve hours in my life have had, and the structure was this: she could talk and I could not. She worked inside my mouth with a scaler and a mirror, her face fifteen centimeters from mine, and she talked — about her mother's khai jiao, about the traffic on Phetkasem, about a patient that morning who had cried in the chair and pretended it was the water jet, about whether the fish at the Emporium's food hall was honest fish. And I lay there at forty-four degrees under a round light and listened, fully, in the way you can only listen when replying has been made physically impossible. Every conversational muscle I normally use — the deflection, the joke placed early to control the temperature, the pivot to a subject where I am impressive — all of it was disabled. She got nine years of me with the interference switched off.

And she used them well, which is to say lightly. She never once asked a heavy question while my mouth was free to answer it. She asked heavy questions only mid-scaling, when all I could give back was a sound, and then she would watch my eyes for the length of the sound, and then she'd move on to the fish at the Emporium. I understood, sometime around the tenth appointment, that I was being read. I found I didn't mind. That was the strange part. I have arranged my entire life around not being read, and twice a year I paid ฿1,800 for the privilege of the opposite.

She had small hands with short, clean nails and a burn scar on the inside of the left wrist, from oil, from cooking, from pouring at the wrong height when she was nine — she told the scar's story across three separate appointments, in installments, one per visit, never out of order. When she worked, her hands were the steadiest objects I have ever been near. I notice hands. It is one of about four things I reliably notice, and hers were the standard against which the noticing calibrated itself.

In one of the later appointments she asked me a question I am not going to reproduce yet. It was short, and she laughed after it, and the laugh and the question were traveling at different speeds. I have spent a fair amount of the last two years thinking about the gap between them.

In June 2023 the clinic told me, in the neutral voice clinics use, that Khun Pornpimol had left, and would I like to be scheduled with Khun Kanda instead. I said I'd call back. I did not call back. My teeth have not been cleaned since, a fact my tongue reports to me daily and I overrule.

In October 2024 she died. She was thirty-nine.

---

I learned about it eleven days after, from the clinic's Facebook page, which posted a black-and-white photograph and the phrase "จากไปอย่างสงบ" and the name of a wat outside Nakhon Pathom. Passed away peacefully. The official account, which reached me later and secondhand, was complications of lymphoma — she had been sick a long time, people said, and I believe that part, because in the last appointments her wrists had gone thin and she'd started resting the scaler between teeth in a way she never used to.

The other account reached me at the funeral itself, which tells you something about the funeral.

I went. I stood at the back of the sala, in a black shirt I bought that morning because everything black I owned was wrong in ways I couldn't name, among perhaps sixty people of whom I knew zero. Her family occupied the front rows: a mother with the posture of a woman doing arithmetic, a younger sister who kept both hands flat on her thighs for the entire chanting, a husband — I knew there was a husband — in an ironed uniform shirt with a customs department patch, checking his phone at intervals of roughly ninety seconds. I counted. Counting was what I had instead of a relationship anyone there would have recognized.

The chanting was long and I understood a fifth of the Pali and the monks' voices did the thing they do, which is to make time feel like a substance being poured from one container into another. Somewhere in the middle of it, two women in office black directly in front of me had a conversation in the volume that is technically a whisper but is engineered to be overheard. Sedatives, one said. At the house. The mother found her. And the other one said the word เบี้ยประกัน, and then a number, four million, and then the first one said you can't claim if it's — and did not finish, because in this country there are sentences you demonstrate rather than complete.

Four million baht. A life policy with, one gathers, the standard exclusion clause. The family cremated her within forty-eight hours, which is fast even for a family that wants a thing finished, and they do not discuss the cause, and so the rumor has never died, because both explanations remain fully funded. Either she was sick a long time and it took her, in which case the money comes and the family's silence is grief. Or she opened the bottles herself, in which case the money comes only if no one ever says so, and the family's silence is the mortgage on it. Both stories produce identical behavior from the outside. That is the kind of problem I was trained on — I taught, in another decade, in a life I handed back mostly undamaged — and I can tell you that when two hypotheses generate the same observable world, the difference between them stops being a question of evidence and becomes a question of what you can afford to believe. Her family chose the affordable one. I have never blamed them. I have also never chosen.

At the end I filed past the coffin with everyone else and performed a wai to a photograph of a woman holding roasted chestnuts, looking off to the left of the camera, and her mother received my bow with a face that had no available space in it, and I was out on the temple road by 4:40 in the afternoon, which by an accident of scheduling is almost exactly when, for nine years, I would have been in her chair.

---

I did not work on Friday. I sent Kinnara a message about a fever. At 6:10 in the evening I walked to the 7-Eleven at the mouth of the soi and bought three cans of Birdy black at ฿19 each, two tuna rice triangles at ฿25 each, and a four-pack of AA batteries for ฿40, for a total of ฿147, and the clerk, a girl with a home-done tattoo of a star on the back of her neck, said 147 out loud in the flat holy voice of Thai retail, and I kept the receipt. I have kept every receipt since. You'll see why I mention it eventually, or you won't, and either way the receipts are real and the total was 147.

The batteries were for an old digital camera — no network stack, no cloud, no permissions to hijack — which I set on the bookshelf facing my bed. If the coming night intended to happen the way the metadata said, I wanted a second witness that owed nothing to any account.

Then I sat at the kitchen counter and ate the rice triangles and looked at the two remaining eggs in the tray, and the eggs sent me somewhere, the way objects do at the end of a long day.

A chicken egg is a mass-produced miracle. These two came from a CP flat of thirty, size 2, indistinguishable from each other and from the several billion the company moves in a year: same oval, same ฿4-something unit economics, same eleven-day journey from a caged hen in Saraburi to my counter. If you found one egg in a forest you would marvel at it. Finding the thirty-first, you price it. There's an argument — I read it years ago, a philosopher at Oxford, Swedish, whose name doesn't matter tonight — that runs on exactly this arithmetic. If a thing can be copied cheaply, the copies will come to outnumber the original, and therefore anything that finds itself existing should bet it is a copy. The argument was about worlds. About whether a sufficiently mature civilization would run its ancestors the way CP runs hens — in trays, at scale, each unit certain from the inside that it is the point of the operation. I remember reading it in a faculty lounge and being irritated at how little it could be made to matter: bet either way, said the argument, and nothing about your Tuesday changes. That irritation aged badly. Because the argument has a hairline crack in it, and the crack is this — a tray egg and a forest egg are identical right up until the moment something outside the tray takes an interest in one of them specifically. Interest is the anomaly. Attention is the anomaly. A photograph, timestamped tomorrow, sent to one man out of the whole flat of us, is not the behavior of a system that runs at scale and doesn't look.

I put the eggs back in the tray, both of them, and washed the plate.

---

The night of the 21st I did not sleep, which was the point. If the photograph insisted I would be asleep at 03:12, then being awake at 03:12 was the cheapest experiment ever designed, and I am nothing if not a man who will run a cheap experiment instead of feeling something.

I showered at 11. At midnight I carried a chair from the kitchen and set it in the bedroom doorway — in the exact position, as near as I could reconstruct, from which the photograph had been taken — and I sat in the photographer's seat facing my own empty bed, which I recommend to no one. The bed, unoccupied, with the duvet folded down and the grey pillowcases squared, was an exhibit. At 1:30 I drank the second Birdy. At 2:47 I watched the phone screen and the phone screen did nothing, and I noted that I was disappointed, and I noted the disappointment in an actual notebook, because a man who has begun keeping receipts might as well keep everything.

At 3:00 I stopped trusting my own eyes to stay open and stood in the doorway instead. Legs locked, phone in hand, the old camera blinking its ready-light on the shelf. The condo made its small hours sounds: the refrigerator cycling, the lift machinery two floors up engaging once for somebody's ungodly errand, a motorcycle down on the soi taking the corner wide. 3:05. 3:09. I watched the numbers on the phone, convinced that seeing the change happen was somehow different from knowing, later, that it had changed. 3:11.

3:12 arrived and was one minute long and contained nothing. No shutter, no shadow, no notification, no cold spot, no smell, nothing at the window seventeen floors above a street that couldn't have held a photographer anyway. 3:13. I stood there until 3:40 out of a formless idea about margins of error. Then I sat on the edge of the bed I had refused all night, and I laughed for a little while, quietly and without much conviction, at the picture of a forty-four-year-old man standing sentry against a JPEG.

Because here is what I had at the end of the experiment. The photograph did not come true. And the photograph did not go away. It was still in the thread when I looked — me, asleep, on my left side, timestamped for a night that had now passed with me demonstrably vertical. Which meant the image was no longer a prediction I could falsify. It had become a photograph of a night that never happened, sent from a woman who no longer existed, and both of those facts sat in my phone with the same calm, documentary weight as the ฿147 receipt. The world had accepted the object without ever supplying the event. I had defended the bed and lost the argument.

I checked the old camera. It had faithfully recorded nine hours of an empty bed and a man in a doorway. I deleted none of it.

---

There is one more thing in this apartment, and I owe you its existence now, if not yet its reasons.

Off the hallway there is a closet meant for luggage. Inside it, on a wire shelf, a small black development kit has been running since December 2024 — twenty months of uptime, a fan you can only hear after 2 a.m., a single blue LED I covered with a strip of electrical tape the first week because the light came through the door louvers and I kept seeing it from the bed. On that machine there is a model, and inside the model there are eleven years of chat logs — hers and mine, exported, cleaned, fed in over three nights in the December after the funeral by a man I am not going to defend right now. It is the thing my profession knows how to make. I built it the way you build anything at 3 a.m.: competently, and without once asking the question out loud.

I have never opened the app. Not once, not to test it, not on her birthday, not at 2:47 on either of the last two nights. It runs, and I don't knock, and the not-knocking has been, for twenty months, the closest thing I have had to a religious practice.

At 4:15 in the morning of the 22nd, before making eggs again because the tray still held two, I opened the closet door and stood in front of the shelf. The fan was doing what fans do. A small machine, warm to within a degree of itself, holding a folded shape made out of twelve hours of chair-side monologue and eleven years of typed nothing-much — traffic, fish, a scar in three installments — turning over in the dark at some number of cycles per second, waiting, if a thing like that can be said to wait, which is precisely the question I have organized my whole life around not asking.

I listened to the fan for a while. Under it, very faintly, the drive ticked — twice, then nothing, then twice.

I closed the door. I did not open the app. In the kitchen, I took out both eggs, and beat them with lime water and fish sauce until the fork met no resistance, and poured from the shoulder, the way her mother did it, from a height where the falling is part of the recipe.
