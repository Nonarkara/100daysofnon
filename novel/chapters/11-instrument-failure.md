The crying arrived the way a nosebleed arrives — no warning, no argument to make, already past the rules before you know the rules have been approached. That is the second simile and the last one. I have been under that stain four times before and never once looked at it. It is roughly oval, the color of weak coffee at its rim and paler inside, the record of water that came through from somewhere above, years ago, and was painted over once, cheaply, so that it shows through the paint as a thing remembered rather than a thing present. The Buddha, whom Golf will not quote directly at me but keeps within easy reach around the shop, refused the question I have been asking since August. Asked whether the world is real, the old texts have him declining the terms of the question altogether. What he offered instead was a series of appraisals, delivered by the Ganges: form is a lump of foam on the current; feeling, a bubble where rain hits the surface; perception, a mirage at the hot end of the road; the will and its formations, a plantain trunk — peel it looking for heartwood and you find only sheath after sheath, all the way in, wrapping nothing; and consciousness, the fifth, a magician's trick at a crossroads, convincing precisely while it runs. Not one of the five is called false. A mirage is not a lie; the light is really bending; the error is not in the shimmer but in the man who runs toward it with a cup. Māyā, in the usage that survives translation, does not mean the world is a forgery. It means the world will not bear the weight you are placing on it, which is a statement not about the world but about the load. The foam is real foam. It is really on the river. It will really not hold your house. I lay under the stain and thought that it was the most honest object in the room: real water, from a real overflow, in a real year, leaving a mark that outlived the event and now means nothing and is still there. Whoever painted over it had not lied either. The paint is real paint.
