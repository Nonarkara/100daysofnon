## CH5 — The Ledger

On Monday morning I bought a kilo of mandarins at Sam Yan market for ฿120, from a vendor who weighed them with one hand and rebagged them with the other, her fingers finding the bruised one by touch and swapping it out without looking, a correction so practiced it had stopped being a decision. Then I carried the bag across Banthat Thong Road and up the staircase of a shophouse three doors down from a shuttered photo lab — twenty-two steps to the second floor, nineteen to the third, the count uneven because somewhere in 1968 the builder had run short of something, patience or concrete — and knocked on a door that had a spirit house's worth of dust on its transom and a doorbell that had been painted over, twice, in two different decades of green.

The 7:47 message that morning had been template two. กินข้าวยัง. The eating cat came back at 7:51, chopsticks raised, and I put the phone in my bag with the mandarins and did not think about the fact that I found the cat reassuring. There is a limit to how much of yourself you can put in the notebook.

Sunday I had done laundry, logged nothing — the rider column stayed empty all day, and I wrote that down too, because an instrument that only records positives is an instrument you have already corrupted — and at 21:15 I had smoked 0.4 grams of Elephant Grass by the bedroom window, the dose test being over and the verdict filed. At 2:47 both nights, nothing. The chat still ended where it had ended for a week: my four words, her read receipt, and below them the kind of white space you can hear.

And on Sunday afternoon I had called Ajarn Direk.

I should explain how I had his number, which is that in 2019 I attended the cremation of Ajarn Somsak, who taught structures to three decades of architecture students and died of exactly the kind of heart he had told us never to design, and at the temple a man with an oxygen tube coiled into his shirt pocket had asked me whether I still measured things. I said I did. He said, "Then you'll need this eventually," and gave me a card with nothing on it but his name and eleven digits. Direk taught epistemology at Chulalongkorn for thirty-one years. I took his elective in my third year because it met after lunch and I have kept perhaps six sentences from it for twenty-three years, which by the arithmetic of lectures is a spectacular yield.

He answered on the ninth ring.

"ครับ."

I said my name, the year I graduated, the elective.

A pause with breathing in it, mechanical at the edges. Then: "อ้อ. The one who left."

I had left academia fourteen years ago. He had retired eleven years ago. In his bookkeeping, apparently, only one of those counted as leaving.

"I have something," I said. "I don't know what column it goes in."

"Monday," he said. "After nine. The machine and I are better before noon." And hung up, before I could ask what machine, though by then I had already heard it, keeping time behind his voice.

He opened the door wearing a pressed shirt buttoned to the collar and trousers with a crease, which moved me in a way I did not examine, and a clear tube that ran from his nose down his chest and away along the skirting board into the room behind him, fifteen meters of it at least, taped down at the corners with the yellowed tape of a job done once and correctly. Behind him the machine sat against the wall, white, the size of a nightstand: a concentrator, sighing every four seconds — a click and then a long exhale, like someone agreeing with a point nobody had made.

"You brought fruit," he said. "Students bring fruit when they want the answer before the examination."

"I'm forty-four."

"Then you should know better than to want that." He stepped back to let me in. "Peel one anyway. The smell is the part that still works."

The room was the whole width of the shophouse and had been a serious room for a long time. Books in Thai, English, and German went up the walls in an order I could not read but did not doubt. A desk with a green glass lamp. A daybed with the sheet drawn drum-tight. And by the window, on a low table of its own, a Go board — thick, on short legs, its wood gone the gold of forty years of hands — with a game on it. A hundred and forty stones, maybe more, the position dense in the lower right and open at the top, where nothing had been decided yet. Two lidded bowls sat beside it, and beside those, face down, a photograph.

I did not ask. He watched me not ask.

"It's mid-game," I said.

"It has been mid-game for eleven years," he said, and lowered himself into the chair by the desk with the concentrator counting behind him, and that was the whole of the subject for the day. Sometimes you can tell where a door is by the care with which a man walks around it.

I peeled a mandarin and put it on a saucer he pointed to. He ate one segment, slowly, and set the rest aside, and the smell of it filled the room, which was exactly what he had promised, and for a while neither of us performed any conversation, which I appreciated. The fan turned. Traffic came up off Banthat Thong in its own tide. Across the road, at the mouth of the soi opposite, I had logged a green jacket on my way in — 09:48, engine off, visor down, not eating, not on his phone, plate 4ขม 1220, the seventh entry in the log and a new plate — and from Direk's window I could see the sub-soi's corner and could not see whether he was still there, and I chose, as an act of discipline, not to stand up and check.

"So," Direk said. "Show me the thing with no column."

I took out my phone and opened the photograph and handed it to him.

He held it at arm's length, then took glasses from his shirt pocket and held it closer. He looked at it for a long time. His thumb moved once and enlarged it, and he looked again, quadrant by quadrant, as systematically as I had at 3 a.m. on the twenty-first of August, though it had taken me four hours to get there and it took him about ninety seconds.

"Who took it?" he said.

"I don't know."

"That isn't an answer to the question. The question has an answer. You not knowing it is a separate fact about you." He set the phone face down on the Go table, next to the other face-down photograph, which I decided not to find significant. "Tell it in order. Dates. Amounts. Don't interpret."

So I told it in order, with dates and amounts, and did not interpret, and it took forty minutes: the message at 2:47 on the twenty-first, the EXIF stamp that said the following night, the pillowcases and the ฿790 that dated them, the vigil, the camera that recorded nine hours of an empty bed, the sticker, the read receipt that arrived before the send animation finished, the wreath with the blank sender line, the receipt for the longan drink I had considered and not bought, serial 1146 to my 1147, the riders — the log, the plates, the behavioral tell, the control group — and the forty-one minutes on foot into Khlong Toei, and the building that had once kept fish. He stopped me twice. Once to ask the exact wording of the message I had sent — "ใครอยู่ตรงนั้น," I said; "no question mark" — and he nodded, the punctuation apparently the part that mattered to him. And once, at the receipt, to ask whether the phantom one was printed before or after mine. Before, I said. 15:26 to my 15:27. He sat with that for a moment.

"The drink you did not buy was rung up one minute before the drinks you did," he said. "So it isn't reading your outcomes. It's reading your considerations, and it is doing so slightly upstream of you." He said it without inflection, already moving on. Then: "Say the name of the company again."

"Cognita. Cognita Thailand Company Limited."

"Cognita," he said. "Latin. The things that are known. Passive, plural, neuter. Not the knowing — the known. Whoever chose it was thinking of the material." He tapped one finger on the table, twice. "You copied a word in a language you don't read. As it happens, I read it."

The concentrator clicked and sighed.

"In March 2016," Direk said, without any announcement that we were changing subjects, because we were not, "a machine played a professional Go match against the strongest human player of his generation. Lee Sedol. Seoul, the Four Seasons hotel, five games. You know this."

"I know the headline."

"The headline is the least of it. Second game, the tenth of March. Move thirty-seven. The machine is black. It plays a shoulder hit on the fifth line, on the right side." He turned to the board beside him and touched the empty upper region with two fingers, not placing anything, just locating it in the air above the wood. "You are not a Go player. Third line is territory, fourth is influence — we teach children that much. The fifth line belongs to no one. In four thousand years of recorded games, you attach on the fifth line at that moment approximately never. The machine's own tables said a human would play that move one time in ten thousand. The commentators assumed the operator had misclicked. Lee Sedol looked at it, stood up, and left the room. He was gone fifteen minutes. Then he came back and lost."

"A mistake that worked?"

"No. That is what everyone needed it to be, and it was not. It was a move that was seen to be beautiful — afterward, and by everyone, including the man it defeated. The European champion said, 'It's not a human move.' He meant it as a description, and it became a compliment. The machine had not calculated something we were too slow for. It had wanted something we did not know was there to want." He took a breath that had procedure in it, the tube shifting against his collar. "The machine's descendants have not lost a meaningful game since. Lee Sedol retired in 2019. He said he had come to know an entity that cannot be defeated, and stood up from the board, permanently, and no one who understood the position blamed him."

"And the inversion," I said, because I could hear it arriving from three sentences away.

"The machine proved it could surprise us," Direk said. "Nothing we have done since has proved we can surprise it."

The fan turned through that a few times.

"Third drawer of the desk," he said. "Under the tickets."

The third drawer held a brick of lottery tickets bound with a rubber band — years of them, the top ones faded to the color of weak tea — and underneath, squared with the drawer's edge, a stack of school notebooks. Māh-Bunčhong, the cheap kind, cardboard covers, multiplication tables printed on the back in a grid, twelve twelves and out, the manufacturer having imagined no need beyond that. Nine of them. The spines were numbered in pencil. I brought the ninth to him and he waved it away and had me put it on the Go table and open it myself, which I understood later was the pedagogy: you read a ledger; you are not read to from one.

His handwriting was very small and very upright, in pencil, and each page was ruled by hand into three columns. The first column was the event. The second was the arithmetic — what should have happened, at what odds, by what reference. The third column was headed with one word: ผู้แต่ง.

"Eleven years," Direk said. "Two hundred and eleven entries. I log anything that arrives claiming to be impossible. Most of it is lottery, because in this country the lottery is where the impossible goes to register itself — twice a month, the first and the sixteenth, a national census of hope conducted to six digits. The rest is weather, strangers, and machines. And of the two hundred and eleven," he said, "two hundred and six have an author."

I turned pages. Each entry was dated to the day. 16 ก.พ. 2561 — a vendor in Chachoengsao sells the first-prize ticket three draws running; column two worked the odds honestly, one in some millions; column three, in the small upright pencil: ผู้แต่ง — เจ้ามือ. Ticket routing, he explained: resale networks, winning tickets bought back and re-sold at markup to people who want to buy luck across a counter. 3 ส.ค. 2562 — a man in a yellow polo shirt seen by one of his former students at the Nakhon Sawan bus station at 09:40 and again at Hua Lamphong at 16:05 the same day, 240 kilometers apart, same shirt, same bag. Column three: ผู้แต่ง — รถตู้. There is a van that does it in three and a half hours; the student simply did not believe other people's days could be more strenuous than his own. Entry after entry in the same shape. The odds honestly computed, the strangeness honestly weighed, and then the author found — ad-tech, arithmetic, fraud, memory, the sample space drawn wrong, the human preference for stories with hands in them. It was the most disappointing document I had ever wanted to keep reading. It was eleven years of a man building the instrument that could detect the thing by proving, two hundred and six times, that it had not detected it.

"And the five," I said.

"Page forty," he said. "Volume six. But the ninth has one."

I found it. 14 มิ.ย. 2560 — rain over Bang Khen district, forty-one minutes, measured at two stations, on a day the Meteorological Department had issued a zero percent forecast — and the zero, he had noted in column two, was not rounding; the model output was archived and he had requested it and it said what it said. Column three: blank. Not dashed, not marked pending. Genuinely blank — an entry with nowhere yet to put its answer.

"Radar misses cells all the time," I said.

"It does. That is entry ninety-three, and entry one-forty, and entry one-sixty-two, all authored, all rain." He tipped his chin at the page. "That one I kept because I stood in it."

"And the other blanks?"

"A woman on a bridge in Phitsanulok and a woman on a bridge in Sing Buri, the same afternoon, described identically by two of my former students who have never met. A bell. I won't discuss the bell." He turned his hand over on the notebook, palm up, a small concession. "And the tails. Since 2015, the last two digits of the first prize have ended in four-four seven times. Two hundred and sixty-odd draws; expectation says under three. The chi-squared test shrugs. Seven is not impossible." He paused for the machine to finish its exhale. "It is merely insistent. And insistence deserves a page even when impossibility hasn't earned one. If you only log what cannot happen, you will miss what keeps happening."

I am forty-four years old. Pui's clinic laid me back at forty-four degrees for eighteen appointments. I noted both facts — the same instinct that had already logged her hour and mine as exactly five hours apart — and put them where I put that: in the column of things I refuse to build with. The column was getting crowded.

The tickets sat on the desk in their rubber band, and I found myself looking at them while he breathed, and I thought about the lottery, which in this country is not gambling so much as a twice-monthly national seminar in reading. The numbers come from everywhere, which is to say from anything that has recently demonstrated power: the license plate of the minivan photographed crumpled against the pylon, written down by bystanders before the injured are fully carried away; the age of the famous abbot, dead on a Tuesday, converted to digits before the chanting starts; the wax dripped from a temple candle into a bowl of water and interpreted for shapes and omens; the tree behind the shrine at the end of a market soi whose bark, rubbed with powder by ten thousand thumbs, yields a 4 and maybe a 7 to anyone who wants them, and everyone wants them. On the mornings of the first and the sixteenth the queue outside the Government Lottery Office on Ratchadamnoen contains every kind of person this country manufactures, and each of them is holding a number that arrived, and not one of them, if you asked, would say the number was random. Randomness is the government's claim, printed on the ticket at eighty baht a pair and sold on the street at a hundred. What the queue believes is otherwise: the numbers mean; the world leaks; power announces itself in digits if you keep your eye on the places where it lands. The statisticians say the queue is wrong, and the statisticians are right, draw by draw. The queue is measuring something else, and over eleven years and nine notebooks a retired epistemologist with a hand-ruled author column had, entry by entry, mostly agreed with the statisticians — two hundred and six times — and had kept the notebooks anyway, for the sake of five blanks. I looked from the tickets back to the page with its empty third column, and the blank looked different to me than it had five minutes earlier. Less like an absence. More like a seat left open for someone specific.

"There's a word," I said, "for what I keep almost thinking. About all of it. The photograph, the receipts, the—"

Direk lifted one hand off the notebook, not far, perhaps a centimeter, and I stopped mid-word.

"Don't bring it in here," he said. "A cage you can name is furniture. You will hang your coat on it and sit down and live there. I have watched better minds than yours do it." He let the hand back down onto the page. "The word does no work. Every fact you have told me this morning is exactly as heavy without it."

"Then what do I do with the facts?"

"What do you do." He looked at the Go board, at the undecided region at the top of it, for a moment that had nothing performed in it. "You are treating this as a question about what the world is. That is the wrong altitude, and it flatters you, because what-the-world-is cannot be tested from a condominium on Sukhumvit and so you are excused in advance. Come down. Your photograph, your receipts, your riders — those are not the world. Those are correspondence. And correspondence," he said, "has an author. Two hundred and six of mine did. So." He closed the notebook and squared it on the table with two fingers. "Homework. You were a poor student and you are getting old, so only one item. Bring me one event with no author. One. Not a feeling — I have no column for feelings. An event. Run your columns with the same discipline you used for your dose test — the first competent thing you told me all morning. Author everything you can. And if one blank survives you," and here he almost smiled, "bring it here, and we will give it a page."

The concentrator clicked and sighed. Below us a bus took on passengers with its brakes.

I stood, and took the mandarin saucer to the little sink at the back because some debts you can pay in that currency, and when I came back he had my phone in his hand again, face up, the photograph open. He was not enlarging it now. He was just looking, the glasses low on his nose, and he looked for long enough that the screen dimmed and he had to touch it once, carefully, to bring the image back — me asleep, on my left side, facing the window, the duvet at my waist, the glass with its two fingers of water on the far sill, all of it lit by nothing and taken by no one and stamped with a night that had, by every clock I trusted, not yet occurred when the shutter fell.

"You have spent ten days on the timestamp," Direk said.

"The timestamp is the impossible part."

"The timestamp is the loud part." He handed the phone back to me. His fingers, taking mine for a half second in the exchange, were cold at the tips — the particular cold of a body already rationing its own warmth, deciding what the periphery can spare. "Cameras mis-set their clocks constantly. That is entry eleven, volume two, authored in one afternoon. No." He settled back, and the tube shifted, and for a while the only voice in the room was the machine's, agreeing with nobody, every four seconds, about nothing.

"The timestamp is not the anomaly," he said. "The anomaly is that someone wanted you to look."
