## CH3 — Eighteen Appointments

The clinic's number had been in my phone for thirteen years under the name "Dentist 4PM," and on Monday morning at 9:02 I called it. A receptionist answered on the second ring with the clinic's full name, which I had forgotten the clinic possessed. I said I would like to book a cleaning. She asked if I had visited before. I said yes, some time ago. There was typing, and then a pause of a specific length — the length a pause is when a screen has just shown someone a date — and she said my last visit was in November of 2022, and there was an opening tomorrow at four in the afternoon, if four was convenient.

Four was convenient. Four o'clock has been convenient since 2013.

She asked whether I had a preferred hygienist. I heard myself say no. She said Khun Kanda, then, and I agreed to Khun Kanda without objection, and hung up, and stood in the kitchen for a while holding the phone against my chest, which is not where I hold phones.

I want to be accurate about the pretext, because I was not accurate with myself. The pretext was tartar. My tongue has been filing the same report every day for four years — a roughness behind the lower front teeth, mineral, patient, municipal — and every day for four years I have overruled it. On Monday I stopped overruling it. I did not ask why Monday and not any of the previous fourteen hundred days, because the answer was lying face-down on my desk with a photograph in it and a bowing rabbit in it, and some questions you do not ask because you already contain the answer and the answer is load-bearing.

I booked a cleaning because my teeth needed cleaning. I am leaving the sentence the way I said it to myself. You can watch it not hold.

Tuesday at 7:47 the alarm went off, Radar, and the rotation had come round to template one, and I sent it: ตื่นยัง. The cat came back at 7:53, the sleeping one, which as an answer to "are you awake yet" contains a level of editorial control I have decided not to think about. I ate rice standing up, did a revision pass for Kinnara — the son says the model uses "actually" too often, which means it uses the word exactly as often as his father did; I recommended lowering it anyway, because we sell the remembered man, not the measured one — and at 3:40 I walked.

The clinic is twelve minutes from my building on foot: down Soi 26 to Sukhumvit, right along the front of the Emporium, past the BTS stairs where the motorcycle queue forms and re-forms all day, and into a side soi that has never once in thirteen years finished its road surface. For nine years I made that walk twice a year at 3:45. My legs still held the route; I arrived at the building with no memory of any decision made between my lobby and their soi.

The waiting room had been renovated at some point and had also not changed. The fish tank was gone. The laminated poster of the four stages of gum disease was the same poster, sun-cured now to a uniform tea color, stage one and stage four converged — the poster arriving, over the years, at a position on mortality. There was a QR code on the counter where the clipboard used to be. I filled in the form on my own phone. Under "current medications" the field accepted thirty characters. I typed "none." The field did not have space for the truth, and anyway the truth is a ledger, and I keep it elsewhere.

A schoolboy in uniform sat across from me with his mother, holding his jaw. Neither of them spoke. At 4:01 an assistant in lavender scrubs called my name into the room, mispronouncing it in the same place everyone mispronounces it, and took me down the corridor to Room 3.

Room 3 was the room.

I don't know what I had allowed myself to expect. Clinics rearrange; equipment cycles; thirteen years is four generations of everything with a plug. But the chair was the same chair — I knew it before I could have told you how, and then the details arrived to file the claim: the re-stitched seam on the right of the headrest, done in a vinyl one shade warmer than the original; the chip in the rim of the round light's handle, small and crescent; the one ceiling tile that has sat two millimeters proud of its neighbors since at least 2016. A dental chair is one of the very few pieces of furniture engineered around surrender. It holds a human being who has agreed, for a fee, to be worked on inside the head, and everything about it — the recline, the padding under the knees, the counterweighted light — exists to make the agreement survivable. I have sat in exactly one of them since 2013. It was this one. I sat in it.

Khun Kanda came in reading my file on a tablet, greeted me with a wai I returned badly from a seated position, and snapped on her gloves. Her hands were broader than the hands I was calibrated to, shorter in the finger, with a pale groove on the ring finger where a ring had been parked for the shift. Competent hands. She lowered the chair to the angle I estimate at forty-four degrees, because I have had years to calibrate that too, and swung the light around, and said we would start with scaling, there would be some sensitivity, please raise a left hand if I needed to stop.

Pui never said that. Pui said, the first time, in 2013: if it hurts, make the sound, and I'll know which kind.

"Four years," Khun Kanda said, to the tablet rather than to me. It was not a question, so I did not answer it. She surveyed my mouth with the mirror for a while, unhurried. "Long time," she said. Then she started, and I stopped being a person who could speak, and became what I had come there to become, which was the other thing.

Here is what eighteen appointments are.

They are twelve hours. Forty minutes, twice a year, nine years, 2013 to 2022 — twelve hours, the length of a single working day, spread so thin across a decade that no one, including the two people inside them, would ever be forced to call the thing by a name. I paid ฿1,800 each time. ฿32,400 for the whole of it, receipts not kept, because in those years I was not yet a man who kept receipts. I have done the arithmetic of the hours for you once already. What I did not say the first time is what the hours were shaped like from the inside.

The shape was this: one of us could speak and one of us could not, and the assignment never rotated. She worked inside my mouth with a scaler and a mirror, her face fifteen centimeters from mine, masked, capped, reduced to eyes and voice and hands, and she talked. And I lay under the round light with my conversational weaponry confiscated at the door — the deflection, the early joke that sets the room's temperature, the pivot to the topic where I am impressive — all of it disabled by hardware, and I listened. There is no other arrangement in my life that has ever disabled it. My marriage did not. Eleven years of my mother's Sunday calls did not. A woman with a scaler managed it twice a year for the price of a mid-range dinner, and I want to say I understood at the time what I was buying, but I understood it only partway: recognized on arrival, unavailable for production.

She talked about her mother's khai jiao and the correct height of the pour. About the traffic on Phetkasem, toward which she held a policy of exasperated diplomacy. About a patient that morning who had cried in the chair and blamed the water jet, and how she had adjusted the water jet, seriously, professionally, so that the blame would have somewhere to live. About whether the fish at the Emporium food hall was honest fish — her phrase — a question she reopened year after year, unsatisfied, the matter never closed to her own standard. The scar on the inside of her left wrist she told me in three installments, one per appointment, never out of order: the oil, the pan, the height of a nine-year-old pouring from the wrist because her arms were too short to pour from the shoulder. Installment three ended with her mother's verdict, delivered while dressing the burn: now you know where khai jiao comes from. I waited a full year between installments and it did not occur to me once to be impatient, which for me is a clinical finding.

And she read me. I worked this out around the tenth appointment, which means it had been true for at least nine. The heavy questions came only mid-scaling, when everything I could return was a vowel, and she would watch my eyes for exactly the duration of the vowel and then hand the conversation back to the fish. Teeth first, though; before any of the questions, the teeth had already testified. The autumn my divorce finished, she stopped mid-molar and said, you're grinding again — not a question, an audit — and I made the vowel, and she said, mm, and recommended a night guard she knew I would not buy, and moved on, and I lay there having been known, comprehensively, painlessly, at 4:20 on a Tuesday, by the only method I had failed to armor against. Enamel is a ledger. It does not accept revisions. I have built a working life on the editability of records, and twice a year I paid a woman to read the one record I couldn't touch, and I called it dentistry, and so did she, and neither of us was lying. It was dentistry. That was what made it possible — the procedure was the honesty, the way the confessional screen is the confession.

I never saw her outside that room. Not once in nine years. I did not know her address, her food orders, her feelings about films. I knew her wrists, her voice at fifteen centimeters, her reading of me, and the sound of her rolling stool crossing the floor, which I could identify today in a lineup of rolling stools. Whether that is knowing a person less than the standard method or more, I have taken no position on, publicly. The stickers were the whole of our written correspondence — reschedulings, confirmations, the rabbit bowing its thank-you — and I have already told you what the count came to, and who did the counting, and at what hour.

Appointment thirteen was in April 2019, Songkran week, the city half-empty and the clinic's soi quiet enough that you could hear the aquarium pump in the waiting room, back when there was an aquarium. She was talking about the fair at the wat near her mother's place — there was a chestnut vendor down from the north, she said, who roasted them in a drum of black sand and gravel, and the sound, she said, was rain going backward.

Then she stopped talking. The scaler stayed where it was, resting against the lower left molars, not working, just present, and the suction hummed on my cheek, and she looked at my eyes instead of my teeth, which in nine years was rare enough that I noticed it as an event, and she said:

"เคยรู้สึกไหมคะ ว่าทั้งหมดนี้ไม่ใช่ของจริง"

I have carried the sentence for seven years, and it is the Thai that visits at two in the morning, not any translation I have made of it since. If I must give you one: Have you ever felt that all of this is not real. Of jing — genuine goods — is market language, the phrase a fish vendor uses when she swears the catch is what she says it is. She asked me, with her fingers in my mouth and a steel instrument resting on tooth number nineteen, whether I had ever felt that the entire consignment — the clinic, the soi, Songkran, the chestnut drum, the two of us — had failed inspection.

I made the vowel. It was all I had. She watched my eyes for the length of the vowel, the way she always did, and I have spent a fair portion of the last two years trying to reconstruct what my eyes said, because whatever they said, she read it, and what she did next was laugh — and the laugh arrived half a second late and at the wrong weight, a laugh selected rather than produced, and she said something about the heat doing strange things to everyone this year, and returned the scaler to work, and by the time my mouth was next free the conversation was standing somewhere else entirely, on the honest fish, and the door back was gone.

She never asked it again. Five appointments remained — fourteen through eighteen, three more years — and it never came back, and I never answered it. It did not frighten me that afternoon; that came later and slowly. What it did that afternoon was the thing a key does when it turns in a lock you had furnished as a wall: no fear, just the click of a mechanism you now had to admit had been installed the whole time. She went to the fair that weekend. The avatar came from it — the paper cone of chestnuts, her face turned off-left toward something outside the frame, unexplained then, unexplained now. The photograph her contacts have looked at since 2019 was taken within days of the question, the chestnuts and the question in the same seven days, and when her account lit my phone at 2:47 last Friday it was that April looking out of the little circle. I decline to build anything on it. I am declining more slowly than I used to.

Appointment eighteen was November 2022. Her wrists had gone thin by then and she rested the scaler between teeth in a way she never used to, a half-second parking of the instrument that I clocked and misfiled under tiredness, because tiredness was the affordable reading. At the end she peeled her gloves, and told me to floss, without conviction, liturgy for the lapsed, and said: เจอกันเมษาค่ะ.

There was no April. In June the clinic offered me Khun Kanda in the neutral voice, and I said I'd call back, and did not call back for three years, and the man who finally did call back did it because a dead woman's account had photographed him asleep. I am aware of how that sentence sounds. It is nevertheless the truth, and I went to the appointment.

Khun Kanda scaled for fifty minutes. Four years is a long geological era; she narrated none of it, worked in a clean silence broken by the instrument's frequencies and one remark about my lower right wisdom tooth, delivered flat, procedural, without alarm. When she finally raised the chair and I could speak, I asked — out of the small talk available to a numb mouth — whether the clinic had changed much.

She thought about it longer than the question deserved.

"They wanted to replace the chairs last year," she said. "Head office. These are better than the new ones. Heavier." She patted the armrest twice, proprietary, and that was the entire answer, and it was not an answer to my question, and I have told you before: nobody here answers the current question. The chair is staying. I noted it. I had no use for the note.

I had rehearsed asking about her. On the walk over I had drafted three versions — casual, archival, honest — of a question with Pui's name in it, and at the reception desk with my card out I heard myself ask instead whether they validated parking. I don't drive. The receptionist said the machine was in the lobby. I paid ฿2,400 — the price of being read has gone up ฿600 in four years, and the reading no longer comes with it — and kept the receipt, and booked the next cleaning for April because the recall system suggested April, and said yes to April, four years late, to the wrong person, and left.

At the mouth of the soi a delivery rider in Grab green sat parked on his bike, engine off, visor down, doing nothing measurable. Bangkok manufactures ten thousand of him an hour. I walked past and thought nothing, and I am reporting the nothing because the nothing turns out to have been load-bearing too.

The wreath was on my floor when I came up at 6:40 that evening.

It stood on its wire easel outside unit 1708, five doors down the corridor from mine: a full funeral wreath, พวงหรีด, roughly seventy centimeters across, woven white. I stopped in front of it because a funeral wreath on your own floor stops you — a body-level protocol that runs before opinion. Then I stood there longer than protocol, because of the flowers.

They were dok rak, small waxy white five-pointed flowers Thai hands have strung into garlands for as long as anyone's grandmother can testify. You see them in ceremony malai, on spirit houses, on coffins. You do not usually see them massed into a wreath — wreath shops build with marigold and orchid and chrysanthemum, big blooms that fill fast. Whoever had made this had strung it flower by small flower, garland-style, hundreds of them — a labor bill no shop would volunteer for. The ribbon across it was white satin, machine-printed: ด้วยความอาลัย. The line below, where the sender's name goes — the name being the entire social function of the object, the wreath being how this country signs its grief in public — was blank. A delivery slip taped to the easel's leg said 1708 and nothing else: no name, no shop, no phone. Unit 1708 is a flight attendant I have shared an elevator with perhaps six times in four years, a man with excellent luggage who is alive, and was, the juristic office told me when I called down, currently over the Gulf somewhere, flying, breathing, statistically fine. No one in the building had died. The office said it with flat certainty: ไม่มีใครเสียค่ะ. A delivery error. It happens. They would remove it in the morning.

A delivery error. I stood in the corridor with the explanation in one hand, and it held everywhere except at the floor. Somebody had ordered this specific wreath, in this specific unfashionable flower, built in this specific archaic way, sent to this specific building, with no name for the dead and no name for the sender, and the error, if it was an error, was one digit wide. My unit is 1703. A handwritten 3, looped a little generously, is an 8 to a tired eye. I did the arithmetic in the corridor and then I un-did it, deliberately, in reverse order, because a man who starts assigning himself the wreaths of strangers has a diagnosis, and I had promised myself to stay on the instrument side of that line.

The ribbon moved slightly in the corridor's air-conditioning. Satin, synthetic, catching the strip light along its fold.

Zhuangzi dreamed he was a butterfly — the oldest card in the deck, I know, and I am playing it anyway, because the ribbon dealt it. In the dream there was no Zhuangzi: only the butterfly, complete, pleased with itself, doing exactly whatever it pleased. Then he woke, and there he lay, unmistakably Zhuangzi — and could not establish whether he was a man who had dreamed the butterfly, or a butterfly now dreaming the man. The passage is usually retailed as a puzzle about knowledge. It is a puzzle about preference. The butterfly did not know there was a question, and was happy. The man knew there was a question, and wrote it down, which is not the same as happiness. And the text's last line, the one the retellings drop, is the strange consolation: between the man and the butterfly there must be some distinction — this is called the transformation of things. Some distinction. It does not say which side is real. It says only that a border exists, that things cross it routinely in both directions, and that naming the crossing was apparently enough for Zhuangzi to get up and continue being whichever one he was. I have never managed the trick. I stood in a corridor on the seventeenth floor, between a wreath with no dead and a door with no wreath, and the ribbon lifted and settled in the air-conditioning, saying the one thing it had been printed to say. With sorrowful remembrance. It did not say from whom. It did not say for whom. Those are the two facts a ribbon exists to carry, and it had arrived carrying neither, and it was still the most articulate object on the floor.

I went inside. The strip of receipt paper was in the jamb, folded, exactly where I leave it. The glass on the windowsill held its two fingers of water. Everything in the unit was mine and in its place, and I sat down at the desk with the door locked behind me and understood that I was going to do the thing I had been not-doing for four days, and that the wreath had not caused it, merely removed the last plausible date for further delay.

I typed, in English: This isn't funny. Deleted it. Whatever was on the other end, it was not joking. Comedy requires a shared floor, and the floor was the entire question.

I typed a third draft that ran nine lines, with subclauses. It set out the possibilities in courteous rows: the recycled SIM, the compromised account, the timestamp bug, the door left open for whoever this really was to step through with dignity intact, no police, no anger, just tell me which one you are. I read it back twice. It was a negotiation. You negotiate with someone who wants something, and four days in, nothing had been asked of me except attention. I deleted it and sat with the empty field and the cursor blinking at whatever rate cursors blink, which I have never measured, which tells you the state I was in.

I typed her name. ปุ้ย. One syllable and a question mark. That one I deleted fastest of all, because a name is a knock, and I know exactly how long I have been declining to knock, and there is a closet in my hallway that could testify.

Who is there. No question mark. A question mark would have been an offered exit, and I did not want to offer the exit. I wanted the sentence to stand in the doorway the way the photographer had stood in mine.

The word อ่านแล้ว appeared under it before the sending animation had finished. Read. Not after a minute, which would have meant a person; not after an hour, which would have meant a person with a life; instantly, in the same breath as delivery — reading and arriving compressed into a single event.

Then nothing. No typing indicator. No rabbit. I sat at the desk with the phone flat in front of me and the wreath five doors away and the water glass holding its level, and I watched the nothing with my full attention until well past her hour, and her hour passed empty, and I want to be precise about what the nothing was, because precision is still the only virtue I have available. The message had been read. Reading is an act. Somewhere — a server floor, a phone-shop drawer, a desk I could not yet imagine in a building I had not yet seen — something had received three words in Thai asking who it was, and had elected, out of every possible reply, silence. And silence, from an account that had spent four days demonstrating it could reach into my bedroom, was not an absence of answer. It was an answer with the modesty switched on.

I had asked. That was the change of state, and I knew it as a change of state even then, with the ink still wet on it. Four days of being observed, and I had finally observed back — one message, three words, logged, read, filed. Whatever had been waiting had not been waiting for proof of anything. It had been waiting, I think now, for exactly this: for me to start asking.
