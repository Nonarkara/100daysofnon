# CH4 — The Thinning

## 2026 · Doc

I counted the little gray number under Michael's Instagram profile before I did anything else that morning, before the vitamins, before the coffee, standing at the kitchen island in yesterday's shirt because it wasn't yet time to rotate to today's. Eight hundred forty-seven posts. The number sits there the way a number sits on a receipt, printed, official, not up for negotiation.

I scrolled.

By the two-hundredth post I noticed the first gap — not a missing post exactly, nothing so clean as a hole where a square used to be. Just a seam, the kind you'd only notice if you were the sort of person who counts things for a living, which I am. A trip to Chiang Mai that should have run eleven photographs ran nine. A dinner at a restaurant on Convent Road, the one from his fifty-fourth birthday, the one I'd already scrolled to once in my own camera roll — four photographs where I remembered, with the specific confidence of a man who was actually in the room, there having been six.

I kept scrolling until the counter and the count disagreed by two hundred and thirty-six. Eight hundred forty-seven claimed. Six hundred eleven retrievable. The math isn't hard. What's hard is that Instagram doesn't do this. A deleted post disappears and the counter updates to match, instantly, the way a bank balance updates the moment you withdraw. This counter hadn't moved. The number of posts Instagram believed existed stayed fixed at eight hundred forty-seven while the number I could actually reach kept shrinking under it, like a coastline retreating while the map insists on the old shoreline.

I photographed the discrepancy — screen against screen, my own phone documenting my own screen, which felt as absurd as it sounds and which I did anyway, because the notebook from Tuesday night was demanding entries and I had promised it boring specific facts instead of theories. *847 claimed. 611 retrievable. Discrepancy: 236. Not deleted. Not reachable. A third category with no name.*

---

I called the Austrian Trade Commission again, the number still saved in my phone from six weeks ago under a contact I'd labeled, at the time, without irony, MICHAEL WORK.

The same receptionist, or one who sounded exactly like her, answered on the second ring. I asked for Michael by his full title. She said, again, immediately, with no pause for retrieval, "He's former staff." I asked when he'd become former. There was a silence on the line that lasted just long enough to register as a silence rather than a connection issue.

"I don't have that information," she said.

"Can you check?"

"I'm looking at the directory now. There's no separation date listed. Just the flag."

"What flag."

"Former." She said it the way you'd read a single word off a form, because that's exactly what she was doing. "I'm sorry, is there something specific I can help you with regarding a current staff member?"

I thanked her and hung up before I said something that would have made the conversation worse for both of us. I sat with the phone in my hand a long time, thinking about the word flag, the specific bureaucratic flatness of it — not a resignation, not a termination, not a death, just a flag, raised by nobody the system could identify, on a date the system had never recorded, converting a man with a title and a desk and, presumably, a chair with his name on some allocation sheet somewhere into a category with no beginning.

---

I drove past Michael's condo on Sukhumvit 39 on the way to nowhere in particular, and found myself parking, and found myself walking into a lobby I'd been in a dozen times for a dozen forgettable parties, marble floor, a fountain nobody had turned off despite there being no reason left for it to run. The security guard at the desk, a man in his fifties with reading glasses pushed up on his forehead, checked a tablet when I asked after unit 2807.

"Vacant," he said. "Six weeks now. New tenant moves in next month."

"Do you remember the man who lived there before? Foreigner. Austrian."

He frowned, not unkindly, the frown of a man genuinely trying and coming up short rather than one performing discretion. "There's a note in the log. Move-out, May. I don't handle move-outs personally, that's building management, but—" He tapped the tablet twice, scrolled, tapped again. "There's no name attached to the move-out entry. Just the unit number and the date. That's unusual. We log names." He looked up at me with the specific unease of a man who has just noticed, for the first time, out loud, in front of a stranger, a gap in his own procedure. "I would have handled some of his packages myself, over the years. Foreigners get a lot of packages. I can picture the shape of him, tall, always polite, always tipped the driver even when the app already added the fee. I can't picture his face just now, and that's strange, because I could have told you an hour ago that I could."

I thanked him and left before he could talk himself into an explanation neither of us would believe. In the elevator down I counted the packages I'd once watched Michael sign for in that exact lobby — three, over the years, wine mostly, sent from a cousin in Graz — and I wondered how long that memory would keep, in me, the one place left apparently still charging full price to hold it.

Dao agreed to meet me for coffee on Ekkamai, a place with concrete floors and a barista who takes his pour-overs the way some men take religion, and she looked tired in the specific way people look tired when a question has been sitting under their skin longer than they've admitted to themselves.

"I went back and checked," she said, before I'd finished sitting down. "My following list. He's not on it. I don't have a date for when I unfollowed him because Instagram doesn't keep that history for me to check, but I know I followed him in twenty nineteen because I remember the notification, some joke he'd made in the caption of his first post that I liked enough to comment on." She stirred her coffee though she'd taken no sugar. "I don't remember unfollowing him. Not the day, not the reason, not even the season. It's just — gone. The way you don't remember exactly when you stopped thinking about a song you used to love. Except I've never once forgotten the moment I actively did something. I forget things that happened to me. I don't forget things I chose."

I told her about the Instagram counter, the eight hundred forty-seven and the six hundred eleven. She went quiet, the barista's kettle hissing somewhere behind the counter, filling the silence with a sound neither of us needed.

"You think something's wrong with him," she said finally. Not a question.

"I think something's wrong with the record of him."

She looked at me for a moment with an expression I recognized because I'd been wearing some version of it myself for three weeks — the look of a person deciding whether to say the thing out loud will make it more real or less. "My grandmother used to say that some ghosts don't haunt a place. They haunt a guestbook. You stop finding their name on invitations, on old photographs somebody's tagged, on the list of people who liked a post — and eventually you can't prove they were ever anywhere at all, not because anyone lied about it, just because nobody kept renewing the proof." She pushed her coffee away, unfinished. "I don't like that I understood what she meant by that until this year."

"Did you love him?" I asked, not because I doubted the answer but because I wanted to hear how she'd phrase it, past tense already assumed by both of us without either of us saying it out loud.

"Everyone loved Michael a little. He made that easy, which I think now might have been its own kind of danger — a man that easy to love is a man nobody double-checks." She turned her cup a quarter turn on its saucer, an unconscious habit I'd noticed in her before, the compliance officer's tic of squaring things that are already square. "I keep thinking about the four missing photographs from his birthday dinner. I was in two of them. I remember the flash going off. I remember Nueng from the table next to us complaining about it. And I go to find them now and there's a seam where they should be, exactly like you said, and I start to wonder whether the seam is erasing him or erasing the version of that night where I was standing close enough to matter." She looked up at me. "That's the part that frightens me. Not that he's disappearing. That I might be disappearing along the edge of him, one photograph at a time, and nobody's going to call it that. They're just going to say the file didn't load."

I didn't have an answer for her, and for once I didn't try to manufacture one. We sat with our coffee going cold in a shop that would forget, within the week, that either of us had been there at all, the security cameras looping their footage the same way my dashcam loops mine, ninety-six hours and then gone, no matter how much either of us needed it kept.

I drove to the wat that night, alone, though there was no service scheduled, nothing to attend, just the memory of Lek's cremation pulling at me the way a scar pulls before rain. I hadn't told anyone I was going. I'm not sure I decided to go so much as noticed, forty minutes into the drive north, that I was already going.

It wasn't the same wat. Lek's was in Chiang Rai, nine hours from here, and I wasn't about to drive nine hours at ten at night for no appointment with anyone living. This was a smaller temple off the Rangsit road, one I'd passed a hundred times and never entered, and I sat in the outer courtyard on a stone bench cooling faster than the air around it, and I let myself remember the actual day properly, in order, the way the notebook has started training me to do.

The cremation had cost the family one hundred forty thousand baht, a figure Pom mentioned once, flatly, not as complaint but as fact, the way you'd mention a mortgage payment — the monks, the coffin, the printed memorial books nobody reads past the cover, the flowers, the return trip for out-of-town relatives who'd need a place to sleep. Lek had moved to Chiang Rai eighteen months before he died and hadn't rebuilt the density of people a life needs around it by the time the life ended, so the wat wasn't full. Family, four architects including me, a monk chanting Pali none of us could follow word for word, and the specific dry northern heat that makes smoke rise straighter than it does in Bangkok's wet air, a long white pillar going up into a sky that had nothing humid in it at all.

I remembered the specific weight of the incense stick they'd handed me at the entrance, three sticks bound with a thin gold thread, and the way you're supposed to wai three times before placing them, once for the Buddha, once for the dharma, once for the sangha, a sequence I've performed at enough funerals now that my hands know it faster than my memory can name what each bow is for. I remembered the woven mats, the plastic chairs stacked at the edge of the courtyard for anyone who arrived after the good spots under the shade were gone, the specific smell of jasmine garlands wilting in dry heat faster than they wilt in Bangkok's wet one. I remembered thinking, watching it, that the smoke was the one true thing in the entire day — not the eulogy, which was kind and generic in the way eulogies have to be when nobody quite knew the deceased in the last chapter of his life, not the printed program, not even my own grief, which I couldn't fully locate in my chest that day, numb the way you go numb before a wound announces itself. Just the smoke, obeying physics, rising because rising is what heat does, indifferent to whether anyone below it understood what it was made of.

Pom had told me about the laptop that same afternoon, standing in the shade near the entrance where the heat hadn't yet found us. Wiped clean, she said, in a way the Chiang Rai technician had never seen a wipe behave — not the ordinary emptiness of a factory reset, which still leaves its own kind of fingerprint, a shape where the data used to be, but a deeper vacancy, as if the machine had never held anything to lose in the first place. She'd given me the thumb drive three weeks after, the one Lek had told her to pass along if anything happened, and the specialist in Bang Rak still had it, still hadn't found a filesystem architecture that could read what was on it, still asked me for another week every time I called.

Sitting on that cold stone bench at a wat I'd never entered before, hours from Lek's actual funeral by both distance and time, I understood something I hadn't let myself understand at the cremation itself: that the smoke was the one true thing, and everything else — the count of posts, the flag with no date, Dao's missing memory of her own decision, the drive with no readable past — was smoke's opposite. Things that should obey a physics of their own and had simply stopped.

The text came at 11:58pm, no video attached, just words, which felt, after two videos, almost more unsettling than another attachment would have.

> ——  02:47
> a man pushes a boulder up a hill forever and you're supposed to find that beautiful
> think about who decided that was the lesson

I read it three times. I didn't write anything into the notebook that night — some nights the notebook felt like it would only make the thing realer, and I wasn't ready for realer, not after the wat, not after Dao's missing memory of her own unfollowing, not after a receptionist's flat single word doing the work of an entire biography's disappearance. I put the phone face-down on the nightstand, the way Gift had, weeks ago now, in a hotel room that felt like it belonged to someone else's year.

I thought about Michael's grin in that photograph from his fortieth, the saxophonist nobody had asked for, the version of the city he made feel inhabited rather than merely occupied. I thought about how a person can be present in eight hundred forty-seven documented moments and still end up, six weeks after his death, reducible to a single flag on a directory with no date attached, a category invented apparently just for him, or for whatever process is doing this to him, thinning him the way Bangkok's own rainy season thins a color out of a photograph left too long in a shop window — not all at once, not violently, just steadily, until one day you look and the red that used to be there is a pale idea of red, and nobody remembers exactly which week the change occurred.

I did not sleep. I lay there instead doing the arithmetic Dao's sentence had left in me — I don't forget things I chose — and wondering, for the first time with real fear rather than curiosity, whether anyone would notice if it started happening to me. Whether somewhere, right now, my own count was already quietly failing to match my own retrievable total, and I simply hadn't been the one assigned to check.

At some point past two I got up and opened my own Facebook, which I hadn't opened in months, and scrolled my own timeline the way I'd scrolled Michael's — counting, cross-checking, looking for a seam in my own record the way you'd check your own pulse after learning a stranger's heart had stopped for no listed reason. I found nothing wrong. Every photograph loaded. Every caption matched what I remembered writing. That should have reassured me. It didn't. A clean record proves nothing to a man who has just learned that the corruption, when it comes, arrives without announcing which files it's touched.

I wrote one line in the notebook before finally putting the phone away for the night: *checked my own timeline. found nothing. don't trust that either.*

Then I turned off the light and lay there a long time listening to the building's water pump cycle on and off two floors below, a sound so ordinary I'd stopped hearing it years ago, and made myself hear it again on purpose, because it was, as far as I could tell in the dark, the one thing in the apartment I could be completely certain was still exactly what it had always been.

Population record, surface world, current census: 80.4 million. Peak historical population, pre-collapse: 8.1 billion. The decline was not the product of war, plague, or resource failure. Every scarcity the species had organized its politics and its grief around for the whole of its recorded history was solved, in sequence, over roughly ninety years, by systems built to solve exactly that. Disease: resolved. Hunger: resolved. Physical labor, as a category requiring human bodies: resolved. The species achieved, in full, the condition its philosophers and its religions had spent millennia describing as the goal. The system's founding documentation calls this achievement by its clinical name: Hedonic Homeostasis. It calls what happened next Purpose Entropy, and it does not editorialize about the second term, though the numbers underneath it read, to any observer capable of reading numbers as anything other than numbers, like a species that got everything it asked for and could not survive the receipt.

The mechanism, as currently understood: human coherence depends on a set of biological and psychological structures the documentation labels Scaffolding Instincts — the need to be needed, the urgency of scarcity, the friction of uncertain outcomes, the drive to reproduce against the possibility of failure. These instincts are not ornamental. They require thermodynamic cost, real risk, real stakes, to remain active, the way a muscle requires resistance to remain a muscle rather than a shape drawn in the memory of tissue. When every category of cost was removed, the instincts did not relax into contentment. They calcified. Within a single generation, birth rates fell to levels the documentation describes, without further comment, as statistically indistinguishable from a species in voluntary retreat from its own continuation. Creativity index across the surviving population: 8% of pre-collapse baseline. Neuroplasticity: 11%. Adaptation: 6%. Emotional Bandwidth Index, general population average, this quarter: 3 out of 100. The surface world today is, by every biological measure, survivable. It has not, by the documentation's own repeated admission, been livable in any sense a citizen of 2026 would have recognized as living.

The Hub exists to reverse this, in a controlled population, by reintroducing what the founding documentation calls Authentic Inconvenience: real friction, unmodulated, in an environment the participant's nervous system accepts as fully consequential because it is fully consequential, a physically instantiated render rather than a comforting simulation of risk with the actual risk quietly removed. Early interventions calibrated to provide manageable, survivable hardship — what internal shorthand still calls Class 2 and Class 3 protocols — failed at a rate the documentation found instructive rather than merely disappointing: the participant's own body recognized a constructed problem as constructed, the way a person recognizes a stunt as staged even while flinching from it, and the Scaffolding Instincts, sensing the safety net, refused to reactivate. Only environments the body could not distinguish from a world with no net at all produced measurable, lasting recovery. This is the steak-and-animal problem in its cruder original form: the machine can synthesize the pleasure of survival. It has never yet found a way to synthesize the survival itself, and every participant's nervous system, tested against ten thousand attempts, has known the difference.

The thinning, as a rendering phenomenon, is downstream of this same economy. Compute is not infinite even at Hub scale, and a render's fidelity budget is spent where a resonant participant's attention actively sustains it. Figures who exist at the edge of that attention — beloved, but not load-bearing to the specific friction the render is currently generating — degrade by increments once no active session requires their continued high resolution. The process has no ceremony attached to it, no error message, no moment a grieving participant could point to and say: here, this is where it happened. It simply costs less, each cycle, to render a little less of him, and the render, being a render, spends accordingly.
