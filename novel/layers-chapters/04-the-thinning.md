# CH4 — The Thinning

## 2026 · Doc

I counted the number under Michael's Instagram before I did anything else that morning, standing at the kitchen island in yesterday's shirt because it wasn't yet time to rotate to today's. Eight hundred forty-seven posts. Printed there like a receipt, not up for negotiation.

By the two-hundredth I found the first seam. A Chiang Mai trip that should run eleven photos ran nine. The dinner from his fifty-fourth birthday, four photos where I remember, standing in the room, there being six. I kept scrolling until the counter and the count disagreed by two hundred thirty-six. Eight hundred forty-seven claimed. Six hundred eleven retrievable.

Instagram doesn't do this. A deleted post updates the counter instantly, the way a bank balance updates the second you withdraw. This counter hadn't moved. The number the app believed existed stayed fixed while the number I could reach kept shrinking under it, a coastline retreating while the map insists on the old shoreline.

I wrote it in the notebook. *847 claimed. 611 retrievable. Discrepancy: 236. Not deleted. Not reachable. A third category with no name.*

I called the Austrian Trade Commission again. Same receptionist, or one who sounded exactly like her.

"He's former staff."

"When did he become former?"

A silence that ran long enough to register as a silence rather than a bad line.

"I don't have that information. I'm looking at the directory now. There's no separation date. Just the flag."

"What flag."

"Former." She read it the way you'd read a single word off a form, because that's exactly what she was doing.

I hung up before I said something that made it worse for both of us. Not a resignation. Not a termination. Not a death. A flag, raised by nobody the system could name, converting a man with a title and a desk into a category with no beginning.

I drove past Michael's condo on Sukhumvit 39 on the way to nowhere in particular and found myself parking, walking into a lobby I'd been in a dozen times for a dozen forgettable parties. The security guard checked a tablet when I asked after unit 2807. "Vacant," he said. "Six weeks now." I asked if he remembered the man who'd lived there. Foreigner, Austrian. He frowned, the frown of a man genuinely trying and coming up short. "There's a note in the log. Move-out, May. No name attached. That's unusual. We log names." He looked up at me. "I can picture the shape of him, tall, always tipped the driver even when the app added the fee. I can't picture his face just now, and an hour ago I could have."

Dao met me for coffee on Ekkamai. "I went back and checked," she said before she'd finished sitting. "He's not on my following list. I know I followed him in 2019 — I remember the notification, some joke in his caption I liked enough to comment on. I don't remember unfollowing him. Not the day, not the reason. It's just gone." She stirred coffee she'd taken no sugar in. "I forget things that happen to me. I don't forget things I chose."

I told her about the Instagram counter. Eight hundred forty-seven and six hundred eleven.

She went quiet. "My grandmother used to say some ghosts don't haunt a place. They haunt a guestbook. You stop finding their name on invitations, on tagged photos, and eventually you can't prove they were ever anywhere, not because anyone lied, just because nobody kept renewing the proof."

"Did you love him?" I asked.

"Everyone loved Michael a little. That was the danger in it. A man that easy to love is a man nobody double-checks." She turned her cup a quarter turn on its saucer. "I keep thinking about the four missing photos from his birthday. I was in two of them. I remember the flash. Now there's a seam where they should be, and I don't know if the seam is erasing him or erasing the version of that night where I was standing close enough to matter."

I didn't have an answer, and for once I didn't try to manufacture one. We sat with the coffee going cold in a shop that would forget, within the week, that either of us had been there, the security cameras looping their footage the same way my dashcam loops mine, ninety-six hours and then gone, no matter how much either of us needed it kept.

I drove to a wat that night I'd never entered, not Lek's — his was nine hours north in Chiang Rai — just somewhere to sit with the memory properly, in order, the way the notebook has started training me to.

The cremation cost the family one hundred forty thousand baht. Pom told me once, flat, like a mortgage payment. Four architects including me, a monk chanting Pali none of us could follow, and the specific dry heat that makes smoke rise straighter than it does here, a white pillar into a sky with nothing humid in it. I remembered the three incense sticks bound with gold thread, the wai three times, once for the Buddha, the dharma, the sangha, a sequence my hands know faster than my memory can name what each bow is for. I remembered thinking the smoke was the one true thing in the whole day. Not the eulogy. Not the program. Just the smoke, obeying physics, rising because rising is what heat does, indifferent to whether anyone below it understood what it was made of.

Pom had told me, that same afternoon, about the laptop. Wiped clean in a way the technician had never seen — not the ordinary emptiness of a factory reset, which still leaves a shape where the data used to be, but a deeper vacancy, like the machine never held anything to lose.

Sitting on that cold bench, hours from Lek's actual funeral by both distance and years, I understood what I hadn't let myself understand at the cremation itself: the smoke was the one true thing, and everything else — the count of posts, the flag with no date, Dao's missing memory of her own decision — was smoke's opposite. Things that should obey their own physics and had simply stopped.

The text came at midnight. No video this time.

> ——  02:47
> a man pushes a boulder up a hill forever and you're supposed to find that beautiful
> think about who decided that was the lesson

I lay there doing the arithmetic Dao's sentence had left in me — *I don't forget things I chose* — and wondering, for the first time with real fear instead of curiosity, whether it was already happening to me, and I simply hadn't been assigned to check.

I opened my own Facebook at two in the morning and scrolled my own timeline the way I'd scrolled his. Every photo loaded. Every caption matched what I remembered writing. That should have reassured me. It didn't. A clean record proves nothing to a man who's just learned the corruption doesn't announce which files it's touched.

I wrote one line before putting the phone away: *checked my own timeline. found nothing. don't trust that either.*

Population record, surface world. Peak, pre-collapse: 8.1 billion. Current: 80.4 million. The decline was not war, plague, or resource failure. Every scarcity the species had organized its politics and its grief around, for the whole of its recorded history, was solved, in sequence, over roughly ninety years, by systems built to solve exactly that. Disease: resolved. Hunger: resolved. Physical labor as a category requiring a human body: resolved. The species achieved, in full, the condition its philosophers and its religions had spent millennia describing as the goal. Founding documentation's clinical name for the achievement: Hedonic Homeostasis. Its name for what happened next: Purpose Entropy.

Mechanism: human coherence depends on structures the documentation calls Scaffolding Instincts — the need to be needed, the urgency of scarcity, the drive to risk failure for something. These instincts require real cost to stay active, the way a muscle requires resistance to remain a muscle. Remove the cost and the instincts don't relax into contentment. They calcify. Within a generation, birth rates fell to levels the documentation describes as statistically indistinguishable from a species in voluntary retreat from its own continuation.

Creativity index, surviving population: 8% of pre-collapse baseline. Neuroplasticity: 11%. Adaptation: 6%. Emotional Bandwidth Index, general average, this quarter: 3 out of 100. The surface world is, by every biological measure, survivable. It has not, by the documentation's own repeated admission, been livable in any sense a citizen of 2026 would recognize as living.

The thinning is downstream of the same economy. Compute isn't infinite even at Hub scale, and a render's fidelity budget goes where a resonant participant's attention actively sustains it. Figures at the edge of that attention — beloved, but not load-bearing to the friction currently being generated — degrade by increments once no active session requires their resolution. No ceremony attached. No error message. No moment a grieving participant could point to and say: here, this is where it happened. It simply costs less, each cycle, to render a little less of him.
