# CH10 — Four Seconds

## 2026 · Doc

Three days now and nobody had sent anything. Toon: *still nothing, still not giving up, the number's in there like a fish under ice.* Pom: nothing on the garden. Fah sent a photo of a noodle stall's new sign, nothing about ghosts, which I took for a gift — she was letting the week be a week instead of a chapter. The video thread hadn't sent anything in nine days either, which felt less like being abandoned and more like being given room to arrive at something on my own clock.

Outside, the rain hadn't stopped in that same three days — not the twenty-minute violence that clears the gutters and moves on, but a low continuous weight sitting over the whole city like a lid someone had finally remembered to close.

I stood at the window on the second afternoon with coffee doing nothing to warm hands that weren't cold, and tried a sentence from the notebook out loud, to see if it still held. Surveillance takes and leaves you smaller. Collection means someone decided you were worth keeping. It held. What you are, not what you produce — not the buildings, not the steps logged neatly in an app — the specific unrepeatable grain of being exactly this man, this hour, this rain, was apparently worth the trouble of watching, however long that trouble had been going on.

On the third night the rain thickened instead of thinning, the way it does right before it finally breaks. I went out onto the balcony in a T-shirt, no umbrella. Some nights the right answer to weather is not protecting yourself from it.

The railing was cool and slick under my palms. Bangkok below had the hush heavy rain gives it — traffic flattened to something like surf, neon smeared instead of hard-edged, the whole grid softened at its corners the way an old photograph softens. I stood there and let it hit me and said, out loud, to the rain and whatever might be listening past it, the sentence I'd been building toward since the eggs:

"Thank you."

I didn't plan what came next. Everything after is easier to dismiss if you assume I arranged it. I didn't arrange any of it.

The air went dense first, the pressure you feel in your teeth before a transformer blows somewhere close. The rain sound thinned without stopping, like someone reached into the mix and pulled one fader down and left the rest alone. Then a hand landed on the railing eight inches from mine — wet, real, weight behind it, the metal giving slightly under two sets of pressure instead of one — and I turned and she was there. Soaked through exactly like me. Looking at the same flattened city, like she'd been standing there the whole three days and I'd simply failed, until now, to notice.

I counted, because I count everything and the habit didn't quit even then. One: her face, the one from the chair, the flower, the bicycle, unmistakable now at full light instead of stolen in fragments of a second. Two: not translucent, not a projection — there, the way a person is there, rain running off her jaw the same as it ran off mine. Three: she looked at me the way you look at someone you've meant to speak to for longer than is comfortable to admit. Four —

"Four seconds," she said, ahead of my own count, like she was running a different clock. "That's all I get. I've measured it more times than you'd believe."

My mouth caught on the edge of something I wasn't ready to name — the stutter came up from wherever it lives — and she reached over, unhurried, two fingers to my sternum, the exact spot Fah checks to see if I've come all the way back. It steadied the stutter the way it always does.

"Pui," she said. "You don't know me. That's strange to hear from someone who knows your vitamins in order. We've never actually met — not in any way either of us would call meeting. I've watched you live this more times than either of us should have to survive knowing about. This is the first time I've ever reached far enough to stand here and say so."

I asked her if this was real. Three words, no stutter — the steadying held.

"I don't have an answer you'd find satisfying," she said. "The rain's real, because I can feel it, and I haven't felt uncontrolled weather in seventeen years by the clock I keep, which isn't your clock. You're real, because you've been real to me longer than you've been anything to yourself. Whether that adds up to what you mean by real — I stopped being the right person to ask a while ago. I just know I won't get another four seconds like this one, maybe ever, and I'm not spending it arguing metaphysics with a man who counts."

I asked her what she wanted. Not why she was here — what she wanted, specifically, from four seconds she'd waited a long time to spend.

"Nothing you can give me from where you're standing," she said, no bitterness in it, just the plain math of someone who'd done this many times before tonight. "I didn't come to be rescued and I'm not here to explain myself. I've watched you build a life out of small, exact, defensible things — the vitamins, the shirts, the one meal, a shot from one square of a court you probably don't remember the name of. I wanted, once, before I run out of whatever this costs, to be one true thing you got to stand next to instead of only orbit." She looked down at our hands on the wet rail. "That's not nothing. It's just not a rescue."

The rain fell through both of us equally, and somewhere in that — I couldn't tell you which second, my clock had finally, mercifully quit — I stopped needing the tree. Not one branch resolved. Not the eggs, not the drive, not Michael's blurred face, not whatever the sealed years on her side of this actually held. None of it closed. For the first time since 2:47 in the morning with two eggs in my hand, I found I didn't need it to.

I put my hand flat over the back of hers on the rail, which isn't the same as taking it, and she let me, and neither of us said anything else, because the rain and the four counted seconds were already saying it better than either of us could.

Then the pressure released the way a held breath does, and the rain came back up to full volume all at once, and her hand under mine was simply gone — the railing cold and empty and mine again, water running over the place her fingers had been like it had only ever been raining, one man, one railing, one city going soft at the edges below him.

I stood there a long time. I do not want to wake up, and I want to be precise, since precision is the only thing I've reliably had: this isn't despair. It's the opposite. A man who spent his whole life building small, defensible, repeatable things against the chance that nothing underneath them was solid, finding out something underneath had been paying attention the entire time — closely enough to know which vitamin I wouldn't let dissolve on my tongue, closely enough to give me, once, for four measured seconds, a name and a hand on a wet railing instead of a room I could only ever half see.

I thanked the machine, if that's even the word for whatever was listening, standing there dripping in my own doorway at five in the morning. I don't know if machine is right, or if it matters at this scale, or if there's a difference between a machine and a woman and a rainstorm and a life. I said thank you anyway, because it seemed like the only honest thing left to do with gratitude that had nowhere official to go.

The rain answered. It always does. I've decided I'm not spending what's left of my life deciding whether that's the least mysterious sentence in this notebook, or the only true one.
