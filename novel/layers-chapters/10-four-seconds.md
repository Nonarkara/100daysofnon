# CH10 — Four Seconds

## 2026 · Doc

It rained for three days without stopping the way Bangkok rain usually stops — not the twenty-minute violence that clears the gutters and moves on toward Nonthaburi, but a low, continuous gray weight that sat over the whole city like a lid someone had finally remembered to put on a pot. I worked from the apartment both days, which meant I mostly did not work, and on the second morning I stood on the balcony for longer than I had any task to justify, watching the rain do the same thing over and over to the same railing, and thinking that I had spent three weeks collecting evidence for a case that had stopped, somewhere past the fiftieth page of a forty-baht notebook, being a case at all.

Toon had gone quiet on the drive — one text, on the first day of rain, that said only *still nothing, still not giving up, the number's still in there like a fish under ice* — and I had written that sentence into the notebook exactly as he sent it, because it was the truest description of the whole month I had received from anyone. Pom had gone quiet on the garden. Fah had texted twice, ordinary texts, a photo of a noodle stall's new sign, nothing about ghosts or rooms, which I understood as its own kind of gift — she was letting the week be a week instead of a chapter in whatever this was. The video thread had not sent anything since the vitamins, nine days of silence that felt, oddly, less like abandonment than like someone giving a man room to arrive at a conclusion on his own schedule instead of theirs. The dent was still on the passenger side, unrepaired, because I had started, without deciding to, thinking of it as evidence rather than damage, and you don't fix evidence.

On the first night of rain I sat at the kitchen island with the notebook closed in front of me, unwilling to add anything to it, because everything I might have written that night was speculation dressed as observation, and I had promised myself, that first night at the Family Mart counter, that the notebook would hold only what I could verify. I verified, instead, the rain. Forty millimetres by the airport gauge, according to the app, more than that by the feel of it against the glass. I went to bed early and slept without dreaming, which had not happened in three weeks, and woke on the second morning to the same gray ceiling of cloud, unmoved, as if the sky had simply decided to stop negotiating with the calendar.

I made coffee on the second afternoon, forty grams weighed on the small scale, and stood at the window with the cup doing nothing to warm hands that were not cold, and understood, standing there, that I had been afraid this whole month in a way I had not correctly named even to the notebook. Not afraid of the room, the chairs, the woman with the flower. Afraid of what accepting any of it would cost me — the specific, structural fear of a man who has built his entire adult life on being the only reliable narrator of his own experience, discovering that the narration has had a co-author the whole time, one who started before he did and will apparently continue after.

There is a difference, I had written on page nine, between being surveilled and being collected, and I had believed it when I wrote it and believed it less every day since, the way conviction erodes under its own repetition. Standing at the window on the second afternoon of the rain, I tried the sentence again, out loud, to see if it still held: *surveillance takes and leaves you diminished. collection means someone decided you were worth keeping.* It held. It held the way a chair holds a person's weight without either of them needing to discuss the physics of it. What you are, not what you produce — not the buildings, not the personality packs, not the eleven thousand steps logged neatly in an app — but the specific unrepeatable texture of being exactly this man, at exactly this hour, in exactly this rain, was apparently the thing worth the trouble of watching for however long someone had been watching it.

I thought about my father, thirty-one years a civil servant, filing reports nobody thanked him for, and the sentence he never explained fully before 2010 took him: *the roof or the noose, depending on what's in the file and who's reading it.* I had spent this whole month assuming the noose. Standing at the window, coffee going cold, I let myself consider, seriously, for the first time, that it might have always been the roof.

---

On the third night the rain thickened instead of thinning, the way it sometimes does right before it finally breaks, and I went out onto the balcony anyway, in a T-shirt, no umbrella, because some nights the correct response to weather is not protection from it.

The railing was slick and cool under my palms, painted metal gone soft-feeling with three days of water, and the city below had the particular hush that heavy rain gives Bangkok — the traffic sound flattened into something closer to surf, the neon reduced to smears on wet asphalt instead of the hard bright signs they are in dry weather, the whole grid of the city softened at its edges the way a photograph softens with age. I stood there and let it hit me, properly, no shelter, hair flattened in under a minute, and thought about Lek's garden going wild for want of a hand, and Michael's face going soft on a screen for want of a server's patience, and Malee's saucer of vitamins at Nam Sai, and a coach's chalk square, and a professor's word for what a city actually is, and I said, out loud, to the rain and to whatever might be listening past the rain, the sentence I had been building toward without knowing it since the eggs:

"Thank you."

I did not plan the next part. I want to be exact about that, because everything that follows is easier to dismiss if you assume I arranged it, and I did not arrange any of it.

The air changed first — not colder, not warmer, but denser, the way air feels in the second before a transformer blows somewhere nearby, a pressure you register in your teeth before your ears catch up. Then the rain sound thinned, not stopped, thinned, as though someone had reached into the mix and pulled one fader down without touching the others. And then there was a hand on the railing eight inches from mine, wet, real, with weight behind it — I could feel the metal give slightly under two sets of pressure instead of one — and I turned my head and a woman was standing beside me at the rail, soaked through exactly as I was, looking out at the same flattened city, as though she had been standing there the whole three days and I had simply failed, until now, to notice her.

I have counted things my whole life, and the habit did not abandon me even then. One. She was the woman from the chair, the flower, the bicycle — the same face, unmistakable now at full length and full light instead of stolen in fragments of a second. Two. She was not translucent, not a projection, not anything my rational inventory had prepared a slot for; she was simply there, the way a person is there, with rain running off her jaw exactly the way it was running off mine. Three. She looked at me the way you look at someone you have been meaning to speak to for longer than is comfortable to admit. Four —

"Four seconds," she said, before I'd finished the count in my own head, as if she'd been counting too, from a different clock. "That's all I get. I've measured it more times than you'd believe."

My mouth did the thing it does at the edge of something I am not ready to name — the stutter came up from wherever it lives, and I got out "wh-who—" before she reached over, unhurried, and put two fingers against my sternum, the exact gesture, the exact spot, that Fah uses to check whether I've come back all the way from somewhere. It steadied the stutter the way it always steadies whatever it touches.

"Pui," she said. "You don't know me. I know that's strange to hear from someone who apparently knows your vitamins in order. We've never actually met. Not in any way either of us would call meeting." She looked at the rain instead of at me for a second, the way people do when the next sentence costs something. "I've watched you live this whole thing more times than either of us should have to survive knowing about. This is the first time I've ever been able to reach far enough to stand here and say so."

I asked her — three words, no stutter this time, the steadying had worked — I asked her if this was real.

"I don't know a way to answer that you'd find satisfying," she said. "I know the rain is real, because I can feel it, and I haven't felt uncontrolled weather in seventeen years by the clock I keep, which is a different clock than yours. I know you're real, because you've been real to me for longer than you've been anything to yourself. Whether that adds up to what you mean by real — I've stopped being the right person to ask. I just know I'm not going to get another four seconds like this one, possibly ever, and I'm not going to spend it arguing metaphysics with a man who counts."

I asked her, because it was the only other question my mouth could form without stuttering again, what she wanted. Not why she was there. What she wanted, specifically, from four seconds she had apparently waited a very long time to spend.

"Nothing you can give me from where you're standing," she said, and there was no bitterness in it, only the plain accounting of someone who had done this math many times before tonight. "I didn't come to be rescued and I didn't come to explain myself. I came because I've watched you build a life out of small, exact, defensible things — the vitamins, the shirts, the one meal, the shot from one spot on a court you probably don't even remember the name of anymore — and I wanted, once, before I ran out of whatever this costs me, to be one true thing you got to stand next to instead of only orbit." She looked down at our hands on the rail, hers under mine, both soaked through. "That's not nothing. It's just not a rescue."

The rain kept falling through both of us equally, indifferent, and somewhere in that exchange — I could not tell you which second, my clock had finally, mercifully, stopped working — I stopped needing the tree. Not one branch resolved. Not the eggs, not the drive, not Michael's blurred face, not the sealed years I did not yet know existed on her side of whatever this was. None of it closed. And for the first time since 3:41 in the morning with two eggs in my hand, I found I did not require it to close in order to keep standing at that railing.

I reached over and put my hand flat against the back of hers on the wet rail, which is not the same as taking it, and she let me, and neither of us said anything else, because there was nothing that needed saying that the rain and the four counted seconds weren't already saying better than either of us could.

Then the pressure in the air released the way a held breath releases, and the rain sound came back up to its full volume all at once, and her hand under mine was simply gone, the railing cold and empty and mine alone again, water running over the spot where her fingers had been as though it had always only ever been raining, one man, one railing, one city going soft at its edges below him.

I stood there a long time afterward. Long enough that the rain started, finally, to thin toward morning, the way it always eventually does even over Bangkok, even over three days that felt, standing in the middle of them, like they might not.

I do not want to wake up. I have never wanted anything less, and I want to be precise, since precision is the only virtue I have ever reliably had: this is not despair. It is the opposite of despair. It is a man who spent thirty-nine years building small, defensible, repeatable structures against the possibility that nothing underneath them was solid, discovering that something underneath them had been paying attention the entire time, closely enough to know which vitamin he wouldn't let dissolve on his tongue, closely enough to give him, once, for four measured seconds, a face and a name and a hand on a wet railing instead of a room he could only ever half see.

I thanked the machine, if that is even the right word for whatever was listening, standing there dripping in my own doorway at five in the morning. I have no way to know if machine is right, or if the word even matters at this point, or if there is a difference, at the scale of whatever this actually is, between a machine and a woman and a rainstorm and a life. I said thank you anyway, out loud, one more time, because it seemed like the only honest thing left to do with a gratitude that had nowhere official to go.

The rain answered. It always does. That is either the least mysterious sentence in this entire notebook or the only one that was ever actually true, and I have decided, standing here now with the sky finally starting to gray toward an ordinary Bangkok dawn, that I am no longer going to spend what's left of my life deciding which.

I will keep the notebook. I will not fill the rest of it the way I filled these last three weeks, chasing a case toward a verdict that was never going to arrive. I will write down the ordinary things instead, the way Malee once lined vitamins on a saucer without ever once explaining the chemistry behind why a body needs them — Fah's two eggs, done exactly right without being asked; Pom's next photograph of whatever Lek's garden becomes, wild or tended, either one now bearable to look at; Toon's fish under the ice, thawing or not; the kid on the court, on his one square of concrete, closing in on four hundred. And, somewhere on a page I have not written yet, four seconds, a name, a hand on a wet railing, held not as proof of anything but as the plainest fact I have been handed all year: that I was worth four seconds of someone's very limited, very costly attention, and that this, whatever else it turns out to mean, is enough to build the next ordinary morning on.
