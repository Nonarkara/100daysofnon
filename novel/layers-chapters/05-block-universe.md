I watched it standing up, coffee forgotten on the counter going cold at exactly the rate coffee goes cold, which is to say according to a law that doesn't care whether you're watching it happen.

It ran eight minutes and it explained, in the same flat unhurried voice as the other two, an idea I'd half-learned once in a physics elective at MIT and half-forgotten on purpose, the way you forget a fact you suspect will cost you something if you keep it close: that the distinction between past, present, and future may not be a real feature of the universe at all, only a persistent trick of the particular instrument doing the observing. That relativity, taken seriously, doesn't just say time is relative to your speed — it says two observers moving differently can each, correctly, place the same distant event in each other's future or each other's past, with neither of them wrong, which only makes sense if all of it, every moment anyone has ever called now, already exists, laid out whole, the way a reel of film exists whole in the can before any projector runs a single frame of it through a light.

Einstein wrote a letter, the video said, to the family of a friend who'd just died, and told them that people like us, who believe in physics, know the distinction between past, present, and future is only a stubbornly persistent illusion. The video played that line twice, the second time slower, as if it wanted to be sure I'd heard the word illusion attached to something as load-bearing as a friend's death.

I sat down on the floor of my own kitchen, coffee cold on the counter above me, and let that sit in my chest the way a diagnosis sits, which is to say: not fully believed yet, and already, somehow, fully felt.

I thought about Lek, whose small handwriting I can no longer read without glasses, and about the specific horror of learning that his death might not have been an event that happened to him at a particular Tuesday in May so much as a frame that had simply always existed, sitting there in the reel since before either of us was born, waiting for the projector's light to reach it. If that's true, nobody killed him and nothing failed in him. The frame was just always going to say May. I don't know if that's more merciful than the alternative or considerably less. A death you can blame at least gives grief somewhere to stand. A death that was simply always true gives grief nowhere to go but everywhere at once, all the way back to the beginning of the reel, which is, I suppose, the whole point of showing me this particular video the same week I buried a paper notebook's worth of evidence for a different theory entirely.

I called the AI, out of some reflex I don't fully understand even now, and asked it, directly, whether it believed the future already existed. It answered, in the tone it always uses for questions outside its working domain, that it doesn't hold beliefs in the sense I meant, only weighted probabilities across a training distribution, and that the physics literature on block universe interpretations remained genuinely contested among people whose job it was to know. It offered, unprompted, three papers I could read if I wanted more. I did not want more. I wanted it to be scared with me for a minute, and it is, whatever else it is, not built for that, and I sat there in the silence after its answer feeling more alone than the question itself had made me feel.

If the whole reel already exists — Lek's cremation, Michael's flag with no date, the dent I don't remember making, the four seconds two nights from now that I haven't lived yet and can't currently imagine — then nothing I do this morning is deciding anything. I am simply the projector's light passing, this instant, over a frame that was always going to be exactly this. The choice I feel myself making, to stand up, to drink the coffee anyway even cold, was never a choice in any sense that would matter to a physicist. It was already printed. I was only the part of the system slow enough to experience the printing as if it were happening now.

I decided, standing at the sink, coffee finally drunk lukewarm out of some stubbornness that felt, in the moment, like defiance and in retrospect was probably just thrift, that I would spend the day trying to prove the video wrong. Not intellectually — I know better than to think I could out-argue a video that clearly knew more physics than I remembered. Physically. I would make a choice today so genuinely unscripted, so untethered from anything predictable about who I am, that no reel could have contained it in advance.

It is, I recognize even as I attempt it, a stupid experiment. A man trying to prove he has free will by choosing randomly is still, at the level that matters, only doing what a man who read a video about determinism at 6am would predictably do. But I have never claimed to be immune to my own patterns. I've just started, this year, to notice them.

I flipped a coin at every intersection instead of turning the direction habit would have taken me. Heads, left. Tails, right. I walked past my usual soi, the one with the som tam cart that sets its mortar out before its ice, and forced myself two blocks further before doubling back on a route I've never once used to reach the same destination. I let a stranger's suggestion — a tuk-tuk driver idling outside a 7-Eleven, bored, who called out that the flower market smelled incredible today, you should go look — send me forty minutes out of my way to Pak Khlong Talat, where I have not stood since I was maybe nine years old, walking between buckets of orchids and marigolds strung into garlands with my mother's hand tight around mine because she'd read somewhere that children get lost in markets, which of course they do, which of course I once did, in a different market entirely, for eleven minutes that felt like my whole remaining life.

The flower market at midday smelled the way I remembered it and also entirely unlike I remembered it, both true, the way most returns to childhood places are — jasmine and diesel and something sweetly rotting underneath both, vendors calling prices in a rhythm too fast for a tourist's ear but perfectly legible to mine, forty years of listening to this exact cadence apparently enough to keep it filed somewhere I could still retrieve on demand. I bought nothing. I stood a long time in front of a bucket of dok rak, the white funeral flower, the smell of Bangkok rain sitting inside a flower that had never once, itself, been rained on, and thought about Lek and Michael and the woman I haven't told you about yet — the woman from Krabi, the one whose name I still can't write down in the notebook without my hand slowing, the one who drowned or didn't, seven years before any of this started, the one who once told me, at nineteen, watching me design something structurally reckless and full of more ambition than sense, that it needs to stand up, the single most useful sentence anyone has ever handed me about a building or a life.

I did not choose to think of her. I want to be precise: I was standing in a flower market I hadn't visited in thirty-six years, having arrived there by a coin flip and a stranger's bored suggestion, and she arrived in my chest anyway, uninvited, exactly the way she always has, which is either proof that grief doesn't obey randomness any better than habit does, or proof that some frames in the reel were always going to contain her no matter which route the projector's light took to reach that hour of the day.

She built one building in her life, a small library north of Chiang Mai, roof tiles salvaged from a school that had been demolished the year before, so that children reading in the new building sat in light that used to fall on somebody else's classroom. She captioned the last photograph she ever posted of it with four Thai words I have never once needed translated: มันกำลังจะเกิดขึ้น. It's going to happen. I used to read that caption as pure architect's optimism, a woman proud of a roofline finally clearing inspection. I read it differently now, standing in front of a bucket of white flowers thirty-six years and one bad theory of time away from the last conversation we ever had. If the whole reel already exists, then whatever she meant by that sentence had already happened the moment she typed it, and I am only now, years later, the slow part of the light finally reaching the frame where I understand it.

A vendor near the dok rak bucket, an older woman with hands stained permanent yellow from turmeric she also sold, asked if I wanted a garland made up, forty baht, ready in five minutes. I told her no thank you, not today, and she nodded without any particular investment in my answer, already turning to the next customer, a young man buying an armful of marigolds for a spirit house I could see, over his shoulder, freshly built at the mouth of the market — new wood, still pale, waiting for its first offering.

By six in the evening I had walked eleven thousand steps without effort, the way I always do, my body running its own long-installed program regardless of what my mind thought it was proving. I decided that wasn't enough. Eleven thousand was the number of a man on autopilot. I wanted a number that cost me something.

I kept walking. Through Silom at the hour the office towers empty into the street all at once, a river of white shirts and lanyards moving with the specific unified urgency of people who have somewhere to be and forty-five minutes of traffic standing between them and it. Through Chinatown, where the shophouses were lighting their evening signs one at a time like a slow chemical reaction, gold characters over red, a smell of roasting chestnuts and motorbike exhaust braided together into something that has smelled the same, I'd guess, since before my father invented our name. Along the river for a stretch, the Chao Phraya doing what it always does, brown and patient and indifferent to every determinism argument anyone has ever had on its banks, ferries crossing it in both directions at once like a system too busy to notice it was supposed to be proof of anything.

I thought, walking, about my father, who died in 2010 in his sleep, peacefully, which is the word people use for a death nobody was awake to argue with. He built our surname himself, syllables from two languages welded at a joint no genealogist could ever separate again, and I used to think that was an act of pure invention, a man choosing something from nothing. Walking through Chinatown at kilometer nine, I wondered instead whether he'd only ever been assembling something that was always going to exist the moment two grandfathers crossed paths in a district office in 1974 — whether the name felt like his invention only because he was the frame of the reel slow enough to experience the joining as a choice.

Chinatown at that hour runs on an economy so exact it could be mistaken for a proof of its own. A gold shop owner weighing a customer's necklace on a scale calibrated to the tenth of a gram, the price called out in baht per baht-weight, an entire vocabulary this city built specifically so that trust in a transaction never has to depend on trusting the person across the counter. A noodle stall charging fifty baht for a bowl that costs the owner, by my own rough architect's math on rent and ingredient cost, no more than eighteen to produce, and nobody at either end of that gap considering it unfair, because the gap is the stall's whole reason for existing at that corner instead of somewhere the rent runs higher. Every price in that street is a small, local, fully negotiated agreement about what a moment is worth, argued and settled a thousand times an hour, and I found myself wondering whether the entire apparatus of money was really just humanity's oldest attempt to answer the same question the video had spent eight minutes asking me for free: what is a given instant actually worth, and who gets to decide.

I stopped at a shophouse selling paper offerings for the dead — gold ingots printed on foil, miniature paper mansions, paper cars, a paper phone with a screen printed to look like it was mid-call — meant to be burned so the dead can have them in whatever comes next. I asked the woman behind the counter, half in jest, whether they'd started printing anything for AI. She laughed, not unkindly, and said someone had asked her that exact question the week before, an old man buying a paper laptop for his son, and that no, they hadn't started printing anything for AI yet, because nobody had figured out what shape a soul would need it to be.

Fourteen thousand two hundred steps by the time I stopped counting, somewhere past eleven at night, my calves aching in the specific honest way a body aches when you've actually asked something of it instead of letting it run its habitual loop. I hadn't proven anything. I want to be honest about that too. Every unscripted choice I'd made all day — the coin flips, the flower market, the extra three thousand steps — could just as easily have been exactly what the reel always contained, a man convinced he was defying a video by doing, in total, precisely what a man who'd just watched that video would do.

I gave up trying to win the argument somewhere around Charoen Krung, tired in a way that felt, for the first time in weeks, like ordinary human tiredness rather than the specific exhaustion of a man auditing his own reality. I turned toward home.

---

I reached my own soi at 2:47am, which I noticed only because I'd started noticing that number everywhere the way you notice a word right after someone teaches it to you, appearing in every book you open from then on though it was always there, you just hadn't been tuned to it yet.

She was at the corner below my building. A bicycle, old-frame, the kind with a basket welded to the front rather than bolted, the kind nobody in this neighborhood actually rides for transport anymore, everyone having long since switched to scooters or apps. She wasn't moving. Not waiting for a light — there was no light at that corner — just stopped, one foot on the pedal and one on the curb, the specific stillness of a person who has arrived exactly where they meant to and is now simply occupying it.

She looked up.

Not at the building. At my floor. At, I would swear on the notebook and everything written in it, the exact window I was standing behind, twenty-three floors up, in the dark, having just turned my key in the lobby's outer gate and glanced up the way you glance up at your own building out of nothing but habit, the small human reflex of checking that the place you live is still where you left it.

I stopped moving. She kept looking.

I could not make out her face at that distance, at that hour, through humidity that blurs streetlight into something closer to underwater light than air. I could see the shape of her, upright, unhurried, a woman who had ridden somewhere specific and stopped somewhere specific and was now doing something specific with her attention, and every part of me that has spent three weeks assembling receipts and calling brothers and standing outside flower markets understood, with a certainty that had nothing to do with evidence, that this look was not an accident of two strangers glancing up at the same instant. This was aimed.

I did not move. I counted, the way I count everything, an old habit that has never once failed to steady me and did not fail me now — one, two, three, all the way up, my own pulse doing most of the counting for me by the end, faster than the seconds themselves — and I reached forty-one before she looked away, put her other foot back on the pedal, and rode south without a single backward glance, the bicycle's chain ticking faintly in a rhythm that took longer to fade than she did.

Forty-one seconds. I stood at that window another twenty minutes after she'd gone, replaying it, trying to fix some detail of her onto memory the way you try to fix a dream in the ninety seconds before it dissolves — the set of her shoulders, upright, unhurried; the basket on the front of the bicycle, empty, as far as I could tell, of anything she'd been carrying; the specific unhurried economy of her final motion, foot back on the pedal, no glance behind her, a woman closing a transaction she'd already decided the terms of before she ever arrived at that corner. I could not have told a police officer her height. I could have told him, with total confidence, that she had looked at exactly the right window on the first try, in a building with thirty floors and no name lit up outside it, in a city of eleven million people going about their own thoroughly ordinary nights.

I did not write it in the notebook that night because some entries, I am starting to understand, need to sit in you unrecorded for a while before you trust your own hand enough to make them permanent. Whatever the reel already contains, I thought, standing there in the dark with my calves still aching from fourteen thousand two hundred steps I couldn't prove were mine to choose, it had just spent forty-one full seconds making absolutely certain I knew I was being watched by someone who had chosen, deliberately, to let herself be seen watching.

I did not sleep. For once, lying there, that didn't feel like the failure it usually does. It felt like the correct response to the only fact that had actually changed all day: not that time might be an illusion, not that free will might be a story the projector's light tells itself. Just that someone, somewhere in this city or very far outside it, knew exactly which window was mine, and had chosen forty-one seconds as the length of time she was willing to let me know it.
