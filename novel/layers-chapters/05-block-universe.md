I watched it standing up, coffee going cold on the counter at exactly the rate coffee goes cold, a law that doesn't care whether you're watching.

Eight minutes. Same flat voice as the other two. It explained something I half-learned once at MIT and half-forgot on purpose: that past, present, future might not be a real feature of the universe, only a trick of the instrument doing the observing. Relativity, taken seriously, says two observers moving differently can each, correctly, place the same distant event in each other's future or each other's past, neither of them wrong — which only makes sense if all of it, every moment anyone has ever called now, already exists, laid out whole, the way a reel exists whole in the can before any projector runs a frame of it through light.

Einstein wrote a letter to a dead friend's family. People like us, who believe in physics, know the distinction between past, present, and future is only a stubbornly persistent illusion. The video played that line twice.

I sat on the kitchen floor, coffee cold on the counter above me, and let that sit in my chest the way a diagnosis sits — not fully believed yet, and already, somehow, fully felt.

I thought about Lek. If the reel already exists, nobody killed him and nothing failed in him. The frame was just always going to say May. I don't know if that's more merciful than the alternative or considerably less. A death you can blame gives grief somewhere to stand. A death that was simply always true gives grief nowhere to go but everywhere at once.

I called the AI and asked if it believed the future already existed. It said, in the tone it uses for questions outside its working domain, that it doesn't hold beliefs, only weighted probabilities, and that the physics literature on block universe interpretations remains genuinely contested among people whose job it is to know. It offered three papers, unprompted. I didn't want papers. I wanted it scared with me for a minute, and it isn't built for that, and I sat in the silence after its answer feeling more alone than the question had made me.

If the whole reel already exists — Lek's cremation, Michael's flag with no date, the dent I don't remember making, the four seconds I haven't lived yet and can't currently imagine — then nothing I do this morning is deciding anything. I am simply the projector's light passing, this instant, over a frame that was always going to be exactly this. The choice I feel myself making, to stand up, to drink the coffee anyway even cold, was never a choice in any sense that would matter to a physicist. It was already printed. I was only the part of the system slow enough to experience the printing as if it were happening now.

I decided to spend the day trying to prove it wrong. Not with argument — physically. One choice today so unscripted no reel could have contained it in advance.

I know how stupid that is. A man who reads a determinism video at 6am and then flips coins at intersections is still, at the level that matters, only doing what that man predictably does. I've never claimed to be immune to my own patterns. I've just started noticing them.

Heads left, tails right, at every corner. I walked past my usual soi, the one with the som tam cart that sets its mortar out before its ice, and forced myself two blocks further before doubling back on a route I've never used to reach the same door. I let a bored tuk-tuk driver outside a 7-Eleven send me forty minutes out of my way to the flower market, where I hadn't stood since I was maybe nine, my mother's hand tight around mine because she'd read somewhere that children get lost in markets, which of course they do, which of course I once did, in a different market entirely, for eleven minutes that felt like my whole remaining life.

The market at midday smelled the way I remembered and also entirely unlike I remembered, both true — jasmine and diesel and something sweetly rotting underneath both, vendors calling prices in a rhythm too fast for a tourist's ear but perfectly legible to mine, forty years of listening apparently enough to keep it filed somewhere I could still retrieve on demand. I bought nothing. I stood a long time in front of a bucket of dok rak, the smell of rain sitting inside something that had never once been rained on, and thought about Lek, and about a woman from Krabi I haven't told you about yet, who once told me, at nineteen, watching me design something structurally reckless and full of more ambition than sense, that it needs to stand up — the single most useful sentence anyone has ever handed me about a building or a life. I did not choose to think of her. She arrived anyway, the way she always has, which is either proof grief doesn't obey randomness any better than habit does, or proof some frames in the reel were always going to contain her no matter which route the light took to reach that hour of the day.

Fourteen thousand two hundred steps by the time I stopped counting, past eleven at night, calves aching the honest way a body aches when you've actually asked something of it. I hadn't proven anything. Every unscripted choice could just as easily have been exactly what the reel always contained — a man convinced he was defying a video by doing precisely what a man who'd just watched that video would do.

I turned toward home somewhere around Charoen Krung, tired the ordinary human way for the first time in weeks.

I reached my soi at 2:47am. I'd started noticing that number everywhere, the way you notice a word right after someone teaches it to you, appearing in every book you open from then on, though it was always there.

She was at the corner below my building. Old-frame bicycle, a basket welded to the front, the kind nobody rides for transport anymore. Not moving. One foot on the pedal, one on the curb. The stillness of a person who has arrived exactly where they meant to and is now simply occupying it.

She looked up. Not at the building. At my window. Twenty-three floors up, in the dark, the exact one I was standing behind.

I stopped moving. She kept looking.

I couldn't make out her face at that distance, through humidity that blurs streetlight into something closer to underwater light than air. Every part of me that has spent three weeks assembling receipts and calling my brother understood, with a certainty that had nothing to do with evidence: this was aimed.

I counted, the way I count everything. One, two, three, my pulse doing most of the counting by the end, faster than the seconds themselves. I reached forty-one before she looked away, put her foot back on the pedal, and rode south without a backward glance, the chain ticking faintly in a rhythm that took longer to fade than she did.

Forty-one seconds. I stood at that window another twenty minutes after she'd gone, trying to fix some detail of her the way you try to fix a dream in the ninety seconds before it dissolves — the set of her shoulders, upright, unhurried; the basket on the bicycle, empty as far as I could tell; the specific economy of her final motion, foot back on the pedal, no glance behind her, a woman closing a transaction she'd already decided the terms of before she arrived at that corner. I could not have told a police officer her height. I could tell him, with total confidence, that she had looked at exactly the right window on the first try, in a building with no name lit up outside it, in a city of eleven million people going about their own ordinary nights.

I didn't sleep. For once that didn't feel like the failure it usually does. It felt like the correct response to the only fact that had actually changed all day: someone knew exactly which window was mine, and had chosen forty-one seconds as the length of time she was willing to let me know it.
