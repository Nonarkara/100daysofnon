# CH1 — The Milli-Second

## 2026 · Doc

I design bridges by talking to a machine. I say a sentence, it hands me three ways to build it, I pick one, I sign my name to load calculations I never ran myself. Nine years since Oxford. Three since I stopped drawing lines by hand. I sleep four hours a night in a city of eleven million, and most of what I feel, I feel in twenty minutes with strangers I pay to be honest with me. Not for the sex. For the second after it. Something leaks through. I call it the glimpse. I've never said that word out loud to anyone.

Her name was Gift, which a third of the women in this city use, the way a third of American men are named Mike. Twenty-six. A photographer's assistant, three photos on the app, one of her actual face and two of her hands doing something with light I couldn't identify and didn't need to. She set her own terms in eleven messages — the room, the hour, the price split down the middle, no negotiation on that, I offered and she said no, we split it, this isn't that. I liked her for the sentence before I liked anything else about her. People who tell you what a thing isn't before you have to guess wrong are rare enough to notice.

Ratchada, a short-time hotel with a gate that swallows your car so nobody driving past sees whose it is. Six hundred eighty baht, three hours, a television the size of a dining table playing a drama with the sound off, a jacuzzi tub neither of us used.

She undressed in one motion, like taking off a decision. She told me what she wanted before I asked — that's the part nobody tells you about the apps, the good ones flip the whole structure. She isn't there to be wanted. She's there wanting something, and you're the instrument that happened to show up on a Tuesday. She held my jaw with one hand, not hard, the way you hold a glass you don't want to spill, and she watched my face the whole time instead of closing her eyes, which almost nobody does. She was somewhere private with her eyes open. I found that more erotic than anything I could have started myself.

Her breath changed first. Then her hands. Then a sound that wasn't a word. I felt her weight shift the way a beam shifts when the load finally reaches the member built to carry it — not collapse, transfer. And in the second after, the one the word millisecond undersells —

I saw a ceiling.

Cream. No source for the light on it, no shadow, the way a shadow needs a direction and this one had none. A chair in the corner of it, gray, wide-armed, the kind you'd sit in for a long procedure, not a short one. Gone before I could turn my head, if turning my head meant anything in whatever position I was actually in.

Third time. Always after. Always brief. Always the ceiling, sometimes the chair.

I asked Gift, after, four inches of hot air between us and the fan doing nothing, if she ever felt like she was somewhere else in that second. She said no. She said she felt more exactly where she was than anywhere else all week, that this was the whole point of a Tuesday instead of something with a name — a name drags you into every other room of your life, this only asks for the one.

I didn't tell her about the ceiling. There's no way to say it that isn't a symptom or a bad line. She got dressed the same way she'd undressed, reversed, one motion, no performance in either direction. She counted out her half, three hundred forty baht, onto the nightstand next to the key without being asked. I offered to walk her to the sky train. She said no thank you, she liked walking alone after, it was part of how she came back to herself, and I understood that completely and felt closer to her in that sentence than in the ninety minutes before it. Anticipation is a kind of architecture too. You build the room the feeling happens in before the feeling arrives, and she'd built hers correctly, for herself, without needing me for it.

A dog eating its dinner doesn't audit the meal. Something in me has civilized itself so far past that dog that I need my body to overwhelm my mind completely, for a second, just so the mind stops asking whether any of it counts — and even then, at the one moment a body is supposed to be unanswerable, mine hands me a ceiling instead of an answer. I don't know if that makes me the most honest animal in the room or the most broken one. I'm not going to pretend I've worked that out.

Driving home, red light on Rama IV, a gray sedan pulled up beside me. Ordinary car. The man driving it looked at me the full two seconds the light allows, the way you look at something you've already decided about and are only confirming. Then it changed and he went right and I went straight and nothing happened. I want to be honest that nothing happened.

I remember his face better than I remember Gift's.

In the garage under my building there was a dent in the passenger door, fist-sized, paint intact, like the car had been leaned on hard by something that didn't want to break the surface, only bend what was under it. I don't remember making it. The dashcam loops every ninety-six hours and showed nothing, which isn't the same as proving nothing happened inside that window. I stood in the garage a long time looking at it, the fluorescent light doing its flat thing, a security camera blinking its small red confirmation in the corner that it was, in fact, recording something, though I've never once seen anyone review that footage or known who could.

Upstairs, showered, one meal — grilled fish, rice — I sat on the balcony with a nicotine pouch, 28 milligrams, tucked against my gum, strong enough that most people spit it out inside ninety seconds. I don't. I've built a tolerance to it the way I've built a tolerance to the two hours after midnight generally, the hour the thoughts stop taking turns and start arriving all at once. My phone lit at 11:52pm. No name. A dash where a photo should be.

> ——  02:47
> stop testing the edges with your body

I read it four times. I checked the number — unregistered, no country code I could resolve. I typed back a question mark and got nothing. I did not sleep well. I did not expect to.

I got up once, at 1:40, and stood at the sliding door to the balcony without opening it. Twenty-three floors down, Bangkok did what it does at that hour, which is pretend to sleep while half of it works. A 7-Eleven sign burned green on the corner. A motorcycle taxi driver sat on his bike doing nothing, which is its own kind of work, the work of being available. I have never once, in forty-five years, put something down and failed to remember where. I lost myself for eleven minutes once, at six, in a mall that doesn't exist anymore. That's the whole inventory. And now there was a dent I didn't make, in a door I lock every night, and a sentence on my phone from a number that resolves to nowhere, telling me to stop testing the edges of something with a body I had, until that message, considered entirely mine to spend.
