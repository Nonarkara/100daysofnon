# CH7 — The Drive

## 2026 · Doc

Fourteen capsules, same order, same six seconds of water run first. I've done it in the dark before. I did it in the dark that morning too, except I wasn't the only one who could describe it.

The text came at 6:14, while I stood on the BTS holding a pole I didn't need.

> ——  02:47
> omega-3. d3. magnesium glycinate. the b-complex and nac together, same brown bottle. zinc. the two you take every other day and forget which day. the last one — you don't let that one sit on your tongue. you learned that before you learned to read a label.

Nobody photographs a tongue. That's what broke loose in my chest on that train — not the order, the order's easy, a camera in my kitchen gets the order in a week. The tongue thing lives inside my own mouth, private the way a scar under a shirt is private, and I have said it out loud exactly once in my life, that same morning, half a sentence, to a man named Toon, about something else entirely.

Fear came first, ninety seconds of it, pure animal, checking exits. Then something else replaced it, and I still don't have the right word for it. Being known the way you're known by someone who fed you before you could read, who isn't trying to use what she knows, only proving she's still counting.

I thought about Malee for the first time in months, the woman who cooked for us at Nam Sai and lined vitamins on a saucer before I could read the bottles, standing over me until I'd swallowed each one. She's the only other person who ever knew about the tongue. She's been dead six years. Someone, or something, had apparently been paying the kind of attention to me that usually only comes from a person who raised you, and she hadn't raised me in a long time.

---

Pom had given it to me three days before, wrapped in a sandwich bag like leftovers, in a coffee shop neither of us liked — neutral ground has to cost you something or it isn't neutral. *He told me if anything happened, give it to someone who'd know what to do with it,* she'd said. *I've gone through everyone I know. You're not even who I'd have picked first. You're who's left.* I told her that was, in its way, the kindest insult I'd received all year. I hadn't opened the bag since.

Toon's shop was three floors up, no elevator, above a place that sold phone cases and nothing else. He took Lek's drive out of its sandwich bag with two fingers, like it might still be warm.

"It's not corrupted," he said. "Every byte's there. I just can't read the shape it's in." He turned the monitor. Hex code, a block that repeated forty-one times, evenly spaced, laid one copy over another until it stopped looking like noise and started having edges. "Encryption's supposed to look like nothing. This looks like something trying hard not to look like something. And there's a rhythm in it. Somebody's rhythm. You get a feel for whose habits are in a file, same as you'd know your mother's handwriting in a language you don't speak."

I said "p-patterns that shouldn't—" and the stutter caught the way it does twice a year, always at the edge of something I'm not ready to name.

"Patterns that shouldn't have patterns," Toon finished for me, gentle about it. "Yeah. Give me a week."

He looked more tired than the last time, the specific tiredness of a man who spends his days holding other people's dead relatives in his hands and has stopped being able to tell his own exhaustion from theirs. I didn't ask if he was alright. Some questions a shop like that won't survive being asked out loud.

---

I walked instead of taking the train home, past the court behind the old municipal school, where a kid was working free throws alone under a light that buzzed every five seconds, missing his ninth in a row and resetting his feet to the same square each time. I used to be that kid, worse knees, different court. My coach made me shoot four hundred from one spot before I was allowed to move an inch. Own one square completely, he said, and the whole court reads you instead.

I own one square too. Fourteen capsules, four shirts, one meal, one route home. Someone had been standing outside that square for years, apparently, closely enough to tell me things only two people alive were supposed to know, and one of them was already dead.

The dental building was two blocks past the court. I told myself I was cutting through. I wasn't.

Same lobby as always — marble the color of weak tea, the six fat koi, the guard's fan turning left, then right, then left. No appointment, no reason to stop. I stopped anyway, just inside the doors, and the cold hit the back of my neck first, no draft attached to it, and for something between two and six seconds the floor under my shoes was a different floor. Chairs at the edge of my eye that turned back into the fish tank the second I looked straight at them. A smell that wasn't there, checked twice, genuinely wasn't. My own hand, empty, in a lobby I had no business standing in.

The guard asked if I was alright. Said I'd been standing there four minutes, maybe five. I said I was waiting for someone. It came out true before I'd decided to make it true.

At the counter, the appointment book — still paper, the only clinic on that street that hadn't gone digital — had a name written into tomorrow's four o'clock slot, small careful letters, and I read it twice and could not tell you afterward what it said. I have never had a four o'clock in that building in my life.

I texted Fah from the sidewalk outside, my hands not quite steady yet: *just froze for four minutes in a building I had no reason to be in.* She wrote back inside a minute: *froze how.* I told her about the cold at the back of the neck, the chairs at the edge of my eye. She didn't ask if I was alright, which I noticed and was grateful for, having had enough of that question this week to last the year. She wrote instead: *sounds like you finally saw something instead of just felt it. that's not worse. write it down.* I did.

Walking home I understood something that had been sitting one inch out of reach since the eggs. Whatever this is, it isn't hunting me. A hunter wants you off guard. This wants me steady — feeds me my own vitamins so I'll trust it, freezes me in a lobby for four minutes and lets me walk out unharmed, texts me at an hour that means nothing on my clock and everything on someone else's. My father used to say the difference between an inspector and a spy isn't the looking, it's whether the file gets used against you or used to keep the roof up while you sleep under it. I didn't know which file I was in. Two blocks from home, phone dead in my pocket, I started to suspect it was the second one — that someone was managing me, not stalking me, the way you manage a bridge you don't want to fall down. Which should have been less frightening than the alternative, and mostly was, except for the part where nobody manages what they don't also, eventually, get to decide the fate of.
