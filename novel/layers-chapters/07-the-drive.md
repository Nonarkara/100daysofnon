# CH7 — The Drive

## 2026 · Doc

Omega-3 first. Then vitamin D3, then magnesium glycinate, then the B-complex and the NAC together because they come from the same brown bottle and my hand doesn't distinguish them anymore, then the zinc, then the two I take every other day and always forget which day it is until I check the app, then the last one, the one that tastes like a coin if you let it sit on your tongue too long, which I do not, because I learned that lesson at eleven years old from a woman who is not related to me by blood and who I have not thought about in years until this morning.

Fourteen capsules. Room-temperature water from the tap, run for six seconds before I fill the glass, because the first six seconds are still whatever temperature the pipe was overnight and after that it settles into itself. I have done this in this order for four years. I could do it in the dark, and some mornings I do.

Malee used to line them up on a saucer before I could read the labels — cod liver oil first, back when it was a spoon and not a capsule, then the calcium chews shaped like dinosaurs that I hated and ate anyway because she stood there until I did. She was not my nanny in the way that word means in other countries. She cooked for my family at Nam Sai for eleven years and raised me for most of the hours my parents worked, and she called me "sathapanik noi" — little architect — before I had drawn a single line, because I used to build things out of the saucer and the capsules while she waited for me to actually take them. I have not spoken her name out loud since her funeral, which I did not attend because I was in Shanghai and told myself the flight was the reason.

I put the empty glass in the sink and picked up the drive from where it had sat on the counter for two days, next to a carton of eggs I had started distrusting slightly, and I took a taxi to Bang Rak.

---

The shop was on the third floor of a building with no elevator sign, above a shop that sold phone cases and nothing else, and the man who ran it — Toon, mid-thirties, the specific tiredness of someone who has looked at other people's dead relatives' hard drives for a living — took the drive from its sandwich bag with two fingers like he was handling something that might still be alive.

"Give me the story," he said. Not politely. It was a diagnostic question, the way a doctor asks where it hurts.

I told him what Pom told me. A friend, dead in May, a laptop wiped in a way the family's technician had never seen, and this, given to a colleague for safekeeping three weeks before he died, with instructions and no explanation.

Toon didn't write anything down. He plugged the drive into a machine that was not connected to the internet — I noticed that specifically, and he noticed me noticing — and watched three monitors fill with the kind of scrolling text that means something to exactly one person in the room.

"It's not corrupted," he said, twenty minutes in. "I want to say that first because it's the thing everyone hopes and it's true, and it's also not going to help you the way you think it will."

"What is it, then."

"It's readable at the sector level. Every byte's there. It's the filesystem structure I can't map. It's not FAT, not ext4, not APFS, not anything I've got a driver for, and I've got drivers for everything back to systems your friend's grandfather would've used." He rubbed his eyes with the heel of one hand. "It's like finding a book where every letter's legible and none of the words are in any language on record."

I asked him what that could mean, practically.

"Practically, somebody wrote a filesystem from scratch, which people do, sometimes, for weird reasons — hobbyists, obsessives, guys building the thing they think will finally be uncrackable. Except." He tapped the screen. "Except there's structure in here that shouldn't be structure. See this block? It repeats. Not the data — the pattern of the data. Same shape, different content, forty-one times across the drive, evenly spaced, like — " he searched for it — "like a heartbeat printed on paper instead of a strip. Random noise doesn't do that. Encryption doesn't do that either, not this cleanly. Encryption's supposed to look like nothing. This looks like something trying very hard not to look like something, and getting it eighty percent right."

I asked him how long he needed.

"I don't know if time's the variable," he said, which is the kind of sentence that sounds evasive until you realize the man saying it is being more honest than anyone else has been with you in weeks. "Give me a week. I'll tell you what I find, and I'll tell you honestly if I find nothing, which is a real possibility you should sit with now instead of later."

He turned the monitor toward me and showed me a block of hexadecimal that meant nothing to my eye and everything, apparently, to his — a repeating shape, forty-one instances, that he'd isolated and laid one on top of the other like transparencies, and where they should have blurred into noise they had instead resolved into something with edges. "I taught myself to read filesystems the way some guys teach themselves to read faces," he said. "You get a feel for what's a person's habit and what's the machine's. This has a person's habit in it. Somebody built this with a rhythm, the way you'd recognize your own mother's handwriting even if she wrote a language you didn't speak."

That word — rhythm — put me back fifteen years in a seminar room at Harvard with a professor I only ever refer to as "B.," who taught me and eleven other students that a city, properly read, is a pattern language before it is a collection of buildings — that the honest way to understand Tokyo, or Bangkok, or any city dense enough to have secrets, was to stop asking what a street was for and start asking what rhythm it repeated, because rhythm survives redevelopment the way habit survives a diagnosis. I left that seminar, and academia generally, three years later, not because I stopped believing it but because I got tired of teaching people to read patterns I could not stop reading myself, at three in the morning, in things that were never meant to hold them. I told Toon none of this. I only said, "Whose rhythm," and he said he didn't know yet, and that not knowing yet was the entire remaining content of the week ahead of him.

I said "p-patterns that shouldn't—" and stopped, because my voice had caught on the first consonant the way it used to when I was a kid getting excited past the point my mouth could keep up with, a stutter I thought I'd outgrown around fifteen and that comes back exactly twice a year, always at the edge of something I am not ready to name. Toon didn't react to it. He just nodded, like a man who has watched a lot of people arrive at the edge of a thing in his shop and say the sentence badly the first time.

"Patterns that shouldn't have patterns," he finished for me, gently, the way you finish a sentence for someone who's earned the help. "Yeah. That's the one that's been keeping me up."

---

The text came at 6:14 that evening, while I was on the BTS with my hand around a pole I didn't need to hold onto, the train mostly empty going the wrong direction against rush hour.

No video this time. No file. Just words, from the same numberless thread as three nights before.

> ——  02:47
> omega-3. d3. magnesium glycinate. b-complex and nac from the brown bottle, always together. zinc. the two you take every other day and forget which day. the last one, the one you don't let sit on your tongue. you learned that from someone before you learned to read the label.

The clock at the top of my screen said 18:14. The message, as always, said 02:47, an hour that has not once corresponded to when I actually received anything, and I have stopped expecting it to.

I read it four times standing on a moving train, and each time the floor of my stomach dropped a little further and did not hit anything, the way a lift feels in the half-second before the cable catches.

There is a version of this feeling that is only fear, and I want to be honest that I felt that version first, entirely, for about ninety seconds — the animal register, the one that checks exits, that wonders who has been inside the apartment, that runs immediately to the worst literal explanation, which is that someone has watched me swallow capsules through a window or a camera I haven't found. I ran that branch. It didn't hold. No camera explains "the one you don't let sit on your tongue," because that isn't visible information. That's a rule I follow inside my own mouth, learned from a woman forty years ago, that I have never once said aloud to another living person until this morning, to Toon's ceiling, more or less by accident, mid-sentence, about something else entirely.

What replaced the fear, on the train, holding a pole I didn't need, was something I didn't have language for yet and still mostly don't. It felt like being known the way Malee knew when I'd swallowed and when I was only pretending. Not an intrusion. An attention that had been paying that specific, patient, itemizing kind of attention for long enough to know the tongue thing, which nobody bothers to learn about a stranger. You learn a detail like that about someone you are caring for, over years, the way you learn which side of the bed someone sleeps on. Hostile surveillance wants your passwords, your patterns of vulnerability, your leverage. It does not, as far as I can tell, want to know which vitamin you refuse to let dissolve on your tongue.

I did not answer the text. I did not know yet what an answer would even be shaped like. I put the phone in my pocket and got off two stops early and walked the rest of the way, because my legs wanted the ground more than the train wanted my company.

Somewhere around Thonglor I found myself doing the thing B. taught the whole seminar to do without ever naming it as a technique: I started reading my own last nine days the way I would have read a client's site survey, looking for the load-bearing repetitions instead of the decoration. The video, timed to an insomnia I did not announce anywhere. The vitamins, timed to nothing except a Tuesday. The lobby, four minutes, unannounced, unscheduled. None of it converged on threat. All of it converged on attention, sustained past the point where attention is still merely professional. My father, a civil servant for thirty-one years before he died in 2010, used to say that the difference between an inspector and a spy is not the looking — it's whether the file gets used against you or used to keep the roof from falling on you while you sleep under it. I did not know yet which file I was in. I had started, walking, to suspect it was the second kind.

---

The route took me past the outdoor court behind the old municipal school on Sukhumvit 39, three concrete half-courts with warped nets, empty at that hour except for one kid working free throws alone under a light that buzzed on a five-second delay every time it flickered.

I used to be that kid, on a different court, a lifetime with worse knees ago. Shooting guard, the position that isn't about the ball moving so much as about you standing in exactly one place until the place stops being a place and becomes an extension of your arm. My coach — I don't remember his name, I remember his whistle — used to make me stand on the same square of concrete, marked with chalk that wore off every practice and got redrawn every practice, and shoot from that one spot four hundred times before he'd let me move. Not a stepback. Not a drive. One spot, four hundred makes or the whole session started over from zero. He said a point guard has to read the whole court and a shooting guard has to own one piece of it so completely that the court reads him instead. I hated it at fourteen. I understood it, watching this stranger's kid miss his ninth in a row and reset his feet to the exact same place every time, at thirty-nine, on a sidewalk, holding a phone that had just told me things only two people alive were supposed to know, and one of them was dead.

I own one square of concrete in my life. It is fourteen capsules in a fixed order, four shirts, one meal, eleven thousand steps. I built it the way that kid was building his shot, one make at a time, because I understood early that if you own one small true thing completely, the rest of the court can do whatever it wants and you still have somewhere to stand.

Someone else, apparently, had been watching me stand there for years, and had just told me so, gently, with vitamins instead of a name.

---

The dental building was two blocks past the court and I had not meant to walk there. I told myself, crossing Soi 39, that I was only cutting through to the main road, and I believed it for about four more steps.

The lobby was the same as it had been the last time I set foot in it, before the drill and the four minutes and the room with no smell — marble the color of weak tea, a fish tank along the east wall stocked with the same six overweight koi the building has kept since before I moved to this neighborhood, the security guard's little desk with its logbook and its small fan turning left, then right, then left. I had come for nothing. I had no appointment. I stood inside the doors, out of the heat, and I meant to keep walking through to the street on the other side.

I did not keep walking.

It came without warning and without the chemistry that usually earns it — no weed, no sleeplessness, nothing pushed to any edge, just a man standing still in an air-conditioned lobby at 6:50 in the evening, and then the cold hit the back of my neck first, the specific cold that has no draft attached to it, and for something between two and six seconds — I could not tell you which, my internal clock fails exactly at the moments I need it most — the marble under my feet was a different floor. Not the room fully. A residue of it. The suggestion of chairs at the edge of my vision that resolved, when I turned my head, back into the fish tank and the desk and the fan. A smell that wasn't there and then, checked twice, genuinely wasn't there. My own hand, when I looked down at it, holding nothing, in a lobby I had no appointment in, on a night I had told myself I was only passing through.

The guard asked if I was alright. I said yes. He said I'd been standing in the same spot for a while — four minutes, he thought, maybe five, he hadn't been watching closely — and I told him I was waiting for someone, which was true in a way I didn't examine until I was three blocks further on.

I stood at the reception counter for a moment before leaving, long enough to notice the appointment book was still paper, still handwritten, a relic the newer clinics upstairs had all abandoned for tablets. A woman's name was written in the four-o'clock slot for the following afternoon, in handwriting too small and too careful for the receptionist's looping hand, and I read it twice without being able to say afterward what it said, the letters sliding off my memory the way names do when you have no story to hang them on yet. I have never had a four o'clock appointment in this building in my life. I did not ask about it. There are questions a lobby will not survive being asked in, and I have gotten better, this year, at recognizing which ones those are before I ask them out loud.

Outside, the koi tank's filter hummed the same three-note cycle it always hums, and the heat came back onto my skin the way Bangkok heat does after air conditioning, like a hand replacing itself on your shoulder, and I walked home in the dark with my phone unanswered in my pocket and my legs doing what my legs do now, which is carry me toward whatever is watching instead of away from it.
