# CH2 — The First Video

## 2026 · Doc

The phone rang at 3:14am. +43. Vienna. Michael's number.

Michael has been dead since the third of May.

I let it ring out. Six rings, then the specific quiet a call makes when it ends without a voicemail attached. I lay there and counted to sixty. It rang again. Same number. I let that one go too.

I didn't call back. Calling back a dead man's number at 3:14 in the morning is a decision you can't take back, and I've gotten careful about decisions like that.

His last text, April 29th, sits unanswered because I only sent a thumbs up, the coward's punctuation: *Good dinner. The ambassador said something I didn't expect. Tell you properly when we talk.* We never talked. Four days later he was dead, and whatever the ambassador said folded itself into whatever gets folded away when a person stops being available to unfold it. I have read that text more times than I've read anything Lek ever wrote me, and Lek's handwriting I could recite from memory.

Michael made this city feel like a scene instead of a place to survive. Knew where to eat before the guidebooks did. Had an opinion on every new building and was usually right about which ones would age badly. Bangkok without him in it is a photo with the color balance shifted three degrees toward gray. Nothing you could point to. Everything a little less itself.

I got up, drank water in the dark because turning on a light felt like admitting something, and went back to bed. Didn't sleep.

At 6am I did the vitamins anyway and told the AI to hold the bridge documents till Thursday. It said understood. It has never once asked me why.

I opened LinkedIn at 6:40. His profile came up — same headline, Senior Trade Advisor, Austrian Trade Commission, though a receptionist had told me six weeks back, no hesitation in her voice, that he was "former." Same photo, some gala, 2021, tuxedo, a glass of something amber. Except the photo was soft now. Not pixelated — soft, the way an image goes when you zoom too far in, except I hadn't zoomed at all. I compared it to a screenshot from three weeks earlier. The old one was sharp, every wrinkle at his eye, the grain of the fabric. This one had lost maybe thirty percent of its information. Nobody re-faxes a static image sitting on a server. Photographs don't degrade themselves out of boredom.

I called the number again. Six rings, cut clean, no voicemail, like a landline that ran out of somewhere to send the sound.

I opened my own camera roll and scrolled back to his fifty-fourth birthday, February, a private room at a steakhouse on Convent Road, fourteen people, a bottle of something Australian that cost more than my rent that month. I counted the faces in the group photo twice. Fourteen people, fourteen faces, Michael dead center exactly where the photographer told him to stand, because he was, as always, the reason everyone else had shown up. Nothing wrong with it. I kept looking anyway, the way you press a tooth that doesn't hurt yet, just to confirm it still can.

I went to the office at ten, a generous word for the chair I keep in a coworking space on Thonglor. The AI had three revisions of a pedestrian bridge waiting, unrequested, plus a note: *I noticed the load path assumption from Tuesday conflicted with the soil report filed in March. Corrected. Confirm.* I confirmed. I thought, not for the first time, that I've built a working relationship with something that catches my mistakes before I've finished making them, and that this should feel like collaboration and mostly feels like being quietly, competently outgrown.

Dao texted at noon. We'd been at Michael's fortieth together, some rooftop with a saxophonist nobody had asked for. I asked if she'd noticed anything strange on his Facebook. She said she hadn't seen his posts in a while. I asked when she unfollowed him. She said she didn't remember unfollowing him at all. She said she wasn't sure she had.

By two I'd walked eleven thousand steps without deciding to, which happens some days, the body doing its assigned work while the mind does something else entirely. I ended up on Sukhumvit 22, outside a dispensary I hadn't planned to visit. Add runs the place, forearms like a man who used to lift for a living. I've been coming since 2022, back when the law said yes, and kept coming after it re-grayed into something nobody fully understands, including the police who enforce it selectively.

"Something for the two hours after midnight," I said, our shorthand. He reached for a jar without checking the label. He weighed it on a scale older than the shop and said, without looking up, "You look like a man checking a corpse's pulse to see if it changed its mind."

I asked what that meant.

"Nothing. My brother went to Chiang Mai for a retreat six years ago and never really came all the way back. Not dead. Thinned out. Still answers the phone. Doesn't sound like him."

I didn't tell him about Michael. I watched the scale settle instead, the little digital numbers ticking down toward the exact gram, and thought about 2022, when this whole street legalized overnight and every shophouse for three blocks sprouted a green cross like a fever breaking, and how within eighteen months the government re-grayed the law into something nobody, including the police who enforce it selectively, can fully explain without contradicting the sentence before it. A thing can be legal, illegal, and simply tolerated, all three at once, in the same country, in the same week. I paid twelve hundred baht, cash, and walked out with contraband an entire industry pretends not to notice.

The video arrived at 9:41pm. I was on the balcony, the city doing its evening thing below.

> ——  02:47
> [video attached]
> you already know what this is about

The timestamp said 02:47. My phone's clock said 9:41. I sat with that for a full minute before opening it.

Four minutes eleven seconds. A voice, flat, unhurried, the cadence people use for things they consider obviously true. It described a universe with no author behind it, no ledger, no floor beneath the floor. It didn't argue. It described, the way weather gets described — a condition, not a position.

I watched it twice. The second time I caught something under the narration, three seconds in — a sound like a chair adjusting under weight. Then nothing. Then the voice continuing like the interruption never happened.

I called Neung. He picked up on the fourth ring, television loud behind him, one of the Chinese dramas our mother used to watch dubbed badly into Thai. I didn't tell him about the video. I asked if he remembered the Pollock wall — the one I painted with fish sauce at two years old, the one Mae framed instead of scrubbing off, told people was a Jackson Pollock though she'd never seen one, had only heard the name once at a dinner party and filed it under things you say about a mess you've decided to love. He laughed, the particular laugh he saves for when he thinks I've lost the plot in some harmless way. "You think that wall was the only honest thing in that whole house," he said, and then the drama was at the good part and we hung up, the way brothers do when somebody else's drama is a perfectly reasonable excuse to stop before either of you says something you'd have to keep meaning afterward.

I lay in the dark running the video back instead of the screen — the flat voice, the chair adjusting under someone's weight — and thought: if nothing means anything, somebody went to a great deal of trouble, at a very specific hour, to make sure I heard that. Someone was looking. I hadn't worked out from where.
