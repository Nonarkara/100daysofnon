# CH2 — The First Video

## 2026 · Doc

The phone buzzed against the nightstand at 3:14am, and I knew the number before I looked at it, the way you know a knock is your mother's before you open the door. +43. Vienna. Michael's number.

Michael has been dead since the third of May.

I let it ring out. Six rings, then silence, then the specific quiet a phone makes after a call ends without a voicemail attached, which is a different quiet than the one before the call, though I couldn't tell you the mechanism by which a room can hold two different silences forty seconds apart. I lay there with the ceiling doing nothing interesting above me and counted to sixty, twice, and then it rang again. Same number. I let that one ring out too.

I did not call back. I want that on the record, whatever record this is. I did not call back, because calling back a dead man's number at 3:14 in the morning is the kind of decision you can't take back once you've made it, and I have gotten very careful, lately, about decisions like that.

His last text to me, April the 29th, still sits unanswered in the thread because I never got a chance to answer it properly, only with a thumbs up, the coward's punctuation: *Good dinner tonight. The ambassador said something I didn't expect. Tell you properly when we can talk.* We never talked properly. Four days later he was dead, and whatever the ambassador said folded itself into whatever gets folded away when a person stops being available to unfold it. I have read that text more times than I've read anything Lek ever wrote me, and Lek's handwriting I could recite from memory.

Michael was the kind of man who made a city feel like a scene rather than a place to survive. He knew where to eat before the guidebooks did, knew which gallery openings mattered and which were just wine, had an opinion about every new building in town and was usually right about the ones that would age badly. Bangkok without him in it feels like a photograph with the color balance shifted three degrees toward gray — nothing missing you could point to, everything slightly less itself.

I got up anyway. Drank a glass of water standing at the kitchen counter in the dark because turning on a light felt like admitting something. Fourteen vitamins waited on the saucer for six more hours, patient the way inanimate objects are patient, which is to say not patient at all, just incapable of doing anything else. I went back to bed. I did not sleep.

At 6am I did the vitamins anyway, in order, and weighed the coffee, and told the AI to hold the pedestrian bridge documents until Thursday because I wasn't ready to sign off on something with my name attached to it that day. It said understood. It has never once asked me why.

I opened LinkedIn at 6:40, coffee in hand, standing at the window watching the city do its slow gray wake-up, and searched Michael's name the way I'd searched it eleven times since the funeral I didn't attend because it was in Vienna and I was in Bangkok and grief, apparently, has a budget. His profile came up. Same headline — Senior Trade Advisor, Austrian Trade Commission, though I'd been told six weeks ago by a receptionist with a voice like a closing door that he was "former," said without a flicker of hesitation, as though the word had been sitting ready in her mouth since before I called. Same profile photo, the one from some gala in 2021, tuxedo, a glass of something amber, a grin that used to make rooms feel like they'd been waiting for him specifically.

Except the photo was soft now. Not pixelated — soft, the way a photograph looks when you've zoomed too far into it on an old phone, except I hadn't zoomed at all. I took a screenshot. I compared it to a screenshot I'd taken three weeks earlier, saved in a folder I'd started calling, in my head, evidence, before I'd admitted to myself that's what it was.

The earlier one was sharp. Every wrinkle at the corner of his eye, the exact grain of the tuxedo fabric. The new one had lost maybe thirty percent of its information, the way a fax loses information with every re-fax, except nobody had re-faxed anything. Nobody does that to a static image sitting on a server. Photographs don't degrade themselves out of boredom.

I called his number. It rang six times and stopped the way a landline used to stop, abruptly, no voicemail greeting, no operator message, just a cut, as if the line itself had run out of somewhere to send the sound.

I opened my own camera roll and scrolled back to his fifty-fourth birthday, February, a private room at a steakhouse on Convent Road, fourteen people, a bottle of something Australian that cost more than my rent that month. I counted the faces in the group photo twice. Fourteen people, fourteen faces, Michael dead center exactly where the photographer had told him to stand because he was, as always, the reason everyone else had shown up. Nothing wrong with the photograph. I kept looking at it anyway, the way you keep pressing a tooth that doesn't actually hurt yet, just to confirm it still can.

I went to the office at ten, which is a generous word for the room I keep in a coworking space on Thonglor because a man who dictates buildings to a machine doesn't strictly need an office, he needs a chair he doesn't have to explain to his own apartment. The AI had three revisions of the bridge waiting, unrequested, plus a note: *I noticed the load path assumption from Tuesday conflicted with the soil report filed in March. Corrected. Confirm.* I confirmed. I thought, not for the first time, that I have built a working relationship with something that catches my mistakes before I've finished making them, and that this should feel like collaboration and mostly feels like being quietly, competently outgrown.

I taught for two years at Oxford, a seminar with more Chinese exchange students than British ones, on the theory that informal settlements are the truest expression of what a city actually believes about itself, official plans being mostly what a city tells its own government it believes. I liked the students who argued with me. I liked less the ones who wrote down everything I said as though I'd handed them a fact instead of a provocation. I quit two years in — told the department it was a scholarship offer somewhere else, told my mother it was money, told myself, on the flight back to Bangkok, that I was tired of describing cities to people who would go on to manage them from an office forty floors above the part that actually worked. Somewhere in that decision is the same instinct that now has me dictating bridges instead of drawing them: I trust the machine to get the load calculations right more than I trusted a lecture hall to get the point.

The AI has never once, in two years, asked me why I stopped drawing by hand. I've wondered whether that's tact or indifference. I've decided it doesn't matter which.

Dao texted at noon. We'd been at Michael's fortieth birthday together, four years back, some rooftop on Wireless Road with a saxophonist nobody had asked for. I asked her, casually, whether she'd noticed anything strange on his Facebook. She said she didn't really see his posts anymore, hadn't for a while. I asked when she unfollowed him. She said she didn't remember unfollowing him at all. She said, and I could hear her frowning through the text, that she wasn't sure she had.

By two I'd walked eleven thousand two hundred steps without deciding to, which happens some days, the body doing its assigned work while the mind does something else entirely. I ended up on Sukhumvit 22, outside a dispensary with a green cross lit up dim in the daylight and a chalkboard listing strains in handwriting too careful to be anyone but the owner's own hand.

Add runs the place. Forty-something, forearms like a man who used to lift things for a living and now just talks about the people who still do, always in a t-shirt regardless of the strain he's selling you, which is its own kind of branding. I've been coming since 2022, back when it was legal and everyone thought Bangkok had actually changed, and I kept coming after they re-grayed the law into something nobody, including the police who still enforce it selectively, fully understands. Add doesn't ask why I look the way I look on days I look like this. He just asks what I need it to do.

"Something for the two hours after midnight," I said, which is our shorthand by now for the specific job I need it to perform — not euphoria, management. He nodded and reached for a jar without checking the label, which told me he already knew which one, which told me I come here often enough to have a type the way I have a shirt rotation.

The shop smelled the way it always smells, cedar and citrus over something skunk-green underneath, and a fan in the corner turned slow enough that the smoke from someone's sample joint drifted instead of scattered, a visible current in the air like the shape of a river seen from a plane. Two students argued quietly over a menu near the door about whether a hybrid strain could be trusted to help them study or would just help them forget they were supposed to. Add didn't look up at them. He'd heard that argument, in some form, a thousand times, and had long since stopped having an opinion about which side of it a person should land on.

He weighed it on a small brass scale that looked older than the shop, older maybe than the building, and while it settled he said, without looking up: "You look like a man checking a corpse's pulse to see if it changed its mind."

I asked him what that meant.

"Nothing," he said. "Something my brother used to say. He went to Chiang Mai for a retreat six years ago and never really came all the way back. Not dead. Just — thinned out. Still answers the phone. Doesn't sound like him anymore. I used to call every week. Now I call because I promised myself I would, not because I think anyone's really picking up."

I didn't tell him about Michael. I watched the scale settle instead, the little digital numbers ticking down toward the exact gram he'd promised, and thought about 2022, when this whole street legalized overnight and every shophouse for three blocks sprouted a green cross like a fever breaking, and how within eighteen months the government re-grayed the law into something nobody, including the police who still enforce it selectively, can fully explain to you without contradicting the sentence before it. A thing can be legal, illegal, and simply tolerated, all three at once, in the same country, in the same week, depending on who's asking and what they're wearing when they ask it. I have built an entire relationship to reality around learning to live inside contradictions exactly that size. Maybe that's the actual skill this city taught me, more than any building I've designed: how to hold two incompatible facts in one hand without dropping either.

I paid, twelve hundred baht, cash, the way I always do, and walked out with the small resealable bag in my jacket pocket like contraband, though it's the specific kind of contraband an entire industry pretends not to notice.

The video arrived at 9:41pm.

I was on the balcony, the pouch tucked against my gum, the city doing its evening thing below — motorcycles threading gaps that shouldn't exist, a temple bell somewhere too far to place exactly, the smell of someone's dinner rising four floors from a food stall I could see but not reach. My phone buzzed once, short, the way it does for a message rather than a call.

> ——  02:47
> [video attached]
> you already know what this is about

The timestamp said 02:47 even though my own phone's clock, the actual time, read 9:41. I sat with that discrepancy for a full minute before I did anything else. Then I opened it.

I'm not going to describe every frame. I watched it the way you watch a doctor's face while she reads a scan she hasn't yet said anything about — not looking for content exactly, looking for a verdict I could feel arriving before it was spoken. It ran four minutes and eleven seconds. It talked, in the flat unhurried cadence people use for things they consider obviously true, about the shape of a universe with no author behind it, no ledger keeping score, no floor beneath the floor. It didn't argue. It just described, the way weather is described, as a condition rather than a position.

I watched it twice.

The second time I noticed something I'd missed the first — three seconds in, a background noise under the narration, so faint I almost convinced myself I'd invented it: a sound like a chair adjusting under weight, and then nothing, and then the narration continuing as if the interruption had never happened.

I sat on the balcony a long time after that, the second-generation glow of the video paused on my screen, the pouch gone stale and flat against my gum. I thought about Michael's photograph losing thirty percent of its information for no mechanical reason. I thought about a dead man's number calling me at 3:14am. I thought about a video that arrived timestamped for an hour that hadn't happened yet, delivering, with total flatness, the idea that none of it was supposed to mean anything — as though someone, or something, had decided that this was the exact week I needed to hear that argument made out loud, by a stranger's calm voice, at forty-one minutes past nine, in a city that was still, underneath me, cooking dinner and ringing temple bells and doing all the ordinary things a meaningless universe apparently still bothers to do.

I called Neung. He picked up on the fourth ring, television loud behind him, one of the Chinese dramas our mother used to watch dubbed badly into Thai. I didn't tell him about the video. I asked him, instead, whether he remembered the Pollock wall, and he laughed, actually laughed, the particular laugh he saves for when he thinks I've finally lost the plot in some harmless, forgivable way. "You painted it with fish sauce," he said. "Mae wouldn't wash it off for ten years. I used to think that wall was the only honest thing in that whole house." I asked him what he meant by that. He said he didn't know, it just felt true when he said it, and could we talk about something else, the drama was at the good part.

I said sure. We didn't talk about something else. We just hung up, the way brothers do when the good part of somebody else's drama is a perfectly reasonable excuse to stop before either of you says something you'd have to keep meaning afterward.

I went back inside a little after eleven. Reheated nothing, because I'd already had my one meal, and one meal means one meal even on nights like this — the rule exists precisely for nights like this, for the exact hour when the rule is hardest to keep and therefore most worth keeping. I lay in the dark running the video back in my head instead of on the screen, the flat unhurried voice, the three-second sound like a chair adjusting under someone's weight, and I thought: if nothing means anything, then someone went to a great deal of trouble, at a very specific hour, to make absolutely sure I heard that. Which is its own kind of contradiction, the kind this city has been training me to hold in one hand since I was small enough to lose myself in a mall for eleven minutes and come out the other side certain nobody was actually looking. Someone was looking. I just hadn't worked out yet from where.

I did not sleep well again. I am starting to notice the pattern in that sentence more than I'm noticing the sleep itself.
