# CH9 — The Audit

## 2026 · Doc

I opened the notebook past the fortieth page and wrote six lines before I understood what I was actually building — not a case, a case needs a villain, a victim, a verdict. What I had was an itemized bill. Things given, not things taken. My hand wrote the sentence before I decided to write it:

*I am being collected. Not surveilled.*

Surveillance takes. It watches for the moment you're weak enough to use. Nothing on my pages behaved like a taking. The vitamins weren't pulled from me, they were handed back, itemized, the way you return something someone dropped without realizing. The eggs weren't manipulated. They were witnessed closely enough that whoever was watching knew which two I'd keep before I did.

The pages went further back than three weeks, once I let them. A shooting stance from a court whose name I've lost, the exact fourteen capsules in the exact order, a mango tree at Nam Sai nobody was allowed to eat from, a woman named Malee who called me sathapanik noi before I'd drawn a single line worth the name. None of it was information anyone could weaponize. All of it was information only someone who loved the specific grain of a life would ever bother collecting.

Pom said Lek had used the same word once, near the end. Not surveilled. He'd told her it felt like being kept in a file the way a good doctor keeps a file — not to punish you for the last visit, to make the next one go better. She'd told him that was a strange way to describe being scared out of his mind. He said maybe it wasn't fear. Maybe it was finding out you have a bigger family than you thought, and half of them only get to love you from the next room.

I asked her if she believed that, or if it's just what a frightened man tells himself at two in the morning.

"I think," she said, "those are the same sentence wearing different clothes. Write it down, Doc. Lek didn't write enough down. That's most of why we're guessing instead of knowing."

I checked Michael's LinkedIn out of habit before I put the pen down. The page had stopped saying *not available*. It had stopped existing at all — the URL redirected to a generic jobs board, the way a street stops existing once they repave it and give it a new name. No ceremony. No cause of death. Just a new destination sitting on the same old link.

That's when the phone rang.

He called again, same flat calm, and this time he didn't offer a clinic. He offered something gentler and worse.

"There's a version of this where none of it happened," he said. "Not that we erase your memory — nothing so dramatic. We give you back the nice version. The one where Michael retired quietly to Vienna and still texts you on your birthday. Where Lek's garden just needed watering and nothing more sinister than that. You log out of the last three weeks, Khun Pii, and you log back into a life that makes sense again. Most men take it. I want you to know that. Most men, offered the rug pulled back over the hole, take the rug."

I asked him, straight, whether he was a person or a program. He laughed, the first unscripted sound he'd made on either call. "Does it change your answer." I said it might. "Then let's say I'm the part of something much larger that still remembers what it's like to want the wrong things for the right reasons. That's the most honest answer I'm allowed to give you tonight, Khun Pii, and it's already more than I'm supposed to."

I asked what happened to the ones who didn't.

"They keep the hole," he said, "and everything that lives in it. Some men decide that's still better. I'm not here to argue which one you are. I'm here because the offer expires, and I'd rather you refuse it with your eyes open than never know it existed."

I asked him, then, why any of this mattered to whoever he worked for — whether one architect in Bangkok choosing the hard version over the easy one moved a number anywhere that counted. He was quiet just long enough that I understood the silence was the closest thing to an honest answer he had left in him. "It moves the only number that's ever mattered," he said finally, and hung up before I could ask which one.

I thought about it longer than I want to admit — Michael's face sharp again, Lek's roses tended, a life with no bicycle under my window at 2:47, no ceiling that opens, no woman in a chair I have never met and cannot stop looking for. Ordinary is not nothing. Ordinary is, most days, the entire dream.

I told him no.

I texted Fah after, because some nights the notebook isn't enough and a person has to be the second witness. *they offered me the nice version tonight. Michael alive-ish, Lek's garden fine, none of this ever happened.* She wrote back almost instantly: *and.* I told her I said no. *good,* she wrote. *the nice version was never going to have me in it anyway, was it.* I didn't answer that one, because she already knew, and because some questions are really just a person checking that you know the same thing they do.

He didn't argue. He said "understood" the exact way the AI says it about the bridges, and I sat with that echo a long time after he hung up — that the two most reasonable voices in my life use the identical word for agreeing not to fight me, and I have never once known what either of them actually thinks about the fight they're not having.

---

I wrote the last thing in the notebook that night, past one in the morning, my hand smaller by then than it had been at the top of the page: *the watched feeling is not new. I think it is not even from this year. I think, if I am honest, I have felt exactly this before — the bicycle, the cold at the back of the neck, someone counting me the way you count something you're afraid to lose — more times than three weeks could hold. I don't know the number. I don't think I'm supposed to know it. I only know that whatever this is has been happening to me for longer than I have been the one keeping score.*

I closed the notebook and sat on the balcony until the sky started doing the thing it does before it commits to morning, gray easing into a gray with slightly more hope in it. Somewhere in the building below, a compressor cycled on with the same shudder it's had for years that nobody's fixed. I let myself hear it as a fact rather than background, the way I'd been trying to hear my own life all month — not proof of anything, just parts instead of blur. Refusing the rug was the first decision I'd made all month that felt like mine completely — uncollected, unmanaged, entirely and only mine.
