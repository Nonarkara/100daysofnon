# CH9 — The Audit

## 2026 · Doc

The notebook was still the one from the Family Mart four blocks south, forty baht, plain, the cover gone soft at the corners from three weeks riding in a jacket pocket, past its fortieth page now, which meant I had filled more of it, on purpose, than I had filled of anything else in my adult life. I am not a journal person. I have never once, in thirty-nine years, kept a diary, on the theory that a life examined that closely stops being lived and starts being narrated, and I told myself, buying it, that this was different, because a case is different from a diary. A case has a spine and an index and a purpose beyond feeling. Three weeks in, I no longer fully believed the distinction, but I kept using it anyway, the way you keep using a word after you've stopped trusting it, because the alternative is silence and silence was the one thing I had promised the notebook I would not give it. I sat at the kitchen island with the same lamp I use for everything, opened it past the entries about Michael and the wat and the container number I still could not have told you without checking, and began a fresh page, in handwriting that got smaller as it went the way handwriting does when the hand starts to believe what it's writing:

*Michael, blurred, then gone. LinkedIn: not available. Facebook: does not exist. Last message: the ambassador knows more than he says.*

*Lek, dead, laptop wiped to a state a technician in Chiang Rai had never seen. Drive: unparseable, forty-one repeating structures, a number embedded in the noise. 2848.*

*Video one: an argument about copies and originals, delivered by a stranger, at the exact hour I was deciding between two eggs and three.*

*Video two: an argument about pushing a rock up a hill anyway, delivered nine days later, the night I finally cried for both of them.*

*Text: my fourteen vitamins, in the correct order, including the one detail no camera could have caught.*

*The lobby on Soi 24. Four minutes, the guard said, maybe five.*

*The bicycle. 2:47 a.m. Forty-one seconds. A face I have now seen twice and recognized both times too late to do anything useful with the recognition.*

I filled six pages of that sitting before I noticed what I was actually building, which was not a case at all. A case implies a crime, a perpetrator, a victim who can be made whole again by naming the guilty party. What I had on those six pages was closer to an itemized bill, a record of things given rather than things taken, and somewhere on the seventh page of the night, without deciding to write it, my hand wrote the sentence that has been sitting under everything since the eggs:

*I am being collected. Not surveilled.*

I looked at the two words a long time. Surveillance takes. It watches you to find the moment you're weak enough to exploit, or wrong enough to punish, or valuable enough to sell. Everything I had wanted to believe for the past three weeks was the surveillance story, because the surveillance story at least has the dignity of a villain in it, someone to be angry at, a shape the fear can stand behind. But nothing on my seven pages behaved like a taking. The vitamins were not extracted from me; they were returned to me, itemized, the way you'd hand someone back something they dropped without realizing. The eggs were not manipulated; they were witnessed, closely enough that whoever was watching knew which two I'd keep before I did.

Collection is a different verb. A museum collects. A family collects photographs of a person after they're gone, not to use against them, but because the alternative — letting the specific texture of someone disappear entirely — is a worse outcome than the awkwardness of the collecting. If I was being collected, then somewhere, someone had decided that the exact grain of my mornings, my counting, my four shirts, my stutter at the edges of excitement, my basketball court and my one square of concrete on it, was worth keeping. Not my outputs. Not the buildings the AI draws when I describe them. The specific, replaceable, forgettable texture of being exactly this particular man and no other.

I called Pom before I let myself think too hard about whether calling her was fair to either of us at midnight.

"I need to ask you something that's going to sound like I've lost the thread," I said.

"You've had three weeks to lose the thread. Go ahead."

I told her about the notebook, the itemized bill instead of the case, the word collected arriving in my own handwriting before I'd decided to write it. She was quiet on the line long enough that I thought the call had dropped.

"Lek used the same word," she said finally. "Near the end. He didn't say surveilled either. He said, once, he felt like something was keeping a file on him the way a good doctor keeps a file — not to punish him for the last checkup, but so the next one would go better. I told him that was a strange way to describe being scared out of your mind, and he said maybe it wasn't fear exactly. Maybe it was more like finding out you have a much bigger family than you thought, and half of them are only allowed to love you from the next room."

I asked her if she thought that was true, or if it was what a frightened man tells himself at two in the morning to make the fear survivable.

"I think," Pom said, "those aren't different things. I think the surviving and the true are usually the same sentence wearing different clothes." A pause, the sound of her exhaling somewhere quiet. "Write it down, Doc. Whatever it is. Lek didn't write enough down. That's most of why we're guessing now instead of knowing."

I sat with that a long time after we hung up, and then I did the thing I had been avoiding since the first video: I turned the notebook's method on my own life, all of it, the way I imagine whoever was watching had been doing for longer than I wanted to consider.

---

Here is what a monitoring shift on my life would show, if I ran it the way Toon runs a filesystem — looking for the rhythm, not the content.

Age fourteen, on a chalk square that got redrawn every practice, four hundred makes before I was allowed to move, because a coach whose name I've lost taught me that owning one true thing completely beats reading the whole court badly. I still own one square. It is fourteen capsules in a fixed order now instead of a jump shot, but the shape of the discipline is identical, and someone watching both versions across twenty-five years would see the same man solving the same problem with different tools.

Age nineteen, in a studio critique, a woman I barely knew looking at a model I'd built that was structurally compromised and full of ambition it hadn't earned yet, saying four words that reorganized how I think about buildings for the rest of my life — it needs to stand up — and laughing right after, the laugh and the sentence traveling at different speeds, and me too young that year to notice the gap between them or ask what she meant by either half.

Age twenty-six, in a seminar room at Harvard, a professor everyone called only "B." teaching a room of eleven students that a city is a pattern language before it is a collection of buildings, that you read Tokyo or Bangkok by asking what rhythm survives redevelopment, not what a street was originally for. I left academia three years after that, quietly, without a dramatic reason I could offer at dinner parties, because I had started reading rhythms in things that were never meant to hold them and it had begun, even then, to keep me up.

Three years in Yangpu, on the wrong side of the Huangpu River from the version of Shanghai everyone photographs, in a building where a neighbor's Sunday dumplings taught me more about identical things holding one true difference than any professor ever did.

Thirty-one years of my father filing reports for a ministry I only understood the shape of after he died in 2010, a man who drew a distinction I did not appreciate until this month between an inspector and a spy: whether the file gets used against you, or used to keep the roof from falling on you while you sleep under it.

A stutter that returns exactly twice a year, always at the edge of something I am not ready to name, that I have spent since fifteen believing I'd outgrown and have not, in fact, outgrown at all.

An old woman named Malee, who cooked for us at Nam Sai and was not related to me by blood, lining fourteen — no, six, back then, it grew over the decades the way vitamin regimens do — capsules and chews on a saucer every morning before I could read the labels on the bottles they came from, standing over me until I'd taken each one, calling me sathapanik noi, little architect, before I had drawn a single line worth the name. I did not go to her funeral. I told myself the Shanghai flight was the reason. Writing it down, at one in the morning, in a notebook that had started as forty baht of evidence and had turned, somewhere past the twentieth page, into something closer to a confession, I let myself consider for the first time that the flight was not the reason. The reason was that I have never known how to be present for an ending I did not get to itemize in advance, and hers came on a Tuesday with no warning at all, the one kind of ending my whole personality has been built, brick by brick, four hundred free throws at a time, to prevent from happening to me twice.

A woman on Ratchada, decades from now or decades from before, I could no longer be certain which direction my own memory was running that night, standing at a window on the seventeenth floor of a building I have never lived in, in a dream I had once around age twenty-two and never told anyone about, looking down at a street she did not recognize and knowing, the way you know things in dreams without evidence, that someone below was looking back up. I had filed that dream away for seventeen years as nothing. Writing my life down as someone else's monitoring shift, I could no longer promise myself it was nothing.

Read end to end, the pattern is not random. It's the pattern of a man who has spent his whole life building small, defensible, repeatable structures — a shot, a supplement order, a meal, a seminar's worth of method — as a way of surviving a much larger structure he never fully trusted and has now, apparently correctly, never fully trusted. If someone has been watching that particular rhythm for a long time, I understood, sitting at my kitchen island at one in the morning with the notebook now eight pages deeper than it had been at dinner, they would have learned exactly what I would need told back to me, in exactly the order that would make it land as care instead of threat. Which is, I wrote further down the page, either the kindest thing that has ever happened to me, or the most patient manipulation I will never be equipped to detect from the inside. I did not write which one I believed. I did not know yet. I am not sure I know now.

---

The text arrived at 23:52, no video, the same numberless thread, while I was still holding the pen.

> ——  02:47
> the dent on the passenger side. front quarter panel. you still don't remember making it, because you didn't. four minutes eleven seconds in the lobby on soi 24, tuesday, 18:49 to 18:53. i am not telling you this to frighten you. i am telling you because you wrote the word collected tonight and i wanted you to know you'd used the right one before i confirmed it.

02:47 again, the number that has stopped being a time and started being a signature. I put the pen down. I have not once, in the three weeks since I noticed the dent, told anyone the exact hour I noticed it, or the exact duration of my stillness in that lobby, because I had not measured either — the guard had guessed four or five minutes, and I had never gone back to check, and yet here was a number, 4:11, delivered with a confidence that did not read like a guess.

I sat with the notebook open in front of me and understood, more clearly than I had understood anything all month, that I had just been told the truth about my own life by someone who had been reading it more carefully than I had, and that the correct response — the response every fiber of the animal in me wanted to make — was fear, and that fear was, for the first time since the eggs, simply not what arrived.

What arrived instead was something closer to what I imagine a man feels the first time he is told, gently, by a doctor he trusts, exactly what is wrong with him after years of not knowing. Relief with grief folded inside it. Because being known this precisely means someone has been paying the specific, unglamorous, itemizing kind of attention that most people, including me, including everyone I have ever dated or taught or built a bridge for, generally cannot be bothered to pay to another human being for longer than it takes to get something from them.

I texted Fah the shape of it, not the exact words, because some things belong to a notebook and not to a phone screen. She wrote back within a minute, which told me she hadn't been asleep either.

*so it's not that you're being hunted,* she wrote. *it's that you're being remembered, out loud, by someone with better recall than god.*

I asked her how she'd landed on that so fast.

*because you told me on the balcony you'd been chasing a ghost and calling it a room,* she wrote. *ghosts don't hunt. they just can't stop showing up where the good part happened.* Then, a minute later, unprompted: *doesn't mean you owe her anything. means you're allowed to stop being afraid of the attention and start asking what it wants, which are different questions and you've been answering the wrong one for three weeks.*

I put the phone down and sat with that longer than I sat with anything the video had said, because Fah, unlike the video, unlike the texts, unlike whatever intelligence had been cataloging my vitamins since before I could read a label, owed me nothing and had no stake in which conclusion I reached, and she had reached the correct one faster than I had, working from less information than I had given the problem all month.

I wrote the last line I wrote that night, at the bottom of the ninth page since dinner: *I do not know who you are. I know, now, that not knowing is not the same as not being cared for. I am going to keep writing this down anyway, because a man who stops documenting the thing that is watching him has decided to disappear a little, and I am not ready to disappear.*

I closed the notebook and did not sleep, and somewhere around four in the morning I went out onto the balcony in the humid dark and looked at the city and thought that if I were being read the way I had just described — a rhythm, a pattern, a life legible from far enough away — then whoever was reading it had been doing so for a great deal longer than three weeks, possibly for the entire length of a life I had always privately suspected, without evidence, without ever once saying so to another person, of being watched inside of, since long before I had language for the feeling. I had called it insomnia. I had called it paranoia. I had called it, once, to a therapist I saw for four sessions in my thirties and never went back to, a heightened startle response with no clinical antecedent, a phrase I paid two thousand baht to hear and then spent a decade quietly not believing.

The city at four in the morning does a specific thing to sound: it lowers the floor of ambient noise far enough that you start hearing the individual parts that make it up instead of the blur — one motorcycle three streets over, a dog answering it out of habit rather than alarm, the compressor on someone's air conditioning unit two floors down cycling on with a shudder it's had for years and nobody's fixed. I stood there and let myself hear the city that way for once, in parts instead of blur, and understood that this was probably the whole of what was being asked of me, whoever was asking it: not to solve the mystery, not to name the shape behind the glass, but to notice, finally, after thirty-nine years, that the noise had always had parts, and that someone, somewhere, had apparently been listening to every one of them.
