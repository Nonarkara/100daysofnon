# CH3 — Last Thursday

## 2026 · Doc

A boy left a notebook on my table at Michael's memorial dinner. Left is generous — he was gone before I noticed, out the door mid-toast, no goodbye, like the room got too loud in a way only he could hear.

He'd sat at the far end, fourteen people who used to be Michael's friends and one stranger nobody could place. Twenty-two, maybe twenty-three. Introduced himself as a "junior researcher" at some institute nobody wrote down. Didn't eat much. Watched me more than he watched Michael's photo on the easel by the door.

I found the notebook under his napkin twenty minutes after he left. Black cover, cheap, the spine already cracking from being opened too many times for something so new.

I keep every receipt myself. Not out of thrift — I have more money than I know how to spend responsibly — but because a receipt is a timestamp with a signature nobody can argue with later, and lately I've started to feel like a man who might need signatures. Maybe that's why I didn't hand the notebook to the restaurant. It felt like evidence before I'd read a word of it.

I read it in the taxi instead, then again at my kitchen island, then a third time standing up.

Most of it was ordinary — dates, a grocery list, a half-finished letter to someone named June he never sent. Three pages in, handwriting getting smaller the longer the entry ran, like he was trying to fit a thought into less room than it needed:

*If the universe began last Thursday, complete, with your memories already installed, you could never prove otherwise. Every scar, every receipt, already there. The theory isn't about time. It's about whether evidence means anything once you admit it could've been placed.*

Two lines down, no explanation, like a thought that arrived from somewhere else entirely:

*Kahneman left the stage himself. Didn't wait for the ending to be written for him. Everyone talks about the ending. Nobody talks about the leaving early.*

I don't know what that has to do with Last Thursday. I've read it eleven times since and it still sits crooked in my head, a door that opens onto another door instead of a room.

Here's the thought that's been sitting under my sternum since Tuesday's video, since the flat voice describing a universe assembled without an author: what if none of this — the receipts, Lek's small handwriting, a wall in a house I grew up in — what if none of it happened, and I only remember it happening, because the whole apparatus of my memory, all forty-five years of it, was installed complete and pre-aged last Thursday. It's an old idea. Russell had a version of it before I was born — the whole universe, including your memory of breakfast, conjured a moment ago, fully furnished with a past that never occurred. Nobody can disprove it, because any evidence you produce — a photograph, a scar, a receipt — could just as easily have been installed alongside the memory as part of the same instantaneous furniture.

I know the argument is unfalsifiable by design. Knowing that didn't stop me laying four years of receipts on the kitchen island Sunday morning like a man building a case for a jury that doesn't exist — thermal paper gone gray, an old boarding pass, a photo of Neung and me at the compound gate, both of us squinting into a sun neither of us knew yet to shield against. None of it proved anything. All of it felt like the only thing standing between me and a version of my life with nothing underneath it.

I called Neung. "Tell me about the mango tree," I said, no hello.

"Nam Sai. The one nobody picked from." He told it back exact — Mae's line about fallen fruit being for people with less money than us, said like a kindness. He asked why I wanted to know. I said just checking something, and he laughed, the specific laugh of a man deciding his younger brother is having one of his episodes and deciding, out of a lifetime of practice, not to make it worse by asking directly.

Twenty minutes later a voice memo arrived, forty-one seconds, him talking about a rice shipment stuck at the port over a customs complaint on moisture content. A broker's name. A container number. Days. Nothing my memory could have pre-installed, because I didn't know any of it until the moment I heard it. I listened to it four times. I don't know what I was testing for — some instinct that a fabricated world would slip somewhere, produce something too smooth, and a real one would keep handing me small ugly specific facts nobody would bother inventing. The theory has a hole in it. An author good enough to fabricate forty-five years is certainly good enough to fabricate a boring memo about rice. Knowing the hole didn't close it. I filed the memo anyway, in the notebook I hadn't bought yet.

Six minutes. It went exactly where the boy's notebook had already gone — the case for a world assembled whole, memories installed as furniture rather than lived. It used Russell's name. At one point it sounded like it was quoting something I read at twenty-two and forgot I'd read.

Fah texted after, *ate already?*, which for us is an invitation with a right answer built into its own casualness. We've been doing this eight months, no label either of us bothered assigning. She works compliance at a bank on Sathorn, reads transaction flags for patterns that suggest a person is lying about where their money came from, and has told me more than once that dating me feels like an extension of her job — a pattern that keeps almost resolving and never quite does.

We went to her place in Ekkamai, a studio with a view of nothing, which she prefers, because a view is just something else demanding your attention when you're trying to give it to a person instead. I won't describe all of it. I'll say this: she likes to be told what she already knows is true rather than asked, and I felt her exhale change before I'd finished the sentence, a full-body agreement arriving ahead of any part of her I was actually touching. Near the end her back arched the degree it only arches near the end, and the glimpse came, and this time it stayed.

Two seconds, maybe three. Long enough to register detail instead of just presence. White composite chairs, a whole row, more than I could count before the room folded back into Fah's ceiling. Empty. Facing a direction I couldn't determine because there was no far wall.

I didn't tell Fah. I've never told Fah. She would ask a reasonable question I don't have a reasonable answer for, and I've decided, for now, that the unreasonable answer is mine to carry alone.

Zhuangzi dreamed he was a butterfly and woke and couldn't say afterward whether he was a man who'd dreamed of being a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming he was a man. I read that at twenty-two and thought it was a party trick, clever, safe because it demanded nothing but admiration. I don't think that anymore. A butterfly dreaming it's a man loses nothing by the confusion. A man who spends his one life treating it as a butterfly's dream loses the only thing he actually had.

I put on shoes at 1am and walked four blocks to a Family Mart and bought a notebook, plain, forty baht, and a pen, and sat on the curb under the fluorescent awning and wrote the date, the time, and: *this is not evidence of anything. this is a record of what I noticed. I am keeping it anyway.* Then I copied the container number from Neung's voice memo, from memory, in case this dissolves and I wake up somewhere else and want one true, boring fact written into something thick enough to survive it.

The cashier's name tag said Now. He rang it up without looking at either item, the way you do ten thousand identical transactions a shift and stop seeing the objects, only the total. I found that comforting. Proof some part of this city still runs on nothing more mysterious than boredom and a wage.
