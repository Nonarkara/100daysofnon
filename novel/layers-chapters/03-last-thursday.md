# CH3 — Last Thursday

## 2026 · Doc

I keep every receipt. Not out of thrift — I have more money than I know how to spend responsibly, which is its own quiet problem — but because a receipt is a timestamp with a signature nobody can argue with later, and lately I have started to feel like a man who might need signatures.

Sunday morning I emptied the drawer where they live, a shallow one under the kitchen island meant for cutlery I don't own enough of to fill it. Four years of thermal paper, curling at the edges, most of the ink already fading to a gray ghost of itself because thermal paper is designed to forget, chemically, on purpose, within about eighteen months. I found a receipt from a 7-Eleven on Thonglor, 2019, one hundred and forty-seven baht, a Coke and a tuna sandwich and something I can no longer read. I found a parking ticket from a mall that closed in 2021. I found, folded into quarters at the very bottom, a receipt for four shirts, identical, gray, bought the same week I started wearing only four shirts, which means the rotation itself has a birth certificate, a date I could look up if the ink hadn't already surrendered.

I laid them out on the kitchen island in rows, oldest to newest, the way you'd lay out evidence, because that is exactly what I was doing. Beside the receipts I put the other things the drawer had been quietly hoarding: a boarding pass to Shanghai, 2013, one way, the kind of ticket you buy when you've told yourself the return date doesn't matter yet. A business card from a professor at Oxford who has since died, his title already one rank behind what I remember him actually holding by the time I left, which either means my memory upgraded him out of affection or the university did, posthumously, out of the same. A photograph, actual paper, printed at some drugstore kiosk because I'd wanted one thing in the apartment that couldn't be edited by anything, of Neung and me at the compound gate — I was twelve, he was six, both of us squinting into a sun neither of us had learned yet to shield our eyes from on instinct.

None of it proved anything. All of it felt, laid out under the kitchen light at seven in the morning, like the only thing standing between me and a version of my life with nothing underneath it at all.

Here is the thought that has been sitting under my sternum since Tuesday's video, since the flat unhurried voice describing a universe assembled without an author: what if none of this — the shirts, the receipts, Lek's small handwriting, the wall in Nam Sai — what if none of it happened, and I only remember it happening, because the entire apparatus of my memory, all forty-five years of it, was installed complete and pre-aged last Thursday. It's an old idea. Philosophers had a version of it before I was born — Russell's five minutes, the whole universe including your memory of breakfast conjured into existence a moment ago, fully furnished with a past that never occurred. Nobody could ever disprove it, because any evidence you produced to disprove it — a photograph, a scar, a receipt — could just as easily have been installed alongside the memory as part of the same instantaneous furniture.

I know this. I studied enough philosophy at Oxford to know the argument is unfalsifiable by design. Knowing that didn't stop me laying out four years of thermal paper on a kitchen island at 7am like a man building a case for a jury that doesn't exist.

I called Neung.

"Tell me about the mango tree," I said, no hello, because we don't do hellos, we haven't since we were teenagers and hellos started to feel like something you said to people you weren't sure would answer.

"What mango tree."

"Nam Sai. The one nobody picked from."

He was quiet a second, the particular quiet of a man deciding whether the question is a joke. "It dropped fruit all over the side yard, by the servant's entrance, and Mae wouldn't let anyone eat it because she said fallen fruit was for people with less money than us, which I always thought was the ugliest sentence I ever heard her say and she said it like it was a kindness." He paused. "Why."

"Just checking something."

"Checking what."

"Nothing. Send me a voice note. Anything. I want to hear you say something you haven't said to me before."

He laughed, not unkindly, the specific laugh of a man who has decided his younger brother is having one of his episodes and has also decided, out of eleven months of seniority and a lifetime of practice, not to make it worse by asking directly. Twenty minutes later a voice memo arrived, forty-one seconds, him talking about a rice shipment delayed at the port, some customs complaint about moisture content, mundane, specific, full of details — a broker's name, a container number, a number of days — that no version of my memory could have pre-installed, because I didn't know any of it until the moment I heard it.

I listened to it four times. I don't know what I was testing for. Some instinct that a genuinely fabricated world would slip somewhere, produce something too smooth, too authored, and that a real one would keep handing me small ugly specific facts about container moisture content that nobody, however good the fabrication, would have bothered inventing. The theory has a hole in it and I know the hole: an author good enough to fabricate the last forty-five years is certainly good enough to fabricate a boring voice memo about rice. But knowing the hole didn't close it. I filed the memo anyway, into the folder I'd started calling evidence.

The AI, unrelated to any of this, sent me a structural report on the bridge at 11am, flagged green, ready for construction documents. I approved it. I noticed, approving it, that I trusted the report completely and trusted my own memory of Tuesday considerably less, and that this asymmetry should probably worry me more than it did.

At two I went to the dentist, which had nothing to do with any of it and everything to do with the fact that I keep appointments the way other people keep promises to God. Dr. Wichit's office in Phrom Phong, the same chair I've used for six years, upper right, minor decay, the drill's specific frequency doing what it does to the part of my brain that processes fear before the rational part gets a vote. Nothing happened this time. I want that on the record too, because I half expected something to, the way you half expect lightning on a day you've already decided is cursed. Nothing happened. The drill was just the drill. The ceiling tile was just the ceiling tile, water-stained in the corner in a shape I've had eleven years to memorize and have never once found meaning in, on purpose, as a kind of discipline.

Walking out, I passed a florist's stall near the BTS with buckets of the white flower they use for funeral garlands, dok rak, and stopped without deciding to, the way your feet stop at a red light before your mind has finished registering that it's red. The smell of it — rain on wet concrete, somehow, in a flower — put me, for exactly as long as a breath, in the wat in Chiang Rai watching smoke go into a white northern sky that had nothing to do with Bangkok's sky at all. I bought nothing. I just stood there a moment longer than a man buying nothing usually stands, and the vendor watched me with the patient boredom of someone who has seen every version of grief a customer can perform without purchasing.

Fah texted at nine. We've been doing this on and off for eight months, no label either of us has bothered assigning, an arrangement built entirely around the fact that neither of us wanted the other thing, the thing with a name, the thing that requires you to explain yourself at family dinners. She works compliance at a bank on Sathorn, spends her days reading transaction flags for patterns that suggest a person is lying about where their money came from, and she has told me, more than once, that dating me feels like an extension of her job — a pattern that keeps almost resolving and never quite does.

*ate already?* she texted, which for us is an invitation, a screening question with a right answer built into its own casualness. I said no, though I had eaten, one meal, hours ago, the rule intact — I just wasn't going to make her responsible for that arithmetic. She sent her address again even though I've had it memorized for six months, a small act of formality she performs every time, like a woman stamping a document that's already been signed, and I understood it the way I understand most of what she does: not distrust, procedure. Procedure is how she loves things. I've stopped needing it translated.

We went to her place this time, a studio in Ekkamai with a view of nothing, which she prefers because a view, she says, is just something else demanding your attention when you're trying to give it to a person instead.

I want to describe what happened the way I'd want someone to describe it about me, if the roles were reversed — which is to say, from inside her experience rather than mine, because I've noticed, this year, that the version worth remembering was never really about what I did. It's about what she let happen to her, on her own terms, in a room she chose.

She likes to be told what she already knows is true rather than asked. She told me that early, the second or third time, matter-of-fact, the way she'd explain a compliance procedure — this is the shape of it, work within the shape. So I didn't ask. I said it, low, close to her ear, the specific thing I'd noticed about her that week, some detail from a text she'd sent about her sister's wedding, and I felt her exhale change before anything else changed, a full-body agreement arriving ahead of any part of her body I was actually touching. That's the part nobody explains about a woman who knows exactly what she wants: the yes happens somewhere behind the sternum first, and everything after is just the body catching up to a decision already made.

She held the sheet in both fists, not from strain, from something closer to anchoring, a person keeping one hand on a rope even when the rope isn't currently needed, because you don't let go of a rope on principle. Her back arched the particular degree it arches only near the end, and I watched her face do the thing faces do when a person stops performing pleasure and starts simply having it — the difference is visible if you're paying attention, a kind of visible relief, like a held breath deciding it doesn't need holding anymore.

And then the glimpse came, and this time it stayed.

Not a millisecond. Two full seconds, maybe three — long enough that I registered detail rather than just presence. White composite chairs, a whole row of them, more than I could count before the room folded itself back into Fah's ceiling and Fah's fan turning slow overhead. Chairs, empty, facing some direction I couldn't determine because there was no far wall to face toward, just chairs continuing past where my attention could follow them. No people in them, this time. Or none I could see in two seconds. The air, even across whatever distance separates her ceiling from that room, carried the same smell as before, which is to say none at all, an absence so complete it registered as its own specific texture.

Zhuangzi dreamed he was a butterfly, and woke, and couldn't say afterward whether he was a man who had dreamed of being a butterfly or was now a butterfly dreaming he was a man. I read that at twenty-two and thought it was a party trick, clever, unresolvable, safe precisely because it demanded nothing of you but admiration. I don't think that anymore. Lying there with Fah's weight settling into sleep against my ribs, I thought instead about the specific cruelty of the parable, which is that it never tells you the cost of guessing wrong. A butterfly dreaming it's a man loses nothing by the confusion. A man who spends his one life treating it as a butterfly's dream loses the only thing he actually had.

I lay there after, Fah's head on my chest, her breathing slowing into the particular rhythm that means she'll be asleep within four minutes, and I thought: the room is getting longer. The first time, a wall. Now a wall and rows of chairs and no telling how far back they go. Whatever this is, it isn't static. It's revealing itself to me one increment at a time, like a photograph developing in a tray, and I don't know yet if that's a mercy — giving me time to adjust — or a method, patient and deliberate, the way you'd reveal something to a person you didn't want to startle into refusing to look at all.

I did not tell Fah. I have never told Fah. She would ask a reasonable question I don't have a reasonable answer for, and I have decided, for now, that the unreasonable answer is mine to carry alone.

Six minutes and two seconds, this time, and it went exactly where the receipts on my kitchen island had already gone that morning — the unfalsifiable case for a world assembled whole, memories included, at some arbitrary recent instant, every scar and receipt and childhood mango tree installed as furniture rather than lived. It used the philosophers' names. It used Russell's. It sounded, at points, almost like it was quoting something I'd read once at twenty-two and forgotten I'd read, which is either a coincidence or isn't, and I no longer trust myself to know which category to file that under.

I watched it standing at the kitchen island, the receipts still laid out in their rows from that morning, oldest to newest, my own evidence arranged directly underneath the exact argument that says evidence like this proves nothing at all. I stood there a long time. Then I did something I haven't done since university: I put on shoes at 1am and walked to the twenty-four-hour Family Mart four blocks south and bought a paper notebook, plain, one hundred pages, forty baht, the kind schoolchildren use, and a pen, and I sat on the curb outside under the fluorescent awning and wrote, in handwriting that has never once in my life looked as small or as careful as Lek's, the date, the time, and the words: *this is not evidence of anything. this is a record of what I noticed. I am keeping it anyway.*

Then I wrote down the container number from Neung's voice memo, from memory, because if this world dissolves and I wake up somewhere else entirely, I want to have written one true, boring, specific fact into a physical object thick enough to survive it. I don't know what I thought that would accomplish. I know it was the first thing I'd done in weeks that made the two hours after midnight feel like something other than a war I was losing quietly, alone, on a balcony, one pouch at a time.

The Family Mart cashier, a young man with a name tag that said Now, rang up the notebook and the pen without looking at either, the way you ring up ten thousand identical transactions a shift and stop seeing the objects, only the total. Forty baht. He said it in the flat automated cadence of someone whose voice has been trained by repetition into something closer to a receipt printer than a person, and I found that oddly comforting — proof, however small, that some processes in this city still run on a script written by nothing more mysterious than boredom and a minimum wage.

Walking home, four blocks, the streets doing the thing Bangkok streets do at one in the morning, which is neither sleep nor waking but a third state entirely — a soi dog asleep across a doorway that would be swept clean by six, a noodle cart's owner counting a stack of twenty-baht notes under a single bare bulb, a security guard at a condo gate scrolling his phone with the specific stillness of a man being paid to be present rather than to be useful — I held the notebook against my chest like something that could be taken from me, and for four blocks, not one thought I had was about whether any of it was real. I was just a man walking home with a new notebook. That, it turns out, was the whole relief. Not an answer. Just four blocks where the question forgot to ask itself.
