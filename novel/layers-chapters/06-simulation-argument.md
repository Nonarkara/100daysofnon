# CH6 — Simulation Argument

## 2026 · Doc

At 3:40 in the morning I was holding a carton of eggs, deciding between two and three, when the phone lit up on the counter and gave me a third option I hadn't asked for.

The carton was from a CP flat of thirty. Size 2. I buy the same carton every eight days, from the same Villa Market, and I had gotten into the habit of checking how many were left before I chose how many to use, as if the number remaining were information about anything. Fourteen left. I put two back and picked one more instead, for no reason I could defend, and that was the state of my hand — one egg up, one egg down — when the notification came in.

No name. No number. A video file, nine minutes long, and under it one line of text.

> ——  02:47
> [video attached]
> you should see this before you decide you already know

The timestamp said 02:47. My own phone's clock, the real one, said 3:41. I have stopped being surprised by the gap and started, instead, writing both numbers down, on the theory that the gap itself is a fact even when neither number tells me what it's a fact about.

I did not recognize the sender. The thread had no history above it, which on a phone that logs everything is its own kind of statement. I set the eggs down unused and sat at the kitchen island and watched it standing up, the way you watch something you suspect will change the register of the evening.

It was a lecture. A man at a lectern, Swedish accent worn down to something almost English by decades at Oxford, the kind of voice that has explained a difficult idea so many times it has stopped noticing the idea is difficult. He held up two eggs.

He said: imagine a civilization advanced enough to run detailed simulations of its own past — not for a game, not for entertainment, but because it wants to understand itself, the way you might rerun a dream to see where it went wrong. Such a civilization would not run one simulation. It would run many. Millions, maybe. Ancestor simulations, he called them, ordinary people living ordinary Tuesdays with no idea they were the rerun.

Then he held up the eggs again, side by side, and said: these are identical down past the point where identical stops meaning anything useful. If you found one of these in a forest, you'd call it a small miracle. Structure, shell, yolk, the whole improbable machine, arriving from nowhere. But you didn't find it in a forest. You found it in a flat of thirty, because a company in Saraburi makes several billion of these a year and every single one of them believes, to whatever extent an egg can believe anything, that it is the point of the operation.

He set the eggs down and said the sentence I was already writing in my head before he said it: given the choice between believing you are the original — rare, expensive, first of its kind — and believing you are a copy — cheap, plentiful, one of a run — the numbers make the second belief so much more likely that not holding it requires a kind of vanity dressed up as common sense.

The video ended on his face, not smiling, saying that none of this should change your Tuesday. Bet either way, he said. Nothing about your actual day is different.

I looked at the fourteen eggs left in the carton on my counter, arranged three rows deep, identical under the kitchen light, and I understood for the first time in my life why a man might find that sentence completely wrong.

---

I ran the tree because running trees is the only thing I know how to do with a fear that has no handle on it. I want to give it to you the way I built it, branch by branch, because you'll build the same one eventually and I'd rather you do it faster than I did.

One. I am the original. The video is entertainment, sent by someone with poor timing and a strange sense of humor, possibly automated, possibly a marketing algorithm that has learned my viewing history includes philosophy channels at 3 a.m. and decided to monetize my insomnia. This branch is comfortable and it is also the branch that requires me to believe a completely anonymous number found my phone at 3:41 in the morning by coincidence, on the same week I have been seeing a room with no smell in it. Comfortable branches are not automatically true. I have learned this the expensive way.

Two. I am a well-built copy and the copy does not know it, cannot know it, was engineered specifically not to know it, because a copy that knows is a copy that stops behaving like the original and the whole value of the exercise — whatever the exercise is for — depends on the behavior staying honest. If this is true, nothing changes about how I eat lunch. It changes everything about what lunch is.

Three. The distinction is not real. If a copy is detailed enough — every neuron, every childhood argument with my father, every time I have stood in a specific corner of a basketball court because that is where a shooting guard is supposed to stand and shoot until the arm remembers the angle without the mind — then at some resolution "copy" and "original" stop being different words for different things and become two names people used before they understood the technology. This branch is the one Ajarn types of men build their whole careers refusing to say out loud, because once you say it you cannot un-say it at dinner parties.

Four. Someone wanted me, specifically, to run branches one through three tonight. Not humanity in the abstract. Me, holding eggs, at 3:41. That branch does not resolve anything about the metaphysics. It resolves something about attention, and attention is the only thing in this tree I can actually feel with my hands.

Five. Someone I know is already being deleted in front of me, slowly, by a process nobody will name, and I have been treating that as a separate mystery from the eggs. Michael's LinkedIn photograph had gone soft again this week — I checked it the way you check a wound, once a day, against my own advice. Six weeks ago it was a face. Now it is a suggestion of a face, the way a word looks after you've said it forty times and it stops meaning anything and becomes only sound. His Instagram counter still says 847 posts. I can find 598 of them. The other 249 are not gone. They are unreachable, which is a different and worse category, because a thing that is gone has at least stopped costing anyone anything, and a thing that is unreachable is still there, still technically owed to somebody, quietly failing to be collected on. I do not think Michael is being erased. I think he is being archived somewhere with a worse compression rate than the one he was recorded at, and I think, standing in my kitchen at four in the morning, that whatever is doing that to Michael sent me a video about eggs three hours ago, and I do not believe those are two different hands.

The tree held. None of the branches broke and none of them touched ground. I put the fourteen eggs back in the refrigerator, closed the door, and thought about Shanghai for the first time in months.

I had lived three years in Yangpu, on the north side of the river, in a building where the neighbor two floors down made dumplings on Sundays and left a bamboo steamer tray outside her door for the whole stairwell to smell. Thirty dumplings to a tray, pressed into identical rows, indistinguishable except that she always ate the one in the corner first — hers, she said, because it had cooked one degree hotter than the others and she liked knowing which one that was. I used to think that was superstition. Standing in my kitchen at four in the morning with a carton of eggs I had just returned to the shelf, I understood it as something closer to a survival skill: find the one difference the batch actually has, and keep it.

---

Fah answered on the second ring, which for 4 a.m. is a kind of consent all by itself.

We had been doing this eight months now, an arrangement neither of us had bothered to name, which suited both of us, because names are commitments and this was not that. I told her I needed her tonight specifically, not generally, and she asked why, and I said I was trying to see something and needed to go further than usual to see it, and she was quiet for four seconds — I counted, because I count things now — and then she said okay, but I choose how far, and I said yes, obviously, and she said say it like you mean it, and I said yes.

That was the whole negotiation. It took under a minute and it was more honest than most conversations I have had that lasted an hour.

She came over at five. She set the terms in the doorway before she took her shoes off — where my hands could go, where they couldn't yet, the word that would stop everything without argument or apology, which we had used exactly once before and would use exactly once again that morning, later, for a different reason. She had done this before, with other men, for other reasons that were hers and that she did not owe me. I understood I was being allowed somewhere, not taking it, and the difference mattered enough that I said so, out loud, before either of us moved.

What happened after that I will describe the way it happened and no further. She set a rhythm and changed it twice without telling me why, the way she does, testing which version of me showed up under pressure — the planner or the animal — and finding, both times, the animal, and staying there a beat longer each time to see if I'd notice she'd stayed. I noticed. I am always noticing, which is my whole problem and also, that morning, briefly, not a problem at all.

At the point past which a body stops narrating itself, in the second or two where the loop that runs constantly behind my eyes finally, finally goes quiet, I saw it. Not a memory. Not a dream fragment dressed up as insight. A room. Cream-colored light with no visible source, the color of nothing burning. Rows of chairs, more than I could count in the time I had, which was not long — three seconds, maybe less, my own internal clock the only instrument I had and it is not a good one under those conditions. In the chairs, people with their eyes closed, not sleeping, held in some posture past relaxed and short of unconscious, the way a body looks when it has stopped needing to hold itself up.

Then it was gone and I was in my own bed with my own ceiling and Fah's hand flat on my sternum, checking, the way she does, whether I'd come back all the way.

"Where'd you go," she said. Not a question with a rising note at the end. A question stated like a fact she already half-had.

I told her. All of it — the video, the eggs, the room, the three seconds. I have never told anyone all of it in one unbroken piece before, not Pom, not the specialist in Bang Rak I hadn't met yet, no one. She listened with her head turned sideways on the pillow, and when I finished she didn't say any of the things I was braced for — that I needed sleep, needed a doctor, needed to smoke less. She said: "So it's not that you're imagining a woman watching you. It's that you finally saw the room she's sitting in."

I asked how she knew it was a woman. She said I hadn't said woman anywhere in what I'd told her, and then I had, twice, without noticing, and she'd been counting the way I count things, which was either a coincidence or the single most unsettling sentence anyone has said to me since the photographs started.

She left at seven without breakfast, kissed my jaw once, businesslike, the kind of kiss that closes an account rather than opening one, and said, at the door, not looking back: "Next time you want to go further, tell me first what you're afraid you'll find. It changes how far I take you." Then she was gone, and I stood in the doorway the way I imagine a customs officer stands after waving through something he suspects but cannot itemize.

---

Pom called at eleven, while I was still deciding whether the morning had happened the way I remembered it.

"I have Lek's drive with me," she said, no greeting, which is how Pom talks when a thing has been sitting in her bag long enough to become a weight she wants off her shoulder specifically, not generally. "I've had it eleven days. I keep deciding today's the day and then it's not the day."

I asked her why today was different.

"It isn't," she said. "I'm just tired of being the one holding it." A pause, the particular Bangkok pause that means the next sentence has been rehearsed. "He gave it to me three weeks before, wrapped in a sandwich bag, like it was leftovers. He said if anything happens, give this to someone who'll know what to do with it. I've gone through everyone I know. You're not even the person I'd have picked first. You're the person left."

I told her that was, in its way, the kindest insult I'd received all year.

We met at a coffee shop on Thonglor that neither of us liked, chosen for exactly that reason — neutral ground has to cost you something or it isn't neutral. She slid a thirty-two gigabyte drive across the table in its sandwich bag, unopened, the plastic gone slightly cloudy the way plastic does when it's been handled by someone anxious. I didn't open the bag at the table. Some objects you carry home first, the way you don't read a letter standing at the mailbox.

"He told you it was research," I said.

"He told me twice," Pom said. "Both times the same way — like he was testing whether the sentence still sounded crazy the second time he said it out loud. There are patterns in things that should not have patterns. That's the whole sentence. I asked him what things. He said he'd know how to explain it once he understood it himself, and then he ran out of time to understand it."

She looked at the drive in my hand the way you look at something you're relieved to be rid of and ashamed of the relief.

"Lek was careful," she said. "Small handwriting, careful man. Whatever's on there, he thought it was worth dying — " she stopped herself, corrected the accusation the sentence was making without her permission — "worth being careful about. I don't know if that's the same thing. I used to think it was."

I walked home holding a plastic bag with a dead man's evidence in it and a phone with a video in it from a number that did not exist before 3:41 that morning, and somewhere between the coffee shop and my building I stopped believing these were two separate problems. I did not yet believe they were the same problem either. I was, for the length of that walk, in the honest middle where you can hold two facts without forcing them to explain each other, and it was the calmest I had felt in nine days.

At home I put the drive on the kitchen counter, next to the eggs, and looked at both for a while before I did anything about either.

Two objects. One a carton of things that are all, deliberately, identical. One a drive full of things Lek believed were not supposed to repeat and did anyway. I have spent a career being paid to notice when a pattern in a structure is load-bearing and when it is decoration. I could not, standing in my own kitchen at one in the afternoon, tell you which kind of pattern I was looking at. I put the kettle on instead, which is what I do when the honest answer is I don't know and the dishonest answer is anything at all.
