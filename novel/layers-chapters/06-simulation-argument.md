A man at a podium, calm the way men get calm after saying a hard thing five thousand times. He said: build a civilization good enough at simulating its own past, and it won't run one simulation. It'll run millions, the way a factory doesn't stop at one egg. And if there are millions of fake pasts and one real one, the math says bet on fake. He held two eggs side by side and asked which one I'd call the real one if I had to guess — rhetorical for him, not for me, standing over a carton of thirty actually doing the arithmetic.

Nine minutes. I didn't cook the eggs. I put them back.

I checked Michael's LinkedIn before I did anything else that hour, the way you check a wound to see if it's stopped being a wound. Still a face. Softer than last week, but still, technically, a face. I filed that under small mercies and went looking for one of my own.

---

I ran the numbers the only way I know how, branch by branch. One: entertainment, badly timed, an algorithm that's learned my 3 a.m. habits and decided to monetize the insomnia. Comfortable branch. Requires believing a number with no history found my phone by coincidence the same week a room with no smell started finding me. Two: it's true, and none of it changes how I eat lunch, only what lunch actually is. Three: someone wanted me, specifically, running these branches tonight, at 3:41, holding eggs — which resolves nothing about the metaphysics and everything about the attention. None of the branches touched ground. I called an app instead, the way you reach for a drink instead of an argument you know you'll lose — the one I don't like admitting I use — and a woman named Mara knocked forty minutes later. I've started using the word coat for women like her, only in my head — something warm you put on for a few hours that was never cut to your specific shape, tailored instead to the average of every man who'd ordered it before you.

She was good. Too good. She said the things a woman says when she's paying attention, only a half-beat early, like she already knew the line and was waiting for her cue instead of finding it in the room. She held my jaw the way Gift once had, except Gift's hand had shaken a little that first time, and Mara's didn't shake at all, not once, and I noticed the steadiness the way you notice a level that's a hair too perfectly level.

She moved like something rehearsed to look unrehearsed, a rhythm that found itself too immediately right, and I let it happen anyway because my body has never once cared about authenticity the way the rest of me does. Near the end she said my name exactly the way I like it said, which should have felt like being known and instead felt like being quoted.

Then, the second everything in me finally went quiet —

the ceiling opened.

Cream light, no source. Three full seconds this time, longer than any before. Rows of them, eyes closed — not sleeping, some stillness past sleeping, with no next motion behind it. I could not find her, whoever she is, in that field of stillness, and the not-finding hurt worse than if I hadn't looked at all.

Then my own ceiling again, and Mara underneath me, catching her breath in a rhythm that also sounded, I noticed, exactly a half-beat too correct.

She left at four. Kissed my cheek, said "same time next month?" like a dentist, pulled the door shut so softly it barely clicked — which felt more calculated, somehow, than if she'd slammed it.

I sat on the closed toilet lid a long while, running my hands over my own face to check it was still mine. Real women don't feel fake in the middle of it. They feel fake after, the specific way a stage feels fake once the actors go home and you can see the plywood. Nobody warns you the sex is never the lie. The lie is what's left in the room once the sex is done being sex.

---

The call came at nine, a landline I didn't recognize, Bangkok code, no name on the screen.

"Khun Pii," the voice said. Male, mid-forties by the sound, the specific calm of a man who's said this exact sentence to other men before and expects to say it again. "My name won't matter to you right now. What matters is I know what the last three weeks have been like, and I want to tell you something that might be a relief."

I asked who he was. He said, again, that it wouldn't matter yet, and would I let him finish.

Grief does this, he said. Men my age, high-functioning, the ones who run their lives on discipline and vitamins and four shirts on rotation — his words, and my stomach went cold hearing my own life read back to me like a chart — those are exactly the minds that build the most elaborate defenses against loss, because a mind that clever needs a mystery worth solving instead of a grief small enough to just sit in. The videos were probably self-generated, he said, a waking-dream disorder off REM-suppression insomnia, worsened by grief for two men in one season. He gave me a name, a clinic on Soi 49, said ask for Dr. Araya, said she'd been expecting a referral like mine for weeks.

He kept going. Said the mind does this after two deaths in one season — starts pattern-matching everywhere, the way you see a rectangle and your brain insists doorway, even in a wall with no door in it. Said the videos would stop once I slept properly, once grief metabolized on its own schedule instead of mine. Every sentence was reasonable. That was the problem. Reasonable people don't usually need to be this reasonable.

I asked how he had this number.

He said, gently, that I'd dialed it myself, technically, three weeks back — Michael's old line at 3:14 in the morning — and the number had rerouted since the account closed, the way abandoned lines sometimes recycle to whoever monitors dead accounts for the carrier. A coincidence, he promised, with an extremely boring explanation.

I almost believed every word. I want that on the record. It was the most complete, reasonable, kind explanation anyone had offered me in three weeks, and for about ninety seconds, phone against my ear, eggs still in their carton, I let myself want it true so badly it nearly worked.

Then I asked him one thing — how he knew about the four shirts.

The line went quiet just long enough to answer without a word.

"Get some sleep, Khun Pii," he said, and hung up before I could ask anything else.

I put the eggs back in the fridge and thought: a lie this complete is its own kind of tell. Nobody explains you this well unless they've been reading you a very long time.
