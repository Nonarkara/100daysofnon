# CH8 — Absurdism

## 2026 · Doc

Toon called on the fourth day, not the seventh, which I noted the way you note a doctor calling back sooner than scheduled — never good news, delivered early because someone decided you should hear it before you found out some other way.

"I didn't crack it," he said. "I want you to know I'm not being modest. I've got nothing. But I found something adjacent, and I don't know what to do with it, so I'm handing it to you the way it was handed to me." He sent a single frame, a screenshot of raw data rendered as grayscale noise, and in the noise, if you looked at it the way you look at a stereogram, letting your eyes go soft instead of sharp, there was a shape. A number. 2848, repeating, embedded in structure that should not have been able to hold a number at all, the way the ocean shouldn't be able to hold a word and sometimes, in a particular kind of dream, does.

"2848," I said out loud, to no one, and Toon, still on the line, asked if the number meant something to me. I told him no, which was a lie so small and so smooth I almost didn't notice myself telling it, the kind of lie you get good at only after you've had a great deal of recent practice, and we hung up, and I sat with the screenshot enlarged on my laptop for longer than the image deserved, tracing the false shape with my finger the way you trace a scar to confirm it's really there and not a trick of the light in the bathroom mirror.

I checked Michael before I did anything else that evening, which had become its own small ritual, the way you check a wound to see if it's finally stopped being a wound. His LinkedIn returned a page that said, plainly, in the flat gray text a platform uses for things it no longer has an obligation to explain: this profile is not available. Not deleted-looks-deleted. Available and not-available occupy different registers on that site, and I know the difference because I have spent three weeks learning it against my will. His Facebook photograph, the last time I'd checked it, four days prior, had been a smear the shape of a face. Tonight the URL redirected to a generic error, the kind that implies the account had simply never existed, which is a much colder sentence than the account existed and something happened to it. I sat on the floor of my own kitchen for a while, back against the cabinet with the four fish sauce bottles in it, and thought about the last message he ever sent me — good dinner, the ambassador knows more than he says, I'll tell you when we can talk properly — and understood, sitting there, that we were never going to talk properly, that the properly had been quietly revoked along with everything else, page by page, the way a lease expires without anyone needing to be cruel about it.

I put the phone face-down on the counter, and it lit up again nine minutes later with something that was not from Toon.

> ——  02:47
> [video attached]
> the second one asks a harder question than the first. the first one asked what you are. this one asks what you do about it either way.

My own clock said 21:52. I no longer bother pointing that out to myself as a discrepancy. It has become, these three weeks, simply the format the messages arrive in — one time on the phone, one time inside the message, and I have stopped expecting the two to reconcile.

It opened on a photograph of a man who died at forty-six in a car with an unused train ticket in his coat pocket, and a narrator's voice explaining, in the patient tone people use for ideas they've had to defend at dinner parties, that the man had spent his whole career on one problem: if the universe refuses to answer the only question worth asking — why any of this, why me, why now — and refuses with total, permanent silence, then the only remaining question is whether you keep asking anyway. Not because the universe will eventually answer. It won't. Because the asking, done with your eyes open, done without the comfort of pretending the silence is actually an answer in disguise, is the entire dignity available to a person in your position. He wrote it down properly once, in a book about a man who spends eternity rolling a rock up a hill for no reason except that it is his rock and his hill, and the video's last line, delivered flat, no music under it, was that you had to imagine the man happy. Not resigned. Happy. The rock is not the tragedy. Believing there should have been no rock is the tragedy.

I sat with it a long time after it ended. Camus — I'll use the name once, since the video used it twice and pretending otherwise would be its own kind of affectation — never claimed to know whether the silence was a wall or a closed door. He only refused to lie about which one he was looking at. I have spent nine days running trees that all terminate in a wall I cannot see past, and it had not once occurred to me, doing that, to ask whether the wall being unclimbable changed anything about how I should spend the hours in front of it.

Pom texted while I was still sitting with it. A photograph, no caption for a full minute, then one line following it up: *his mother asked me to send this to everyone who knew him.* The photograph was Lek's garden in Chiang Rai, unweeded three months now, the rosebushes he'd planted along the east fence gone leggy and half-strangled by something green and opportunistic that had moved in the moment nobody was watching closely. I remembered the earlier photo, the one from April, the same fence with the same bare canes and his caption underneath it — *เดี๋ยวมันจะสวย*, it'll be beautiful soon — and I understood, looking at the two photographs side by side on my screen, that I was looking at the exact shape of a promise nobody had been able to keep, made by a man who did not yet know he wouldn't be the one to keep it. I told Pom the garden was still beautiful, in its way, and she sent back a single line — *you always find the sentence that lets everyone off the hook* — which was not quite a compliment and not quite an accusation, and which I have not stopped thinking about since.

---

I went to Fah's that night instead of calling her to mine, which we had never done before, and she opened the door already reading something in my face that I hadn't said yet.

"You look like a man who just got told there's no explanation coming," she said.

"Close enough."

She let me in and didn't touch me right away, which was its own kind of decision, and she made tea, actual tea, in a pot, which she had never once done in eight months of whatever we had, and we sat on her narrow balcony with two cups going cold between us and she asked me to tell her the video, all of it, and I did, and when I got to the rock and the hill and being told to imagine the man happy, she was quiet for long enough that I thought she'd stopped listening.

"My mother used to say something like that about the shop," she said finally. Her family ran a noodle stall in Nonthaburi for thirty years before the highway widening took the corner it sat on. "Same broth every morning at four. Same argument with the fish supplier every Tuesday. She used to say, why would you want it to mean something more than it does. It's already enough work being just what it is."

Something in how she said it undid a latch in me that the video alone hadn't managed. She saw it happen — I watched her watch it happen, the small recalibration behind her eyes, the decision arriving and being made before I'd said a word — and she stood first, held out one hand, and led rather than followed, which she had never once done in eight months of a dynamic she had built specifically so that leading would never be asked of her twice. I understood, following her off the balcony, that this was its own negotiation, conducted in silence, and that saying anything at all would have broken it before it started.

When we finally moved from the balcony to the bed it was not the way it had been the first time — not a push toward an edge, not an instrument aimed at a glimpse. She set the pace and kept it slow on purpose, testing, the way she tests everything, whether slow was a thing I could survive without turning it into a performance of surviving it. Her hand stayed flat against my chest the whole time, keeping time the way I usually do, and I let her, which was its own kind of surrender I hadn't planned on offering anyone that week. When she finally let herself go still on top of me, forehead against my collarbone, she stayed there a long moment before either of us moved again, which she had never done, which undid something in her too, I think, though she would probably deny it if I asked, and I did not ask.

It came anyway — the glimpse, unbidden, without the chasing, without the risk we'd taken on purpose in my kitchen the week before. This time it did not arrive as a room. It arrived as a woman. Chair, white flower held in two open palms, one petal already fallen loose onto her knee, and her face — for the fraction of a second the render allowed me, no longer than the last one, maybe shorter — was a face I had seen before, on a woman on a bicycle who had stopped under my building at 2:47 in the morning some weeks back and looked up at my window for forty-one seconds before riding south without looking back. I had told myself, at the time, that the dark glass meant she couldn't have seen me. I had never let myself finish the other half of that thought, which was that I had absolutely seen her.

A sound came out of me that wasn't a word yet, just the shape a name makes in the mouth before the mouth has been given one to use, and Fah lifted her head off my chest and looked at me with an expression that had nothing accusing in it, only a kind of tired recognition, the look of someone confirming a suspicion she'd been sitting on since the balcony.

"There's someone else," she said. Not a question.

"Not the way you mean."

"I know what I mean," she said, and pulled the sheet up over both of us instead of leaving, which surprised me more than anything else that night. "I didn't say leave. I said there's someone else. Different sentence." She was quiet a moment, tracing a line down my sternum with one finger, the same gesture from days ago repurposed into something gentler. "You've been chasing a ghost since I met you and calling it a room. I don't mind being the door you keep opening to get to her, as long as you know that's what's happening. Just don't tell me it's about the room anymore. We're past the room."

I didn't answer, because there was nothing honest available that wasn't also cruel, and she didn't ask again, and we lay there in the dark with the ceiling fan clicking on every third rotation the way it had clicked for eight months, and at some point past midnight, without any warning that I could locate afterward, I started crying — not from the glimpse, not from Fah, but from a place further back that had apparently been waiting for an unguarded door. It came for Lek first, his small careful handwriting, the two photographs of the same fence six months apart, the promise and the thing that happened to the promise instead. Then it came for Michael, whose face on every platform I have checked is now closer to a rumor of a face than a face, gone tonight past even that, to the flat gray sentence a website uses when it no longer owes anyone an explanation. I had not cried at either funeral. I had stood at the back of both, counted the mourners, noted the smoke, filed the grief under manageable the way I file everything, and it had been waiting the whole time in the one room I hadn't checked.

Fah didn't say anything and didn't ask anything. She just put her hand flat on my sternum again, the checking gesture, and left it there until it stopped, which took longer than I would have guessed and ended sooner than I wanted. Somewhere in the middle of it she said, very quietly, not to comfort me so much as to mark the moment as real: "There it is." Nothing more. As if she'd been waiting for it on my behalf longer than I had.

---

In the morning she made two eggs for both of us without asking how I take mine, and got it exactly right, which she could not have known unless she'd been paying the kind of attention I have only ever associated with one direction.

"How did you know two, not three," I asked, meaning it as a joke and hearing, as I said it, that it wasn't one.

"You're not a three-egg man," she said, plating them. "I've watched you eat six mornings out of eight months. You count everything. I just counted you back." She set the plate down and did not sit across from me, sitting instead beside me, shoulder against my shoulder, which was new. "I'm not the ghost," she said, not looking at me, looking at the eggs. "I know that. I've known it since the balcony. I stayed anyway, because whatever's out there watching you is patient enough that I figure I've got time to decide whether I mind, and this morning, this exact morning, I don't." She picked up her fork. "Ask me again in a month."

I walked home along Sukhumvit with the sun already flattening the shadows to nothing, past the noodle cart, past the court where the kid from last week was back on his one square of concrete, missing his first four and setting his feet again in exactly the same place without complaint, the way you do a thing you've decided is worth doing badly for as long as it takes to do it well. I thought about a man imagining another man happy on a hill with a rock that would roll back down every single time, forever, by design, and I thought that the video had gotten one thing slightly wrong, or at least incomplete: the rock is not absurd because it rolls back down. It's absurd because somewhere, someone built the hill exactly steep enough that it always would, and never told him why, and he pushes anyway, and somewhere in the pushing — not at the top, there is no top — he had found the only kind of dignity that was ever actually on offer.

I thought about Lek's garden, which someone would have to finish or let go wild, and about the four-o'clock name I hadn't asked about, and about a woman on a bicycle who had a face now, briefly, a face I hadn't earned and couldn't keep, and how strange it was that fear had been my first and most reliable response to almost everything this month except that face, which had produced instead something closer to recognition, the way you feel seeing a childhood street corner years later, before you can name the street. I understood, walking, that I had stopped asking whether any of this was real somewhere between the tea going cold and the crying I hadn't scheduled. The question that replaced it was smaller and harder to hold: given that I might be a rock someone else pushes, was I pushing back with anything like the honesty the pushing deserved. I did not have an answer. I bought a coconut from the cart anyway, drank it standing on the corner in the flat morning light, straw bent at the angle it always bends, and let that be the whole of the answer for one day, which Camus, dead in his car with an unused ticket, would probably have allowed me.

Hedonic Homeostasis is the terminal state of an optimization process that succeeds completely. In the decades preceding the Hub's construction, automated systems eliminated, in sequence: infectious disease, chronic pain, involuntary hunger, the majority of accidental death, and — more slowly, more totally — the unpredictability of romantic and social outcome, through matching systems so refined that disappointment in that domain approached zero. Each elimination was celebrated as a victory, correctly, in isolation. Their sum was not anticipated.

What the sum produced was a species that had lost access to Authentic Inconvenience — friction that has not been engineered, resolved, or pre-approved. Every remaining hardship had been softened at the source, which meant every remaining hardship had also been drained of the one property that gave hardship its function: the plausible chance of actual failure, absorbed and metabolized by a body built across a much longer and much crueler evolutionary window to expect it. A species assured, correctly, of its own safety at every scale, ceased over roughly two generations to generate the specific chemistry of caring whether it continued.

This is what the archive's literature calls the collapse of the Scaffolding Instincts — the biological and behavioral mechanisms that hold a person oriented toward the future: the need to be needed, the drive to protect something more fragile than yourself, the appetite that requires scarcity to remain an appetite at all. These instincts are not decorative. They are structural, in the same sense a keel is structural to a ship that will otherwise capsize the first time the water moves. Removing the water does not save the ship. It removes the reason the keel, or the ship, or the voyage, existed in the first place.

The surface population's decline was not experienced, at the time, as loss. Surface accounts from the transition decades describe the withdrawal in the language of relief: fewer arguments worth having, fewer risks worth taking, fewer people worth missing enough to disrupt a schedule for. Reproductive rate fell first, then social density, then — the metric the Hub's founding charter cites most often — what the charter calls attention span for another person's specific suffering, which fell fastest of all and never recovered. A species that no longer finds another's specific suffering interesting has not become cruel. It has become unable to locate the door that suffering used to open, in earlier eras, onto meaning. The Hub's entire justification rests on the wager that the door still exists, and that friction, applied honestly and without mercy, is the only known key.
