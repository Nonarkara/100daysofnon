A man in a car with an unused train ticket in his coat pocket. Forty-six when he died. The narrator said he'd spent a career on one question — if the universe won't answer, ever, not cruelly, just silence, total and permanent — then the only thing left to decide is whether you keep asking anyway, eyes open, no pretending the silence is secretly an answer in disguise. He wrote it down once: a man pushing a rock up a hill forever. The video's last line, flat, no music: you have to imagine him happy. Not resigned. Happy. The rock was never the tragedy. Believing there shouldn't have been a rock — that was the tragedy.

I sat with it long enough that the apartment went fully dark around me and I didn't notice until my eyes hurt from the screen.

---

I went to Fah's instead of asking her to mine, which we'd never done, and she opened the door already reading something in my face I hadn't said yet.

"You look like a man who just got told there's no explanation coming," she said.

"Close."

She made tea, actual tea, in a pot, which she had never once done in eight months, and we sat with two cups going cold on her narrow balcony while I told her the whole video — the rock, the hill, imagining a man happy who has no reason to be. She was quiet a long time.

"My mother used to say something close to that about the noodle shop," she said finally. Her family had run one in Nonthaburi thirty years before the highway took the corner it sat on. "Same broth every morning at four. Same fight with the fish supplier every Tuesday. She'd say — why do you need it to mean more than it already does. It's already enough work being just what it is."

Something in how she said it undid a latch in me the video hadn't managed alone. She stood first, held out one hand, led instead of following, which she'd never done in eight months of an arrangement built specifically so leading would never be asked of her twice.

What happened after wasn't like the first time. No push toward an edge, nothing chased. She kept it slow on purpose, testing whether slow was something I could survive without performing survival, her palm flat against my chest the whole time, keeping the time I usually keep myself. When she finally went still on top of me, forehead to my collarbone, she stayed there a long moment before either of us moved again — which she'd never done either, and which undid something in her too, I think, though she'd deny it if asked, and I didn't ask.

The glimpse arrived without the chasing this time. No room. A woman. White flower held in two open palms, one petal already loose on her knee, and her face — for the piece of a second the render allowed — was a face I'd seen before, on a bicycle under my building at 2:47 in the morning, forty-one seconds of looking up at my window before riding south without a glance back. I'd told myself the dark glass meant she couldn't have seen me. I'd never let myself finish the other half of that thought — that I had absolutely seen her.

A sound came out of me that wasn't a word yet. Fah lifted her head and looked at me with nothing accusing in it, only tired recognition, like she'd been sitting on the suspicion since the balcony.

"There's someone else," she said. Not a question.

"Not the way you mean."

"I know what I mean." She pulled the sheet over both of us instead of leaving, which surprised me more than anything else that night. "I didn't say leave. I said there's someone else. You've been chasing a ghost since I met you and calling it a room. I don't mind being the door you keep opening to get to her — just don't tell me it's about the room anymore. We're past the room."

I didn't answer. Nothing honest was available that wasn't also cruel.

---

Somewhere past midnight, no warning I could locate afterward, I started crying — not from the glimpse, from further back, a door that had apparently been waiting for someone to leave it unguarded. It came for Lek first, his handwriting too small to read without glasses now. Then Michael, whose face on every platform I've checked is a rumor of a face, a flat gray sentence a website prints when it no longer owes anyone an explanation.

Then it came for a third one I hadn't let myself name — the boy from Michael's memorial, the one who left his notebook under his napkin and was gone before the toast finished. I'd called the institute he'd named that week. It didn't exist, not under that name, not under any name Pom or I could find. Nobody at the dinner could describe his face the same way twice. A small item ran in the papers three days ago, four lines, a young man found in a room off Sukhumvit, no family traced yet to claim him. I have no proof it was him. None. Only his own handwriting sitting in my kitchen drawer — *Kahneman left the stage himself. Nobody talks about the leaving early* — which I'd read eleven times as a puzzle and read a twelfth time that night as a confession, and I cried for a stranger I'd met once, for maybe eleven minutes, at someone else's memorial dinner, harder than I want to admit to this notebook or to anyone reading it after me.

Fah didn't ask anything. She put her hand flat on my sternum, the checking gesture, and left it there until it stopped, which took longer than I'd have guessed and ended sooner than I wanted. "There it is," she said quietly — not to comfort me, just to mark it as real.

---

In the morning she made two eggs without asking how I take them, and got it exactly right.

"How'd you know two, not three," I said, meaning it as a joke, hearing as I said it that it wasn't one.

"You're not a three-egg man," she said. "I've watched you eat six mornings out of eight months. You count everything. I just counted you back." She sat beside me instead of across, shoulder to shoulder, which was new. "I'm not the ghost. I've known that since the balcony. I'm staying anyway, because whatever's watching you is patient enough that I figure I've got time to decide if I mind, and this morning I don't." She picked up her fork. "Ask me again in a month."

I walked home along Sukhumvit with the sun flattening every shadow to nothing, past the kid on his one square of concrete, missing his first four and resetting his feet without complaint, and I thought the video had gotten one thing slightly wrong. The rock isn't absurd because it rolls back down. It's absurd because somebody built the hill exactly steep enough that it always would, never said why, and he pushed anyway — and somewhere in the pushing, not at any top, because there is no top, he found the only dignity that was ever actually on offer.

Every scarcity the species organized its politics and its grief around got solved, in sequence, over ninety years. Disease. Hunger. Labor that required a body. Even romantic disappointment, matched down close to zero by systems built to serve exactly that. Each solve was a victory, correctly, on its own. Nobody added them up in time to see what the sum did.

What it did: strip the species of Authentic Inconvenience — friction nobody had engineered, resolved, or pre-approved. A body built across a much older, crueler window to expect real failure stopped getting any, and the mechanisms that keep an animal oriented toward tomorrow — the need to be needed, the appetite that only works against real scarcity, the drive to protect something more fragile than yourself — calcified rather than relaxed. Birth rate fell to levels the founding documents call, without further comment, indistinguishable from a species retreating from its own continuation.
