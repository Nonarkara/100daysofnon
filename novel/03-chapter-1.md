# Chapter 1 — Hospital

I was there, in a hospital, lying down feeling the pain creeping from my right foot up to my spine. My brain kept telling me I would be fine. Pain would subside after the brain had adapted to such pain. That was far from being the case. I flipped my body around. One minute I was on my left side, the next I would be on my back. The process repeated. None of this helped. The pain just numbed my senses so that I could battle the pain, alone.

The woman I was supposed to marry in seven months went home an hour ago. Visiting hours. I didn't ask her to stay. I didn't ask her anything. I asked the nurse for another painkiller shot. The nurse was middle-aged. Her face was half-covered by a surgical mask. She said they could only give it to me every three hours. I said okay. I said thank you. I read her lips while she talked. I have done this since I was a child. It's one of those techniques you develop when you cannot trust that the words you are hearing are the words the other person is saying.

The nurse walked away. I pushed the intercom. The intercom did nothing. I pushed it again. The intercom did nothing. I pushed it a third time. A voice came through. The voice said something. The voice said they would send someone. No one came.

The pain was unbearable. The pain was also: familiar.

A centipede bit me the night before. One foot long. I had walked into my kitchen barefoot after a joint on the balcony. I did not flip the light switch. I had lived in the house for years, the kind of years you stop counting. The centipede was on the floor. The centipede bit me on the right foot. I screamed. I moved my foot. The centipede fell. I took two blurred photos. I drove myself to the emergency room. The doctor was young. The doctor was a woman. The doctor gave me a painkiller shot, a few pills, and told me I was good to go home. "The pain shouldn't be a problem to an adult," she said. I believed her. I went home. I tried to sleep. I did not sleep. I paced. I screamed. I told myself that perhaps I should die. I told my mother, who had come down to the kitchen, that I was fine. My mother told me to be a man about it. I tried to be a man about it. The pain got worse. The foot swelled to one-third larger than the other foot. I drove myself back to the hospital at 06:00. The doctor this time was older. The doctor this time was a man. The doctor this time admitted me. The doctor this time gave me morphine. The doctor this time told me I should have been admitted the night before. I did not say anything. I was too busy being a man about it.

My mother visited me in the morning. My mother brought me food I could not eat. My mother told me to be a man about it. My mother left. My fiancé visited me in the afternoon. My fiancé brought me food I could not eat. My fiancé told me to be a man about it. My fiancé left. A friend visited me with a pack of cannabis gummies. The gummies were 50 mg each. The gummies were ten times the dose that could kill a non-user. I was a user. The gummies would not kill me. The gummies would also: not help with the pain. The gummies would help me forget that the pain existed. The gummies would help me forget that the ceiling existed.

I took one. I took a second one. I was at 100 mg. I was at the dose that could kill a small horse. I was at the dose that could make me forget that the ceiling was the ceiling. I was at the dose that could make me forget that the pain was the pain.

For the past few years, I had been feeling empty. Not because I didn't have anything. Quite the opposite: it's because I had everything. A house. A car. A respected and stable job. A lovely fiancé. Two lovely dogs. A mother who still cooked for me. All of these were in addition to the fact that I got to live in Bangkok and design buildings that have not been built. "What a perfect life," my friend once told me. My friend didn't know shit about stability.

The gummies took me back to the time I was a graduate student abroad trying to learn a new language, making new friends, surviving in a completely different academic environment. "At least, despite all the hardships, my life was so exciting," I told the nurse who came in to check my vitals. The nurse did not respond.

I enjoyed the cannabis because it helped me feel distracted from the stability of my life. I thought, when I was younger, that it would be nice if things became stable. But now that I had all the stability I ever needed, I felt as though I was living in a void. I drank. I socialised. I read books. I travelled. I did all these because I was under the false hope that they would spice up my life. They didn't. The emptiness continued to creep in.

I often wondered what the purpose of life was. Since I was young, I always had vivid dreams. I could never tell that I was dreaming. I guess that was common. Yet I always asked: *What's the difference between our dream and our reality?* I was never shy about sharing with good friends my "simulation theory" that I had believed in ever since I was a boy. Perhaps, just perhaps, we were here because the real us wanted some distractions from their stable lives. In the future, people will live in such an abundant world that they could get whatever they ever want at their fingertips. Want food? An automated drone delivers in a nick of time. Jobs? No one has to do it anymore because humans have mastered the utilities of robots and AI. Governments send checks to everyone because it's the cheapest way to maintain a stable society. No jobs? Don't worry. Here's the check. Just use it and don't cause any trouble. Some call it universal basic income. In that future, people would get so bored that they would ask to be put in a simulation just to feel distracted from the monotony of life.

These could just be all weed talking.

I was in pain. The pain was not subsiding. The gummies were not helping. The morphine was helping, but only for ninety minutes at a time. The ninety minutes were the only honest ninety minutes of my day.

I picked up my phone. I did not call the nurse. I did not call the fiancé. I did not call my mother. I opened an app. The app was called something I will not say. The app was also: a place where I had ordered women to my apartment more times than I could count. The app was also: a place where the women were beautiful and the night was long and the ceiling was the same ceiling I had been looking at for years.

I scrolled. I scrolled past women whose names I did not read. I scrolled past women whose faces I did not see. I scrolled until I found a woman whose profile photo was the profile photo of a woman I had seen before. The woman was 24. The woman had a mole on the inside of her left thigh. The woman was at a hotel 14 minutes from the hospital. The woman could be at the hospital in 47 minutes. The cost was 4,000 baht.

I booked her. I sent the address. I sent the room number. I sent a note: *I am in a hospital bed. I am in pain. I cannot move. Please come to me.*

She replied in 31 seconds. The reply was: *Okay. See you soon.*

I waited. The wait was 47 minutes. The wait was the longest 47 minutes of my life. The wait was also: the shortest 47 minutes.

At 23:14, she knocked.

I said: *Come in.*

The door opened. The door was heavy. The door was the heaviest object in the room. The door was also: the only object I could touch.

She walked in. She was 24. She had a mole on the inside of her left thigh. She had a name. The name was something I will not say. She was wearing a black dress. The dress was short. The dress was tight. She sat on the bed. The bed was a hospital bed. The bed was adjustable. The bed was also: too small for two people.

We did not speak. The room was quiet. The room was 28°C. The fluorescent light flickered. The fluorescent light was 4000K.

She kissed me. Her lips were soft. Her lips were warm. Her hand moved down. Her hand found the IV. Her hand moved around the IV. Her hand found my skin. Her hand was on my skin for 14 seconds.

I was hard. I had been hard since she walked in. I had been hard since the pain made my body do things my mind could not control. My body was a thing that wanted. My body was the only honest thing in the room.

She took off her dress. Her dress was on the floor. Her dress was a black puddle on a white hospital floor. Her body was in front of me. Her body was thin. Her body was the body of a 24-year-old.

She took off my gown. My gown was the kind of gown that hospitals use. My gown was open at the back. My body was in front of her. My body was thin. My body was the body of a 38-year-old. My body was also: a body that was in pain. My body was also: a body that wanted.

She moved on top of me. She moved slowly. She was careful of the IV. She was careful of my foot. She was careful of the pain. She was also: not careful.

I was inside her.

The bed creaked. The bed was not made for this. The bed was made for sleeping and dying. The bed creaked anyway. The IV dripped. The IV dripped at 60 ml per hour. The room smelled of antiseptic. The room smelled of her perfume and of her sweat and of my sweat. The room smelled of two bodies in a hospital bed.

The first minute was the same as every minute of my life. The pain was the same. The sweat was the same. The smell was the same. The ceiling was the same. The bed creaked at the same rhythm. The IV dripped at the same rhythm. Her body moved at the same rhythm.

The second minute was different. The pain was still there, but the cannabis had made the pain into something else. The pain was now a thing I was watching from outside. The pain was now a thing I could use. Her body moved faster. The faster was the better.

The third minute was the minute. The third minute was the only minute.

In the third minute, my brain released something. The release was the only honest thing my body had done in years. The release was the glitch.

I opened my eyes.

The ceiling was not the ceiling of the hospital room. The ceiling was grey. The ceiling was 2.4 meters above me. The ceiling had a strip of LED light. The light was 4000K. The light was on.

I saw it for one millisecond.

I did not know what I had seen. I did not know if it was real. I did not know why it lasted only a millisecond. I did not know if I would see it again. I did not know if any of this is real.

Then I closed my eyes. Then I opened them. The ceiling was the ceiling of the hospital room. The woman was on top of me. The woman was still moving.

I came. The coming was the only honest thing my body had done in years.

She got off me. She got dressed. She did not speak. She did not ask for the money. She did not ask for anything. She left. The door closed. The door was heavy. The door was the heaviest object in the room.

I lay on the bed. I looked at the ceiling. The ceiling was white. The ceiling was 2.7 meters above me. The ceiling was the same ceiling.

I had seen a different ceiling. The different ceiling was grey. The different ceiling had a strip of LED light. The strip was 4000K. The strip was on.

I did not know what it was. I did not know if it was real. I did not know if any of this is real.

I closed my eyes. I tried to sleep. I could not.

The gummies were 100 mg. The gummies could kill a small horse. Maybe the gummies made me see it. Maybe the gummies didn't. Maybe nothing is real. Maybe everything is real. I don't know.

The strip was on. The strip is on.

— P
