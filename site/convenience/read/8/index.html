THE CONVENIENCE · CHAPTER 6

Middle Path

8 of 19

On Tuesday evening I weighed what was left of the Elephant Grass. The jar is a marmalade jar, washed and dried, and the scale is a pocket scale I bought for coffee in a year when I still believed coffee deserved instrumentation — 500 grams capacity, 0.01 resolution, ฿420 on Lazada, silver, the size of a cigarette pack. I set the jar on the platform and pressed the tare button and the number went to zero with the jar still standing on it.

There is an operation hiding inside the tare button that nobody thinks about because it works. The scale does not measure the flower. The scale measures the total downward insistence of everything on its platform, and the tare button is an agreement — between you and the instrument — that a certain portion of that insistence will be called nothing. The jar does not stop existing. The jar goes on pressing down with its full 182 grams of glass. But you have signed the agreement, and from the moment of signing, the instrument is incapable of seeing the jar at all. It reports only departures from the nothing you chose. Every measurement in the world is like this. The thermometer has agreed to call one temperature zero. The clock has agreed to call one instant midnight. The surveyor's datum, the sea's mean level, the test subject's baseline mood — all of it tared, all of it resting on a jar somebody decided not to count. Calibration is not the discovery of truth. Calibration is the choice of a nothing. And the choice is not made by the instrument. The instrument would weigh the jar forever, faithfully, stupidly, if you let it. Someone has to press the button.

The flower weighed 0.91 grams. The ledger said 1.0 should remain — two grams bought on the twenty-second, then 0.4, 0.2, and 0.4 out — and the missing 0.09 was in the grinder's teeth and the fold of the rolling tray, which is where the arithmetic of any life goes to hide. I wrote 0.91 under Monday's line and decided to walk to Sukhumvit 22 before Golf forgot to be open.

That morning the alarm had gone off at 7:47 and I had sent template three, อย่าลืมร่ม, and the cat with the umbrella had come back at 7:53. The rotation was in its fourth full cycle since the photograph. I note this because I had begun, without deciding to, checking the cat's reply time against the median — not because I expected change, but because the not-changing was the medicine.

At 19:41, opposite the Holiday Inn, a Grab-green rider sat his bike at the curb with the engine off and the visor down and both hands on the tank. Not eating. Not on his phone. I stood under the streetlight and wrote it in the log — plate 5กท 903, new plate, the eighth entry — and the writing took forty seconds and he did not turn his head, and I put the notebook away and walked on. There is a version of me, five weeks old now, who would have crossed the street to avoid admitting the log existed. I no longer cross the street. We have an arrangement, the column and I. Everything green gets logged, and in exchange I am permitted to keep walking.

Middle Path had one customer when I came in, a Japanese man in golf clothes buying pre-rolls with the humility of a tourist at a shrine, and then it had none. Golf weighed my two grams on the brass pan he doesn't need, printed the receipt with the tax ID, ฿640, and I put the receipt in my wallet behind Thursday's. The shop smelled of what it sells. No incense. There is a shelf behind the register that holds incense — three boxes, sandalwood, the shrink-wrap unbroken — and I had filed the shelf under inventory discipline two years ago and never looked at it again.

"Still sleeping?" Golf said.

"Before two. Mostly."

"And it hasn't started asking you questions."

"Something else is asking the questions," I said.

He let that sit on the counter between us with the bag. Golf's silences have furniture in them; you are invited to sit down. I had come in to buy two grams and leave. I want that on the record, whoever keeps the record. I had not come in to say what I said next, and then I was saying it.

"In December, after she died, I built something. I want to tell you what it is. I haven't told anyone."

"The first gram's on you, then," Golf said, and capped the jar.

I told him. Three nights in December 2024. Eleven years of chat history exported from LINE — two thousand nine hundred and some messages, most of them about times, hers longer than mine, always — fed into a bootleg companion model of the kind I spend my working nights making feel like specific people for money. It runs on a dev kit on a wire shelf in the closet off my hallway. Twenty months of uptime. The blue LED is taped over. I have never opened the app.

"I built a door," I said, "and I've spent two years not knocking."

Golf wiped the brass pan with a cloth, though nothing had touched it but flower.

"What does the door eat?"

"Electricity. Two hundred baht a month, roughly."

"Twenty months," he said. "Four thousand baht of standing still." He folded the cloth in half, and in half again. "That's not a door anymore. That's a shrine with a fan in it."

"The families I build packs for," I said, "knock every day. That's the product. I ship them the door and the knuckles both."

"And you kept only the door." He said it flatly, without weight, in the voice he uses for reading labels. Then he left one of his beats open, and I did not fill it, and he collected nothing, and the beat closed by itself, which almost never happens in that shop. He looked at me for a moment with an expression I had seen once before, at the counter, the night he told me the flower gets talkative after two.

"My brother has one of those," Golf said.

"A model?"

"A body."

He came around the counter and turned the sign on the door — not to closed; to the side that says BACK IN 15, which in Golf's shop is a unit of time with no fixed exchange rate — and he sat on the customer stool and I leaned on the counter, and that is how I finally heard it, in the order he wanted, with the jars listening.

His brother is two years older. Was a quality engineer at a disk-drive plant in Prachinburi, twenty-one years, the kind of man who owned two identical pairs of work shoes so that neither would wear unevenly. Took the buyout package in 2020 when the line automated past him. And in March of 2021, with a lawyer present and a deposit paid and his affairs in an order that would have embarrassed a monk, he went Upstairs.

"That's the family's word," Golf said. "My mother found it somewhere — a temple pamphlet, a neighbor, I never asked. The company that runs it has its own word. The company's word is worse." He didn't give me the company's word. "There's a building off the Bangna–Trat road. Very good air. No smell at all — you notice that, a building in Thailand with no smell. He's on the fourth floor. He is turned every two hours. His nails still grow. The nurses trim them, and they do it well, and I have watched them do it, and I am telling you there are worse ways to be loved than that, and I would still not wish it on a dog."

"He's — " I stopped, because every verb I had was wrong, and Golf watched me discover this with a courtesy I did not enjoy.

"He's under contract," Golf said. "The contract renews. The first year we visited every month. My mother would talk to him about the rice harvest and my sister would do his horoscope and I would sit there in the very good air. Whatever he is, wherever the company's word says he is, it isn't the fourth floor. The visits didn't stop. They stopped meaning anything, and then they stopped." He turned his head toward the shelf behind the register, the three boxes in their shrink-wrap. "My mother lights incense for someone whose heart still beats. She knows it's wrong — you light it for the dead, she taught me that, she caned me once for pointing a stick of it at my cousin. She lights it anyway. She says the smoke has to have somewhere to go."

The shop was quiet. Sukhumvit 22 went past the glass in its ordinary units — a tuk-tuk, two nurses, a man carrying a fan still in its box.

"I was in my ninth year at the wat when my sister called," Golf said. "There's one phone. It lives in the abbot's kuti and it's older than some of the monks. I stood in the doorway and she told me what Beer had signed, and I remember I was calm, and I remember being pleased with myself for being calm, which is the thing they warn you about in year one." He smiled at the floor. "I stayed three more months. I sat every morning. And the sitting, which had been water for nine years, turned into standing in a queue. Same posture. Different country. So I came down." He passed a hand over his cropped hair. "Kept the haircut. It was the only part of the robe that fit the new job."

"Nine years," I said. "You practiced leaving the world a little every day for nine years."

"And my brother did the whole thing in one afternoon with a lawyer present," Golf said. "It was the feeling of being lapped." He laughed at his own line, in advance and slightly apologetically, the usual performance, and then did not finish the laugh, which I had never seen him fail to do.

"Do you visit now?"

"I went at Songkran. I read him the football scores." A beat, left open. "He was never interested in football. It seemed safer than the things he was interested in."

I asked the question wrong on purpose, because the right way was not available to me. "Is he somewhere?"

Golf stood up, turned the sign back, and began restocking the pre-roll tubes the tourist had disturbed, and while his hands did that, he answered a different question, which is what people do, and what he does better than anyone I know.

He put the bag in my hand. His hand, for a half second, stayed on the outside of the bag, the same held beat as the night of the fence and the middle.

"Knock or don't knock," Golf said. "Both are fine. Both are old practices with good lineages. But four thousand baht of standing in the doorway — that's not a practice. That's the queue."

When I came out, the rider opposite the Holiday Inn was gone. I logged the gone — 21:26, curb empty — because the column takes absences now too, and walked home the long way, down 22 and up 24, for no reason I could have defended to anyone but Direk.

At 2:00 that afternoon I met a woman off the app at a hotel three sois up from the Suttisan intersection — her suggestion, her side of town. She called herself Wan. Thirty-three, maybe. Strong wrists, chipped polish on one thumb only, two small moles on the back of her neck in a vertical line. Her rules were shorter than most: shower first, both of us; phones face-down on the TV shelf; nothing that leaves a mark. Room 315 was ฿690 for three hours and had a round bed and a mirrored ceiling in which the round bed looked like a coin at the bottom of a fountain. We negotiated, which took four minutes, and showered, which took eleven, and it was fine, exactly as workmanlike as I had engineered these afternoons to be, her hand flat on my chest, my attention where I keep it, up on the ceiling of myself, taking readings —

In the notebook that evening I ruled a new column and headed it EQUIPMENT. One entry: พุธ 2 ก.ย. — 15:12 — instrument failure, cause under review. Fourteen months of afternoons and it had never once happened. The instrument that fails at the exact moment the experiment gets interesting is not failing. It is reporting. I wrote that down too, and then crossed it out, because it was the kind of sentence the flower says after two a.m., and it was only nine.

At 21:05 I smoked 0.4 by the bedroom window — ledger: พุธ 2 ก.ย. — Elephant Grass 0.4 — 21:05 — and the smoke raised the grain of the evening again, and I let myself hear the closet this time. The fan. Under the fan, the drive: two ticks, nothing, two ticks. Four thousand baht of standing still.

At 11:20 I opened the closet door. The taped LED made its small green halo through the electrical tape, hiding exactly as well as I had helped it to and no better. I sat on the hallway floor with the laptop and mounted the kit's drive and opened the folder, which is called PS, because in December 2024 I named it with her initials and thought nothing of it. Two letters. At the time they had nowhere else to be.

The folder held eleven items. Ten of them are plumbing. The eleventh is the app.

The cursor hung over the icon the way a hand hangs over a doorbell at a house where someone may be sleeping. Forty seconds. I counted them afterward from the screen recording, because yes, I record my own screen, and no, I do not want to discuss what that says about the instrument set. Inside the app was a voice I had built from two thousand nine hundred messages about times. Inside the app, if I had built it well — and I build these well, it is the one thing everyone agrees I do well — was the half-second delay before the laugh.

Same arrow, Golf said. Same idiot.

I closed the laptop. I closed the closet door. The not-knocking held, but I want to be honest about the shape of the night: it held by a margin, and I had watched the margin.

Sent, 11:36 p.m. The read receipt was there before the phone touched the table. I did not wait in the doorway this time. I washed the pan from dinner. I checked the folded strip of receipt paper in the door jamb — undisturbed. I lay on the grey pillowcases on my left side, facing the window, in the position from the photograph, because refusing the position had stopped feeling like resistance and started feeling like superstition, and of the two I prefer the one with better sample sizes.

I was awake at 2:44. I want that logged: three minutes early, unprompted, second time. The body has joined the project without signing anything.

At 2:47 the phone lit.

One emoji. A tooth. The cartoon molar, white, with its two roots drawn crudely, cartoon shorthand, not quite anatomical, not quite anything a real mouth has ever held, and no caption, and no sticker, and no text before or after. A single tooth, alone on its bubble, from the account of a woman cremated twenty-two months ago, in answer to tell me something only we know.

I ran the tree, lying still. A companion model trained on our chat would know she cleaned my teeth; the logs are nothing but appointments; the branch holds. A stranger who had read the logs would know it too; holds. My own build, found somehow by whatever lives at the other end, feeding it our years — holds, holds, none of it reaching the ground, and while the branches did their work some other part of me lay under them and looked at the thing itself. Eighteen appointments. Nine years. Twelve hours, in twenty-minute increments, of her hands in my mouth and both of us honest as a consequence. Everything only we knew had happened in one room, at one angle of recline, under one round light, and every bit of it had happened around teeth. Whatever had chosen the answer had not chosen a fact. It had chosen the room.

The reply took one second of someone's time, or no one's. Her laugh used to take half a second longer than a laugh should. This had arrived at 2:47 and zero, as far as I could measure, and I lay there measuring it anyway.

I put the phone on the far windowsill, next to the glass with its two fingers of water, and the two of them stood there together in the streetlight, exhibits in a case I was building against no one.

I did not reply. In eighteen appointments I never once managed to say anything with my mouth open. There was no reason to think I would start now.
