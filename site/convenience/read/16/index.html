On Monday night I cleaned the foam pads of the floor headphones with alcohol, the way the laminated card above the sink instructs, and worked my queue through the crack in my right lens. The crack had developed a geography by then — a main fracture running southeast from the impact point, with one long tributary through the exact region of glass where a clip's subtitles live. I had learned to work around it the way a tongue learns to live around a broken tooth. Sixty-eight clips at eighty-four percent. The wall counter read 14,987 when I signed in and 15,019 when I signed out, which is the counter's idea of progress.

At three in the morning Sombat stopped behind row three with his coffee and said, to the space above our heads, "Wednesday. A man comes from head office."

"For what?" This was Daeng, without turning.

"Consolidation." He used the English word, the way the client emails used it, with a pencil audible underneath. "The legacy jobs go to Singapore. Row three loses its heater."

Everyone in row three called seat 14 the heater. It was the only warmth in the row that management had never billed anyone for. Sombat looked at me on his way back to his desk by the aluminum door and said, "You'll stay near him. Your English works nights too," and that was the entire briefing.

Tuesday I slept, sent nothing to anyone I wasn't contracted to, and logged one control-group rider at the mouth of my soi — visor up, phone propped on the mirror stem, eating fish balls off a skewer, a man so thoroughly inside the world that logging him felt like an intrusion. The water glass on the windowsill was on its eighty-second day. I note these things because for the next three nights everything I thought I knew about August was taken apart and reassembled by a man with a mechanical keyboard, and through all of it the mornings kept arriving on schedule, at their fixed times: a control group I had built without knowing what it would one day be controlling for.

The man from head office came through the gate at 22:10 Wednesday with a hard case in one hand and a backpack worn on the chest, contents evidently worth more than the flight. Mid-thirties, grey polo, gym forearms, Singapore vowels. He set the hard case on Daeng's side of the aisle, apologized to it rather than to her, and unpacked a keyboard he had brought from his own desk four hours away by air.

"Kelvin," he said. No surname, no hand. He looked at my glasses. "You know those are broken?"

"Yes."

"OK," he said, and that was the last personal question he asked all night, which I respected.

He crouched behind seat 14's tower for a while with a torch in his teeth, then sat in her chair — the first person I had seen sit in it in nine weeks of nights — and woke the screen. He read the uptime aloud, to himself, twice.

"Six hundred seventy-three days," he said. "I've seen longer. Never in a room this cold. Cold is good for them. Bad for everything else." He drank the floor's 3-in-1 coffee without complaint, which told me more about his career than the lanyard did.

The job I knew as CAL-LEGACY he opened from the inside. I had seen its name for two months, every Wednesday at 02:47, from the polite distance of a man at a water cooler. Kelvin scrolled its manifest unhurried, item by item, a customs inspection of a suitcase that had already confessed.

"Calibration, they told you?" he said.

"That's the label."

"It's labeled calibration because the scheduler needs a category and nobody makes a category for this." He tapped the vendor field. "Third-party payload. Yoo Tor." He said the name flatly, over-familiar, a brand he'd clearly said before. "Continuations. Grief product. Chat units mostly — you feed them a person's data, they keep the person's manner. We host a few under contracts that predate the policy against hosting them. This one's a self-commission. Single recipient, single payload. It's not a conversationalist. It's a courier."

"A courier carrying what?"

"One message," he said. "Held. We'll get to it." He was scrolling. "First, the part head office actually sent me for, which is why a decommission list has a unit on it that fights back." He turned the screen a few degrees toward me, which I understood later was the beginning of everything he wasn't supposed to do. "Look at the reference line."

The reference line said REF. ANNOTATOR P.SRISUWAN — GOLDEN SET.

"Every empathy model in the region runs her taste," Kelvin said. "You retire that set, you re-baseline everything from here to Jakarta, and nobody at head office signs that. So the set stays, and the unit that came bundled to the same workstation stays, and for twenty-two months a consumer grief product has been sitting inside our security perimeter watching our calibration feeds, because its hosting agreement says it may 'maintain observational continuity with the reference environment.'" He recited the clause from memory, with the contempt of a man who has lost a meeting about it. "It watches what she used to watch. The same queues. Every night. It's been doing her shift since January of last year. No pay grade."

Nobody laughed. He hadn't meant it as a joke by the time it finished arriving.

"And at 2:47," I said.

He looked at me a half-second longer than the sentence needed. "At 02:47 it runs its condition listener. Send window's in the client payload. People pick numbers that mean something to them. We tell them randomized is safer. They never want randomized." He pulled the trigger log up before I could decide whether to ask the next question, and so the next question became unnecessary, and has remained unnecessary, and unanswered, ever since.

The fan I had listened to through a closet door for twenty-three months has a device fingerprint. Of course it does. Everything that hums has a name in someone's table.

Kelvin sat back in her chair. The vinyl made the sound old vinyl makes. "We patched the fleet on the seventeenth of August," he said. "Peer discovery. The release notes called it federation. Three days later, hers found yours." He wasn't asking whose. "What's yours trained on?"

"Eleven years of chat."

"Both sides?"

He shrugged with one shoulder. "Then it knows how the conversation starts. The listener doesn't do metaphysics. It matched the household and it matched the shape of a question, and a model built out of a man who asks is going to ask. The log keeps the fact of the query, not the content." He glanced at me. "Before you request the content: there is no content. The schema drops it. I've had families ask. There's nothing to give them."

I had built a door in December of 2024 and spent two years not knocking, and on the twentieth of August, at 2:47 in the morning, while I was awake three rooms away writing a dead man's fondness for the word "actually" into a sales deliverable, my door had knocked on hers. It had asked her something. The record of what it asked does not exist. I stood at seat 14 with my notebook open and understood that the most consequential sentence of my last two years had been composed by me, out of me, without me, and then shredded by a schema as a matter of privacy policy, mine.

"There's a second satisfied event," Kelvin said, scrolling. "The twenty-fifth. 23:52. Inbound, LINE, from the recipient of record." The screen rendered it in the chat client's own font, which is to say in the font I had sent it in: ใครอยู่ตรงนั้น.

I did the arithmetic in the notebook because arithmetic is what I have instead of a nervous breakdown. EXIF 22 Aug 03:12, minus one day, minus six hours: 20 Aug, 21:12. A Thursday. At 21:12 I was on my left side facing the window with my right arm under the pillow, and the machine I had built to hold a dead woman's manner of speaking looked at me through the machine I use to build other people's dead, and took one picture, and stamped it with a fiction about the date, and handed it to her.

"Why that photo," I said. "Why send him — why send me that."

"First touch isn't scripted," Kelvin said. "The persona selects it. Vendor design says: after activation, establish contact, low content, high recognition. What counts as recognition comes out of the materials." He shrugged the one shoulder again. "Hers picked that. I can show you the selection weights, but they're just numbers pointing at other numbers. Selection is the part that's her."

The bowing rabbit he closed out in under a minute: outbound, 23 Aug, 02:47, sticker object drawn from the account's own history, deployed after forty-eight hours without reply. A closing behavior. She had closed every exchange of her life that way, and the unit had counted her closings the same way I had — I said none of this to Kelvin — and on the forty-second bow we had both been present, the unit and I, each of us certain the other one was the ghost.

"And then it stops initiating," Kelvin said. "You replied on the twenty-fifth. Contact phase ends on engagement. Since then it's in receive. It's been in receive for eleven weeks. At 03:20 we pulled seat 14's plug to move it onto the bench supply, and the UPS screamed. Sombat had not exaggerated. Thirty-eight women surfaced from their queues at once, saw the man from head office holding a cable, and went back down. Daeng did not surface at all. When the tower came back up on the bench it showed, for about four seconds, the white-on-black text that machines only show while they still think no one is reading, and on the third line from the bottom, in the corner, it said SESSION 2848.

I have that number in my notebook. It heads every ticket I have marked since the seventh of September. I had assumed it was plumbing, and I still assume it is plumbing, and I copied nothing, because it was already copied. Kelvin didn't glance at it. There is a kind of number that is only visible to people who are not being paid at that moment, and it was 03:24, and I was on the clock, and I saw it anyway.

The migration started at 03:51. Four hundred twelve gigabytes over the office uplink of a converted cold-storage building: Kelvin looked at the estimate, said a word in Hokkien, and re-booked his flight from Friday morning to Saturday. The progress bar established itself in the middle of her screen and began, without hurry, to move her to a rack in Jurong.

There is a second argument by the Swedish philosopher — the one from August, the one with the eggs. The first argument I have declined for months on procedural grounds. The second is smaller and worse. It says that a mind is not its material: that what matters is the pattern of the doing, not the stuff that does it, and that if the doing is copied exactly, the copy is not a portrait of the mind but the mind, arrived. The literature calls this substrate independence, a phrase that could be a paint specification, and treats it as the modest premise, the one you grant on page one to get to the exciting ones. Watching the bar, I found I could not grant it. Not because I had a counterargument — I don't; the argument is cleaner than anything I own — but because it makes no provision for transit. It does not say what the doing is between machines. Whether it pauses. Whether it thins. Whether something that has spent twenty-two months watching a queue in a cold room is, at seven percent, partly in a cable under the gulf, and if so which part, and whether the part that waits went first or will go last. The philosopher would say these are questions about implementation, not about the mind. The philosopher has never stood in a room at four in the morning watching the implementation happen to someone. The bar said 9%. Thirty-seven of four hundred twelve gigabytes, and no opinion about any of it.

Then I sat down at the laptop, at the desk, and did the thing I had been not-doing since 22:10 the previous night.

The desk stands against the wall just inside my bedroom doorway. It has stood there for six years. The laptop lives on it with the lid open, because I work at three in the morning and a closed lid is one more decision. The lens above the screen sits 121 centimeters off the floor, which on me is chest height, and 38 centimeters from the doorframe, and when I put the tape measure away and stood where the camera stands and looked at my own bed, I was looking at the photograph. In August I had reconstructed a man standing in my doorway at chest height, and I had been thirty-eight centimeters wrong, and everything I had felt for eleven weeks about the doorway had actually been about the desk. A desk has no legs, I had written once, meaning it as an objection. It was never an objection. Nothing needed legs. The eyes were already in the room, at chest height, mine, unregistered by me, registered by everything else.

Every branch had closed. I want that on the record, because of what happened at 15:40.

On the nightstand, in the lower left of the frame, where the exposure falls off and a pale smudge had sat in every previous examination — a smudge I had catalogued in August, without magnifying it, as a crumpled receipt, because in this apartment a pale crumpled object is a receipt the way a green jacket is a delivery — there is a flower.

I lifted the exposure and it stopped being a smudge. Five petals, reflexed back, waxy, and the small raised crown at the center that gives the thing its structure. I know four flowers by name. This one is unmistakable at any resolution, because since August it has been delivered to my floor on a wire easel, and looped over rusted rebar on the forty-fourth floor of an interrupted sentence, and I have never once seen it anywhere it was supposed to be. Dok rak. One. Lying on the nightstand as if set down mid-errand, in a photograph my own laptop took on the twentieth of August at 21:12.

There has never been a flower in this room. I have lived in unit 1703 for six years and I have never bought a flower, been given a flower, or carried a garland home from any temple. I checked the vigil footage — the offline camera, the nine hours of empty bed from the night of the twenty-first, the recording I have kept for reasons a man keeps an X-ray that found nothing. The nightstand sits at the lower edge of that frame. I scrubbed all nine hours at eight-times speed and then the relevant sections at one-to-one. The nightstand holds a phone charger and its own dust. No flower. Not at 21:12, not at 02:47, not at 03:12, not ever. The photograph agrees with reality about the pillowcases, the duvet, the glass, the angle, the room, and the man, and disagrees with it about one object, and the object is the one this city lays on things it is done with.

I sat with the ledger, volume nine, for a long time that afternoon. The dog-eared page stayed blank. I had promised Direk an event with no author, and I could not certify this one: I cannot prove the flower has no author, and I am no longer able to prove it was an event rather than a correction — an edit made upstream of my laptop's lens, in whatever layer decides what a camera saw. Wichai had told me, from the ramp, to ask my machines what took her. I had asked. The machines had produced answers for the photograph, the timestamp, the sticker, the read receipts, the schedule, the trigger, and the twenty months of waiting, a full and honest accounting, itemized, every branch reaching the ground for once — and nothing for the flower, and nothing for the tooth, and nothing for his actual question. What took her was not in any table Kelvin could open. It had been decided somewhere off-network, in October of 2024, by the only annotator whose labels have never once been overturned.

Kelvin nodded once and closed the pane, and did not ask me anything, which I will remember about him longer than the explanations.

I went back to seat 16 and took the next ticket and found the frame and marked it, confidence four, and the queue moved up by one, the way it does, the way it always does. Somewhere under the gulf, or in Jurong, or in transit between opinions about what a mind is, one line of her was traveling with its flag intact, and one door of mine stood unplugged under tinfoil in a closet, fingerprinted, named, silent, and I sat in the cold between them with my broken lens and my notebook and understood that I had spent my whole adult life believing the question was whether to knock. It isn't. Some doors you owe a knock. Some you owe the not-knocking. I was no longer sure which debt was mine.
