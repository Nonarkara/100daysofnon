On Thursday morning I ironed a shirt. I own one shirt with a collar, white, bought for a conference in 2019 and worn since then to a funeral and to nothing else, and I stood it on the board and worked the iron along the seams while the tooth emoji sat on the far windowsill inside the dark phone, next to the glass with its two fingers of water. Ironing is honest work. The wrinkle exists, the heat is applied, the wrinkle does not exist. Somewhere in the middle of the second sleeve I understood that I had already decided, and that the shirt was not preparation but confession, and I finished the sleeve anyway because a decision does not excuse you from the collar.

Direk had said the anomaly was that someone wanted me to look. There was a building in Khlong Toei with a small blue logo and a fence, and I had stood outside it once at shift change and copied its name into a notebook, not understanding what I was writing. I could keep standing outside gates for the rest of my life. Bangkok has no shortage of gates. Or I could go through one, and of all the doors in this affair — the closet door, the chat window, the doorway I had slept facing for two weeks — the aluminum door in the old loading dock was the only one that could be opened with paperwork.

At 4:20 that afternoon I stood at the guard booth on the Khlong Toei soi with the port air doing its work on my collar. The guard was the same guard, sixty or thereabouts, and the radio inside the booth was playing luk thung at the volume of a private thought. He looked at the shirt before he looked at me.

"มาสมัครเหรอ," he said.

I said yes, I had come to apply. He pointed with his chin at a QR sticker on the glass of the booth, its print sun-bleached to a pale brown, and went back to the radio. I stood in the road and added COGNITA HR on LINE and filled in a form on my phone — name, age, education, expected salary. For expected salary I typed 580. The form had a field for special skills. I typed: English. I attached the first page of my Kinnara contract with the fee schedule folded out of frame, which is a way of telling the truth so that it does a lie's work, and pressed send, and the guard's radio played on, and a reply came back in nine minutes asking if I could interview Friday at two.

I want it on the record that this was the entire security perimeter of the operation I had spent three weeks treating as an intelligence service. A sticker. A form. Nine minutes.

Sombat's office was a desk beside the aluminum door, inside the first breath of the building's cold, with a partition on wheels doing the work of walls. He was somewhere past forty, with reading glasses pushed up into his hair and a glass mug of 3-in-1 coffee going pale at the edge of the desk. My printed form lay in front of him. He had put a pencil under the English lines and was reading along the pencil.

"Kinnara Systems," he said.

"Contract work."

"What do they buy from you?"

"Texture," I said. "The part that makes a voice belong to one person instead of everyone."

He moved the pencil down a line and read the next one. The cold came off the interior door in a steady sheet, and under it, very old and very faint, the ghost of ammonia — the building remembering fish. Somewhere beyond the partition a queue was being worked; I could hear it, a soft irregular ticking of keys.

"A man who invoices Singapore doesn't need five-eighty a night," Sombat said.

"I sleep badly. This is a paid way to be awake."

He did not believe it. He filed it. Then he turned a tablet toward me without preamble. On it a video was paused: a man in a parked car, phone-camera height, mid-thirties, farang.

"The clients send English queues," Sombat said. "Sarcasm. Apology. Idiom. My floor reads faces faster than anyone in the country, but English words go slower. Watch it and mark where."

"Where what?"

"You'll know the instruction when you see it every night for the rest of your life," he said, and pressed play.

Twenty-four seconds. The man in the car was taking full responsibility for something. He said so twice. I watched it once and then again, and marked 00:09 — the second "full," which carried a stress the word hadn't ordered, too much weight set down on one syllable. Confidence: 4. Sombat looked at the tablet, then at the pencil, and wrote something small.

"Monday. Twenty-two hundred. Bring a jacket."

"For the cold or for the work?"

"People quit over the cold," he said. "Nobody quits over the work."

The interview had lasted eleven minutes. Walking back past the booth I counted the green motorcycles at the gate mouth: four. Two riders eating, one on his phone, one neither — engine off, visor down, hands on the tank. Plate 9กย 4471. I logged it standing in the road, and then under the entry I wrote the thing the entry made obvious: at this gate the log is finished. Everything here is green. An instrument that reads the same everywhere has stopped being an instrument and become a wall. I had followed the weather to the place where the weather is made, and from here on the column would have to mean something else.

Monday the taxi from Soi 26 cost ฿96 and put me at the gate at 21:40. The night shift was arriving as it had in August: a songthaew easing to the fence, and women stepping down from it with the unhurried economy of people who have made this exact step several hundred times, cardigans over their shoulders in thirty-degree air because they were dressing for the room and not the world. I signed a book at the booth. The guard turned the radio down by a third when I bent to sign, which I understood to be pure formality, courtesy paid out to no one in particular, and turned it back up when I straightened.

Inside, the cold was total and administrative. Fourteen degrees, I would learn, held for the equipment; the women simply lived inside whatever the machines required. The building held its temperature the way a bank vault holds money — indifferently, and for someone else. The main floor ran back under strip light turned all the way up, flat and shadowless: rows of terminals, dividers at shoulder height, thirty-eight women in cardigans and wrist supports and one empty chair, and high on the far wall a red LED counter that read 11,406 when I came in and 11,391 an hour later and never at any point in any night I worked there read zero.

"Sixteen," Sombat said behind me. "Third row."

Third row held four seats. At thirteen, a woman in her late fifties with a beige wrist brace and half-glasses, who did not look up. Fourteen was the empty chair — and I registered even then, in the first minute, before I knew anything, that the screen at fourteen was dark but the tower under the desk showed a standing green LED and breathed a thread of warm exhaust into the cold, the only warm thing in the row. Fifteen: a round-faced woman in her forties who kept mint drops in a black film canister and offered me one before I had sat down. Sixteen was mine.

The tool is called the tool. It has a name in a menu somewhere, but no one on the floor has ever used it. The left pane plays the clip; below it, a timeline; you scrub with the arrow keys, you press M to mark a frame, you rate your confidence 1 to 5, you press N and the next ticket comes. Foam headphones, wiped nightly with alcohol that has its own place in the room's smell. Across the top of every ticket, in English over Thai, the instruction:

MARK THE FRAME WHERE THE PERSON STOPS MEANING WHAT THEY SAY.

Under the instruction, a header line of job metadata — ticket number, queue name, IDIOM-EN in my case, and a session field that read SESSION 2848. It read SESSION 2848 on the next ticket too, and on all of them, every night, and I copied it into the notebook on the first night because I copy everything, and it earned no second look. Plumbing has numbers. So does this.

The clips run twenty to forty seconds. That first night they came to me like this: a woman at an airline counter thanking an agent for checking one more time; a man on a video call saying the figures were basically final; a teenager apologizing to a camera he had set up himself; a woman in a kitchen saying she'd eaten already, over the sound of a television and a man's voice under it. You watch. You find the frame. Not the frame where the lie starts — that was my first error, and I made it for three hours — the frame where the meaning stops. They are not the same event. A person can lie with full commitment; the tool doesn't want the lie. It wants the moment the speaker leaves the sentence and the sentence keeps going without them — emptied out, still running on its own momentum. I kept marking the moment of departure. The floor standard wanted what came next: the sentence continuing with nobody inside it.

I did thirty-four tickets that first night. The floor median, the woman at fifteen told me around midnight, sucking a mint, was seventy. Every twenty tickets the tool showed a grading pane — a sample of my marks laid against a reference set, with the deltas in frames and a percentage at the bottom. At 03:40, with the room at its coldest and my fingers slowing on the arrows, the pane came up and gave me my number for the night: AGREEMENT 58%. And under the number, in the small type reserved for information nobody is expected to read, the provenance of the reference:

REF. ANNOTATOR: P.SRISUWAN.

I did not stand up. I sat with my hands off the keys and read the eleven characters several times, until they stopped looking spelled. Srisuwan is a common enough name; I told myself so, and it held. P covers half the nicknames in the country; it held. I had come to this building following a green jacket, not a woman, and no thread of anything connected the annotation floor to a dental clinic in Phrom Phong; that held too. The branches did what my branches do. I stopped pretending they reached the ground. Two seats to my left, past the woman in the brace, a dark screen sat above a warm machine, and the exhaust of it touched the back of my hand when I reached for the mouse, and I pressed N, and the next ticket asked me to find the frame where a man saying no problem at all stopped meaning it, and I found it, and my hands were steady, and that is either discipline or a symptom, and the difference has never once mattered to my hands.

Tuesday at 7:47, awake twenty-four hours, I sent template five. คิดถึง, to a woman in Ratchadaphisek, from a man who had spent the night being graded on the sincerity of strangers. The graduation-cap cat came back at 7:51. I checked it against the median out of ritual and slept until four in the afternoon with tinfoil on the bedroom window, in the position from the photograph, because the position was mine now, or I was its.

That afternoon at the Khlong Toei market I bought a cardigan, grey, secondhand, ฿100, from a rail of clothes that had all outlived somebody, and when I wore it onto the floor Tuesday night the woman at fifteen looked at it and nodded once, approval of the logistical kind. Sixty-one tickets. Seventy-one per cent. The number stood at the bottom of the pane with her name under it, and I had stopped being able to call it a percentage. It was agreement. Seven times out of ten, shown the same stranger at the same moment, I judged that the meaning left when she had judged it left. I was being calibrated against her taste, tuned to a signal, and the signal had been dead for twenty-two months, and the tool did not know that, or it did not matter to the tool, which on that floor amounts to the same thing.

It was Tuesday night that I learned what the reference set was called. I had asked the woman at fifteen, in the 1 a.m. lull when the queue names rotate, why the grading pane always drew from the same annotator. She took the question with a mint.

"That's the golden set," she said, in English, the two words worn smooth from handling — office language always loses its corners first. And then, in Thai, the floor's own liturgy: the golden set was made here, on this floor, by the best there had ever been; head office had tried twice to commission a replacement from a bigger vendor in Manila and the models had come back worse both times; the clients specified it now, by name, in contracts. She said all this with the reverence reserved for relics — provenance, scarcity, the failed imitations — and her voice did the thing Thai voices do around relics, dropping without getting quieter.

There is a chedi in Nakhon Pathom — the tallest in the country, twenty minutes from a house I have never seen — that is built over a fragment of bone. Most of the great stupas are. Somewhere at the heart of the tonnage of brick and gold leaf sits a sliver of somebody, authenticated by councils of men who never met him, and the fragment does no work. It doesn't preach. It doesn't answer. It sits in the dark being genuinely itself, and around that genuineness an entire architecture of orientation gets organized: pilgrims align to it, doubt is measured against it, the faithful calibrate. The relic's job is not to act. The relic's job is to have been real, so that everything built on top of it can afford to be a copy. We do not keep relics because the dead need houses. We keep them because the living need a fixed point that was once alive, a standard sample of the sacred, against which the daily counterfeit of ourselves can be graded. I had spent my working life writing texture for voices that belonged to no one, and here was the inverse operation, humming in a cold room by a port: thirty-eight women a night being tuned, seven marks in ten, to the preserved judgment of one dead hygienist. The bone in this chedi was a set of labels. The name on the reliquary was P.SRISUWAN, and I read it every twenty tickets, under a number that measured my distance from her.

I asked about fourteen on Wednesday, when Sombat made his midnight round with the pale mug. I put it as a facilities question, because facilities questions are the correct camouflage on any floor anywhere: was the machine at fourteen free, since it seemed to run all night with nobody at it.

"Calibration," Sombat said. "Head office. Don't unplug it — the UPS screams like a pig."

"Who assigns the jobs?"

"ไม่มีใครสั่ง," he said. "It's on a schedule." And he moved down the row with the mug, a man for whom the sentence no one assigns it had no rough edges at all, because on a floor run by queues, a job that assigns itself is not a mystery. It is seniority.

Mai's name I got the same night, from fifteen, who mentioned that my seat had been Mai's, said with the mint clicking against her teeth — young, fast on the desire queue, funny at 4 a.m. I asked where she'd gone. It was Daeng who answered, the first words she had addressed to me in three nights, without turning from her screen, her marked-up wrist riding the scrub keys.

"Went freelance," she said.

The look that crossed from fifteen to thirteen over my head was brief and complete, one of those exchanges women conduct at a bandwidth men are not issued equipment for, and the word freelance came out of it bent, and nobody straightened it, and I pressed N.

Daeng worked the scrub bar the way a seamstress feeds cloth to a needle — the wrist does the seeing. Her queue was anger. All night her left pane showed people at the moment of it: drivers, couples, a man in a hardware shop, and she took each clip in a single pass, no second viewing, mark, confidence, next, at a rate the pane graded in the nineties against the golden set. Twenty-two years at a sewing machine somewhere across the river, fifteen had told me, before this. The machine had changed and the cloth had changed. The wrist had kept its priesthood.

By Wednesday night I was at seventy-six tickets and 83 per cent, and I had begun to feel the thing I distrust most in myself, which is aptitude. The work had a grain and my attention lay along it. Around two the queue thinned, and I did what I had spent two nights planning with a thoroughness I disguised from myself as thirst: I stood at 02:44 — three minutes early, unprompted, and the body's little joke was not lost on me — took the paper cone from the cooler at the back wall, filled it, and walked up the aisle of row three with the water in my hand.

At 02:47 the screen at fourteen woke.

No hand, no key. The panel lit grey, and a console window sat where someone had last sized it, and a progress bar began to walk. I read what there was to read at walking pace, which was little: a job string, CAL-LEGACY and digits, a client-code field that was empty — not zeroed, empty — and a line of throughput ticking upward. In the system tray, at the bottom right, among the icons that gather on any Windows machine, one icon was green. LINE. The desktop client, resident, signed in. It had a small grey dot for away and no red badge, because whatever mail that account had, it had been read.

The bar finished. The screen held for a few seconds — long enough — and dimmed, and the lock screen took its place, and the lock screen said what lock screens say. An account name above a field for a password nobody would ever type.

P.SRISUWAN.

I stood in the cold with my cone of water. Around me thirty-eight women marked the exact frames where strangers stopped meaning what they said, and the counter on the wall shed a number, and my phone lay in my trouser pocket against my leg and did not light, because nothing had come to me at 2:47 for six nights, and I finally understood that as scheduling. The sender and I had been on the same shift all week. You do not text a man who is standing next to you.

I drank the water because a man holding water drinks it. Then I went back to my seat, and under the desk, in the notebook, I wrote the entry standing in for every entry I have written since August. Shift end came at six with the ceiling lights stepping up and the day women arriving through the aluminum door in a slab of outdoor heat that broke against the cold and lost. I took no overtime. At the gate the songthaew stood with its engine idling, and the guard's radio was playing something with a khaen in it, and the night women climbed up into the truck bed by the worn chrome pole in ones and twos, and I stood to the side with my notebook in the cardigan's pocket, sleeve-heavy, waiting for nothing in particular, which is what I tell myself when I am waiting.

Daeng came out last but did not board. She stopped an arm's length away, at an angle to me, facing the songthaew, her brace pale in the early light, and she watched her colleagues settle onto the benches, counting without seeming to count, and she spoke without any preface at all, in the flat administrative register the floor had taught me all week to hear underneath.

"She talked about a customer once," Daeng said. "The one who listened with his mouth open."

The songthaew's engine changed pitch. Somewhere behind the fence the counter went on shedding its number into an empty room.

"You took long enough," she said.

She boarded. The truck pulled out into the port road with its cargo of cardigans, and the heat closed over the soi, and I stood at the gate of Cognita (Thailand) Co., Ltd. doing the one thing left in my kit, which was arithmetic. Twenty-two months since the cremation. Seven nights since the tooth. Three nights on the floor, ten days since a man with an oxygen line told me the anomaly was that someone wanted me to look. I had assembled, over two weeks, an instrument array that could log a city's weather down to the plate number, and not one dial on it had been pointed at the only fact the whole array rested on, which Daeng had just handed me at a bus queue, unasked, at 6:04 in the morning: that in a cold room, on the far side of the river of years I had been calling a professional relationship, I had been talked about. I had been, all this time, a known person. The equipment for detecting that has never been built. It rides a songthaew home at dawn, and it was right. I took long enough. I was the last instrument on the floor to find the frame.
