The notebook was free. The bank gave one to every agent at orientation — green cover, gold logo in the corner, eighty pages of paper thin enough that a ballpoint left braille on the next three sheets. Most of the girls used theirs for shift swaps and merit-point arithmetic and lost them by Songkran. Fon had ruled hers into columns in her first week, because the trainer had said one thing she believed: what you don't log, you will retell wrong, and what you retell wrong you will start to believe.

Five columns. Date. Time in. Code. Time out. Disposition.

The reply window was hers too. She had decided it in the second week, June of last year: answer before 7:49 and he would learn that nothing had been read, only received. Answer after 7:56 and a man like that would begin to escalate somewhere inside himself, quietly, in whatever columns he kept. So: 7:49 to 7:56, drift the minutes so the drift looked human, hold the median where a person would hold it. Three times in seventeen months she had broken the window — Songkran, her grandmother's house in Roi Et, where the phone signal lived out by the road and she had walked to it in the heat at ten past eight three mornings running. He probably had those three replies filed as her only anomalies. She had them filed as her only errors.

On a sheet of graph paper from the copy shop, two baht, she had once plotted his send times. Five hundred and twenty-one points at 7:47. One gap. At work they taught you to read the shape of a caller's history before the call connected, and the first rule of shape was that a person drifts. A flat line is not a person. A flat line is a system, or a person gripping one.

Her disposition code for him had been settled for over a year. At the bank there was a kind of customer every agent knew — the ones who called daily to hear the balance of an account they never drew on. เช็คยอด. Balance checkers. They were not checking the money. They were checking that the account still answered to their name. In seventeen months he had never asked her a question that a reply could settle. He was not checking on her. He was checking that the checking still worked. It was the most honest thing she knew about him, and she had never said it, because there was no script in which it helped.

The money had its own page. Twelve thousand on the first of every month, memo blank, seventeen transfers, ฿204,000. Her salary was ฿16,800 before incentives. Eight thousand went to Roi Et on the fifth. The twelve thousand went into an account she had opened for it and never touched, and in the column where she should have written what that account was for, she had written nothing, for seventeen months. She was aware the blank was doing a great deal of work. Blanks always did.

In October the bank had installed a new quality system. It listened to every call on the floor and marked the exact second an agent stopped meaning the script; the agents called it ครูใหม่ and adjusted their sincerity accordingly. Two weeks ago it had flagged her at minute six of a call with a widow in Nong Khai who was three payments behind — flagged the one sentence in the whole call that Fon had meant entirely. Her dispute was denied by something with no name field. On the 514 bus home she had reread her own green notebook front to back, all five columns, and had not liked which side of the headset it put her on.

So: tonight she would end it. She built the sentence on the MRT by the same three rules she used for every de-escalation script — no blame, no doors left open, no numbers — and rode the escalator up out of Phrom Phong station with the sentence ready and two temple garlands in her bag.

The garlands were an accident of change. Dok rak, both of them, strung close and white with a marigold at the head. One was for the shelf above her bed. The other rode along because there was nowhere to leave it that wasn't a bin.

Then the door, and the sentence she had built did not survive it. His glasses were broken. The right lens carried a crack in the shape of a star, the left arm sat bent at the temple, and he was wearing them anyway — had been wearing them for some time, she understood, because the crack had a settled look, the look of a thing that has been explained to its owner and accepted. A man with money in his account and a lens shop two hundred meters away in Emporium, looking at her through a broken window and saying nothing about it.

She said she had happened to be nearby. They both let that stand.

She ordered food on her app without asking, because a situation that is being fed becomes easier to read. At 8:14 the doorbell rang once and his shoulders came up and locked. He stood, too fast, and then stood completely still, facing the door without going to it, and she watched him make himself go. It was a rider with rice boxes. Green jacket, wet delivery bag, gone in forty seconds. He carried the boxes back to the counter, checked the printed slip against the bags before he would set them down, and only then remembered her. She lowered her voice one step and slowed down, which is what you are trained to do when the customer is not going to tell you what is wrong.

She had come to close the account. She stayed instead, and knew while she was doing it that staying was not the professional move. It was — she made herself file it honestly — fascination, of a kind she did not respect in herself: watching this man was watching a man drown in a pool with no water. You cannot save him, and you cannot look away, and there is no water.

They ate at the counter. He asked about Roi Et and listened to the answers the whole way through, which almost nobody did. He did not mention the glasses, the doorbell, or the seventeen months. Before eleven he fell asleep on top of the duvet with the glasses folded beside him, one lens starred, and she sat for a while in the light of the kitchen with the green notebook unopened in her bag.

Then she stood in the bedroom doorway with the bag on her shoulder and watched him sleep — on his left side, facing the window, right arm under the pillow — and on the windowsill sat a glass with two fingers of water in it and a fine grey dust on the water. She reached toward it to carry it to the sink and then didn't, because in other people's houses you do not move the things that look decided.

The second garland had been in her bag since four o'clock and the string had loosened. When she lifted it out, one bud came free in her hand. A whole garland on a man's nightstand is a statement. One flower is barely anything. She preferred barely anything. She set the single dok rak down on the near corner of the nightstand, the left one, closest to the door, and let herself out, and rode home in a taxi with the window cracked, ฿112, and made the day's entry at the red light on Asok: date, time in, time out. On Monday at 11:26 an unknown number called and a young woman's voice asked whether I was the one who brought mandarins. I said I was. She was Ajarn Direk's niece. He had died on Sunday morning; the woman who cooked for him found him at half past nine, in the chair by the window, and the oxygen concentrator was still running. He had left a list. Nine items, in his handwriting, with a page number for each in case anyone doubted the handwriting. The chanting would be at Wat Hua Lamphong, three nights, cremation the following Sunday. Item four was the Go board.

A Go position is not the stones. The stones are stamped shale and clamshell; a factory in Japan or a workshop in Yunnan makes thousands of them and they are all the same stone. The position is the keeping — an arrangement that exists only for as long as at least one mind holds it as meaning something. For eleven years, about a hundred and forty stones on Banthat Thong had been a held thing: a Wednesday afternoon in March 2015, kept at the temperature of the moment Anek's hand left the last black stone. On Tuesday two men who did not play lifted the stones off in handfuls, and somewhere in that minute the position stopped being anywhere. The stones went back to being what the factory made.
