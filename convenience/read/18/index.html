THE CONVENIENCE · INTERLUDE E

The Queued Message

18 of 19

The flower stall by Wat Lam Duan sold garlands by the meter and wreaths by the size of the grief, and the woman who ran it, seventy if she was a day, kept a laminated price sheet under a jam jar of pens that nobody used because she remembered everyone's order by face. Pui had been coming here since January, when she'd first stopped in for a merit-making garland and left with a brochure she hadn't asked for. Now, on a Tuesday in October, she was back for something else.

She wanted dok rak. Woven, not shop-built — strung the old way, garland-style, roughly seventy centimeters across. White. No color mixed in.

"For a wat?" the woman asked, already reaching for string.

"For a person," Pui said.

"Name for the ribbon?"

"No name." She watched the woman's hands, the same economy of motion Pui herself used on a clip at 3 a.m., no wasted frame. "Just the words. ด้วยความอาลัย. Machine print is fine."

The woman wrote it on a slip without asking why a wreath needed no name if it had an address, and Pui gave her the address: a condo tower off Sukhumvit, floor seventeen, unit seventeen-oh-three. She recited it without looking it up. She had filled it in herself, once, on an intake form in a dental clinic in 2013, in handwriting she no longer used, and she had typed it into a scheduling system twice a year for nine years after that, and some corner of her that filed things the way she filed a turning frame had simply never let it go.

"And the delivery date, khun?"

This was the part the woman had handled before, for people who ordered ahead of a chedi anniversary or a birthday nobody would live to see. Pui said she didn't know the date. She said: hold it. There would be a condition. She would not be the one to trigger it.

The woman didn't blink. She had a ledger for exactly this, a spiral notebook with a column for prepaid holds, dates left, amounts right — the same shape of page, Pui noticed, that she herself had been keeping in a grey notebook with a rubber band since January, though the woman's held funerals and Pui's had mostly held frames of other people's faces. She wrote CONDITION: TBC and took a deposit, six hundred baht, cash, folded twice, and said the flowers themselves, when the day came, would be fresh-cut and no older than a day, because she did not deliver stale work no matter how long the wait. Pui thanked her and did not correct the arithmetic running quietly behind the thanks, which said this would probably take longer than the woman's system was built to hold something, which meant it would probably never come, which was, in its own crooked way, the outcome she had actually been hoping for when she started the sentence. A wreath that never arrived was a wreath that had never been needed. She walked out past the temple's collection of caged birds sold for release, priced by the pair, and did not buy any, because she had never once believed that was how the debt worked.

Wednesday, the envelope, same as always. A boy on a Honda Wave idled at the mouth of the soi at six, engine running, and Pui counted nine thousand baht into a rubber band with the same hand that carried, on the inside of its wrist, a burn scar from a pan of oil she'd tipped at nine years old, a mark she had shown a dentist across a reclining chair once, years ago, as a story about cooking rather than a story about carelessness. The boy counted it again with his thumb without looking at her and rode off toward whatever office kept the real number, the one that never shrank no matter how many Wednesdays fed it. Wat did not know the amount had, for four months now, been arriving one thousand short of what she actually earned, the difference sewn into a cardigan pocket and later carried, in two visits eight months apart, to an office above a travel agency on Charoen Krung. Wat believed the debt was simply winning. He was not wrong. He had just never once asked where the losing side went, only that it kept losing on schedule, and a wife who kept it on schedule was, to him, a wife doing her job.

She had written the line for the recipient in September, in the room above the travel agency, with Khun Nan waiting by the door with a patience that had a script behind it. She remembered the writing better than she remembered handing it over.

The first draft said too much. It explained. It used the word sorry four times and she crossed it out because sorry was a door left open for someone to walk through and reassure her, and there would be no one there to do that when it opened.

The second draft was a joke, the kind he'd have laughed at once — something about the reclined angle of a dental chair, forty-four degrees, a private number only the two of them had ever had reason to know — and she threw it out because a joke needed a room with two people breathing in it, and this room would only ever have one.

The third was a question. Did you ever believe me? She sat with that one the longest. It was the closest to true and the least fair, because it put the weight back on him at the exact moment she was choosing, finally, to carry something alone.

What she wrote instead — one line, folded once, sealed, handed across the laminated counter — she read twice before letting go of the envelope, and the reader of this account does not get to read it either. Some things a person is allowed to finish without an audience. Khun Nan filed it under a trigger condition Pui had already dictated to the technician, in the flat professional Thai she used for shift instructions, the same words that would sit unread in a system for two years before anyone tripped them: เงื่อนไข: เมื่อเขาเริ่มถาม.

Thursday's shift was ordinary in the specific way the last ordinary thing always is, which is to say it did not announce itself. The floor held its fourteen degrees. Sombat read an English client email with a pencil under the words he wasn't sure of, and got to the end, and was sure of none of them, and sent it anyway. Daeng, at thirteen, cleared the anger queue at her usual rate — she had crossed sixty clips before Pui had crossed thirty, arthritis be damned, twenty-two years of a sewing machine still living in her wrists as speed — and complained about the wrist at her usual volume, and somewhere in the second hour she said, not looking up from her screen, that Pui looked tired. Pui said she was fine. Daeng let it go. She had learned which forms on this floor were hers to correct and which weren't, and she filed this one, without comment, under the second kind.

Pui cleared two hundred and nine clips that night, close enough to her own median that no one flagged it, and marked the frame where the person stopped meaning what they said two hundred and nine times, and was right, by Singapore's count, in every case Singapore ever bothered to sample. Somewhere in the building a workstation she did not use kept running a job with no client code, the way it always did, the way it would keep doing long after anyone still working here remembered why.

She stayed forty minutes past shift-end. She had done this before, alone, the fluorescent bank over her row switched to the dimmer setting the cleaners used, the rest of the floor breathing its 2 a.m. quiet around her.

She pulled the staff New Year video — herself, third from left, laughing at something off-camera, a company polo, HR's phone wedged on a tripod someone had borrowed and never returned — and ran it through the tool the way she'd run it through once before, in July, when it had stalled the way it stalled on the hardest clips and marked a frame at 00:02:47, her own face on the machine's own guess of the second her smile stopped being about the thing it claimed to be about.

She opened that flag now and sat with it a long moment, the confidence bar frozen the way it always froze on her, as if the software understood it had reached the one annotator whose judgment about this particular frame it could not, on principle, out-rank.

She overrode it.

FALSE POSITIVE, she typed, and beneath the field for a reason she wrote nothing, because there was no ticket header asking her to explain herself to Singapore, only the small mercy of a system built to trust her more than it trusted itself. The correction logged the way every correction logged, stamped with the header nobody else at Cognita bothered to spell with a space: P.SRISUWAN, 03:12.

She watched the frame play forward past the second the machine had doubted her. Nothing in it changed. She had known it wouldn't. That had always been the point of asking.

She closed the client dashboard. She closed the annotation queue, and the internal messaging tool, and the calendar that still had a note in it from June about a dentist's appointment she was never going to book. She logged out of the shift-tracking app that totaled her hours for pay, out of the HR portal, out of the private folder on the office server where, for eight months, she had kept a second life's worth of footage nobody at Cognita had ever billed a client for.

The LINE icon sat at the bottom of the screen the way it always did, green, undramatic, a small square that had never once in eleven years asked her for a reason.

She left that one open.

Then she gathered her things — the grey notebook with the rubber band, its second volume, seven lines on the inside cover and six of them checked now in pencil, the seventh left exactly as it had been for months, unmarked, patient, hers alone — and clocked out, and signed nothing, because Cognita did not require a signature for leaving, only for arriving. The elevator down was empty. The gate guard nodded at her the way he nodded at everyone on their way home at six in the morning, not knowing, the way none of them ever knew, which mornings were the ones that mattered, and Pui nodded back, and stepped out into a city already going about the business of a Friday it had no idea was different from any other, the sky over Khlong Toei still holding the flat grey of a night shift not yet finished deciding whether to become morning.
