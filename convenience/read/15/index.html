The 7-Eleven at the mouth of my soi sells eggs in cartons of ten. On Saturday night, the seventh of November, I bought one carton, a bottle of dishwashing liquid, and a can of Birdy — ฿62, ฿35, ฿19, total ฿116, printed at 20:38, kept. The clerk with the star tattoo had the night off. A different clerk counted my change without looking at it, which is a skill, and I walked home the back way. I mention the eggs because two of them did not survive the next twenty minutes, and I want their deaths on the record.

That morning the alarm had rung at 7:47 and I had sent template five, คิดถึง, the fifty-fifth morning of the second streak. A cat with its face pressed into its paws had come back at 7:51. The rider log had taken an absence for Saturday, entered as an absence. The water glass on the windowsill was on its seventy-eighth day. Everything was accounted for. I want it noted that everything was accounted for.

There is a steel pedestrian door from the sub-soi into level B1 of my building's garage, and using it instead of the lobby saves forty-one steps. I had counted the saving in March, on a night when counting was the only instrument I had that still returned values. The door closes on a spring that has been tired for years. It was still swinging when I smelled the whisky.

A man was standing between a grey Fortuner and pillar B1-11. He had a flat bottle of Hong Thong in his jacket pocket with the cap showing, and a phone in his left hand, and he was looking at the phone when I came through the door, and then he was looking at me. I knew the face before I knew where from. Then he checked the phone again — a small tic of the wrist, involuntary, a man consulting a machine that had never once told him anything good — and I knew where from. At the funeral he had done it about every ninety seconds. He was doing it at about every ninety seconds now. Some instruments survive their owners' circumstances.

"You signed the book at the temple," he said. "Forty-first name."

I had signed it. There had been a pen on a string, and a queue of three people, and I had written my name smaller than usual and left before the chanting. I had not thought about the book since. A guestbook is a sensor too. It just has a very long refresh rate.

"People at the factory say a man came asking about my wife," he said. "English queue. Older. Polite." He said polite in the tone other men use for contagious.

"I work there," I said. "Nights."

"I know where you work."

He came off the pillar. He was not a large man. Customs officers are weighed twice a year and Wichai was inside his weight, a grey polo, civil-service haircut, the kind of build that comes from standing at a checkpoint window for twenty years. What he had instead of size was certainty, the specific certainty of a man who has done arithmetic all night and gotten the same answer every time, and the whisky was not the source of it. The whisky was just the solvent.

His hand went into my chest and my back went into the pillar. It was not graceful and it was not cinematic. The bag swung against the concrete and I heard the carton take the loss. My shoulder blades hit first and then the back of my head, once, a tap, enough to make the garage lighting do something optical. My glasses went. I heard them land and did not hear them break.

"Where is it," he said.

"Where is what?"

"อย่ากวน." His face was close enough that the Hong Thong stopped being a smell and became a climate. "The papers. Whatever she left with you. หกหมื่นหนึ่งพัน."

Sixty-one thousand. He had carried that number up and down stairs for two years; you could hear the stairs in it. It was specific in the way only true numbers are specific — nobody invents sixty-one; an inventor says fifty or a hundred — and I had never heard it in my life, and I understood at once that this was not a defense I could use, because the men who most need to hear I've never heard that number are the ones who have stopped being able to hear it.

"I did the arithmetic," he said. "Fourteen months of nights. Overtime. It's not in the book. Money doesn't evaporate. It goes somewhere. Everything goes somewhere."

"I was her customer," I said. "At the clinic. Four o'clock, twice a year. Eighteen appointments in nine years. She cleaned my teeth."

"Nine years," he said, and the two words carried an accusation, and in a sense I had confessed to something.

The box-cutter came out of his trouser pocket. Yellow handle, hardware-store, the kind sold in bins of ten. He thumbed the blade out two clicks and then held it low, against his own thigh, pointed mostly at the floor, and I want to be precise about this because precision is the only dignity available to either of us in that garage: he did not raise it. He held it low and without ceremony, a document he had hoped not to need at the meeting. It was not a threat so much as an exhibit.

"The insurance paid," he said. "It's finished. Signed. Closed." He was not really talking to me by then. "If a paper exists that unfinishes it, I'm dead. Not the word. The thing."

"She never gave me anything," I said. "In eighteen appointments she gave me one rule about omelets."

He stared at me. It was the truth, and it sounded insane, which is a property truths acquire around their eleventh year of storage.

I got my wallet out slowly and offered it, which was a miscalculation. He knocked my hand aside, not hard, offended in a way the shove had not been. "I'm not a thief," he said, and he wasn't. That was the whole problem with him, standing there with a blade and a bottle and a number. A thief improvises. This man had a settlement to protect, a figure, a document trail, a closed file, and he had come to my building on administrative business — after hours, out of channels, but administrative business all the same, the kind conducted when an office has made an error in your account. He checked his phone. Ninety seconds, more or less. I timed it against my own pulse, which was not a reliable clock just then.

Then he stepped back, and in stepping back his heel came down on my glasses. The sound was small and complete. He looked down at them the way a man looks at a glass he knows he did not knock over, and did not say anything, and stepped off them.

At the bottom of the ramp he stopped. The garage does a thing to voices, adds concrete to them, and his came back to me with the concrete still in it.

"She was already gone a year before she went," he said. "Ask your machines what took her."

Then he walked up the ramp into the soi, an ordinary man in a grey polo, and the spring-loaded door I had come through clicked, finally, shut.

In the corner above the ramp there is a camera with a red diode. It blinks at its own rate, which corresponds to nothing — not to recording, the installer told the juristic committee once, just to power. It had blinked through all of it. For two and a half months I had objected, in writing, with tape and seals and dice, to being watched. Under pillar B1-11 I had wanted nothing else, and there was no way to know whether I had gotten it.

I picked up the glasses. The right lens had gone to web. The left arm was bent about fifteen degrees, which I would later correct to eleven with two pairs of pliers. I have worn glasses for distance since I was thirty-one; up close I was fine, which meant I could see the receipt in the bag and not the ramp he'd left by. Two eggs of ten had cracked. I carried the survivors upstairs.

The strip of receipt paper in the door jamb was where I had left it. The apartment was in order. I stood at the kitchen counter and held my hands out flat over it, and the right one stopped at 21:15 and the left one at 21:18, and I wrote the times down, and then I opened the notebook to the EQUIPMENT column and entered: เสาร์ 7 พ.ย. — 20:52 — optics — ผู้แต่งภายนอก. External author. I looked at the entry for a while. It was the first line in that column with an author in it, and the author was not a system, and the entry did not make me feel what I had assumed an entry like that would make me feel.

The box-cutter stayed with me longer than the shove did. A box-cutter's blade is scored crosswise so that when a segment dulls you snap it off with pliers and the next segment is new. You buy one blade and discard it a centimeter at a time. There was an anthropologist — dead now, young, I used to assign a chapter of his to third-years who wanted to talk about markets — who spent five hundred pages establishing that debt is older than money: that the earliest writing we have is not poetry or prayer but ledgers, grain owed, silver owed, names in columns, and that the whole apparatus of obligation ran for millennia on nothing but memory and shame before coinage arrived to make it portable. Morality, he argued, did not descend into the ledger from somewhere higher. It came out of the ledger. First the column, then the commandment. I used to present this as a provocation. Standing at my kitchen counter I understood it as a set of operating instructions, because I had just spent nine minutes against a pillar with the oldest algorithm in the world, an algorithm that needs no server and no camera and no dev kit, that runs on interest and fear at room temperature, that had taken an ordinary customs officer — a man who in another column of the spreadsheet waves trucks through a checkpoint and eats lunch and worries about his weight — and optimized him, snap by snap, a centimeter at a time, until the segment presented to me in a parking garage was new and clean and had a factory edge on it. He had used two clicks of the blade. There were eleven left, and none of them were for me, and that was the least comforting fact of the entire evening.

I did not cook the eggs. I made coffee I did not drink and sat down with Direk's ledger, volume nine, which had lived on my table since the first of November with the dog-eared blank page waiting in it. For a quarter of an hour I considered whether the garage was homework. One event, no author. I wrote nothing on the dog-eared page. The garage had an author; I had met him; his tic ran at ninety seconds; his number was sixty-one thousand. In my own notebook I entered the event under its own date and in the third column, in block letters because Direk's hand had earned block letters, I wrote: หนี้. Debt. Amount unknown. Then, because there was nowhere else the evening could go, I turned to the last page of the ledger, the one with the pencil lines, the one I had been forbidden.

That page has a condition on it, Direk had said. It will be met.

The condition was the first line, in Thai, in the small level hand he'd had before the shaking: เงื่อนไข: เมื่อผู้แต่งลงมือกับตัวคุณเอง. When an author lays hands on you personally. Under it, in English, four lines:

Charoen Krung 51. The tall one you can see from the river — the one that stopped. The fence has opinions; the gap behind the noodle cart does not. 44.

And under that: Tell the sweeper I still owe him for a molar. He keeps accounts. He will know which one.

I read it three times. The condition had been met at 20:52 against pillar B1-11, and Direk had written the page before the first of November, in a hand that already shook, for a night he could not have known the date of and had not doubted the arrival of. I thought about phoning him. It was 23:40. He answers on the sixth ring now, and the concentrator sighs every three seconds, and there are only so many rings in a man. I did not phone. I took the bent glasses, and the notebook, and went down to Sukhumvit and raised my arm.

The taxi took Rama IV to Sathorn to Charoen Krung, ฿118 on the meter, and the driver asked one question at the end — ตึกร้างเหรอ — and I said ครับ, and he made the sound Thai drivers make when a fare's business is legal but not sensible, and left me on the far side of the road from it.

Everyone in this city knows the tower without knowing they know it. Forty-nine floors above the river, capped and columned and empty, begun in 1990 by a man who was also building its sister a kilometer downriver — the sister got a golden dome and a sky bar and a decade of tourists photographing their drinks; this one got 1997. The money stopped in July of that year and the building simply held its position. I taught it once, a single slide in a second-year lecture: a building is a decision that takes years to finish saying, and this one had been interrupted mid-sentence and never permitted to complete the thought. From the road at 1:44 a.m. you could see rebar still standing up out of the roofline against the sky, decades of it, rusted and patient, like unfinished sentences. The architect's other building got to finish its sentence. Nobody remembers what either sentence was about.

The hoarding along the road is corrugated sheet, painted, official. Behind the noodle cart — chained for the night, stools stacked and cabled — two sheets overlap without fastening, and the gap took me and the bag without argument, at 1:52. Inside, the ground floor smelled of dust and dog and the river. A dog materialized, medium, ginger, professionally unsurprised, and walked me as far as the second floor and then stopped, having reached the edge of a territory or a union rule.

I climbed with the phone's light. The stairs are real and mostly finished — the building died late enough to have stairs — and I counted steps out of habit and lost the count around nine hundred, somewhere past floor 31, and the loss did not upset me, which I noted, because it would have upset me in August. Someone had spray-painted floor numbers at the landings and the numbers ran two floors optimistic from floor 20 up; whoever counted for the paint had wanted the building taller. On the landings the details start: balustrades, actual balusters, classical profiles cast in concrete and installed on floors that never received walls, so that ranks of little Corinthian columns stand at the open edges holding up nothing, facing the river, attended by pigeons. It is the most honest building I know. Every other tower in this city is a rendering that got built. This one is a building that got un-rendered, and it does not pretend otherwise, and the wind goes through it all night making the sound of a scale model of weather.

I reached 44 at 2:39. Forty-seven minutes. My legs were announcing themselves and the right lens webbed the left half of the river into a mosaic whenever I turned my head, and I stood on the landing a moment to let my pulse come down, and that is when I smelled them — before I saw anything, over the dust and the pigeon and the river: dok rak.

They were on the rebar. The floor is open on the river side, a whole bay of missing wall, and the rebar comes up out of the slab at the edge in a row, and on five of the bars someone had looped garlands — not wreaths, garlands, พวงมาลัย, the everyday kind, but made entirely of dok rak, white, and fresh. I put the light on them and then put my fingers on one. The petals were cool and firm. The string was clean. At the foot of one bar stood a plastic bucket with three more garlands lying in two fingers of water, and I looked at the two fingers of water for a while, and filed the resemblance where I file things now, in the crowded column, and took the light off it.

The chair faces the river. It is a dental chair, real, old — a cast-iron base, a hydraulic pump with a foot lever, vinyl the green that hospitals retired in the eighties, cracked along the seat and mended along the cracks with tape in a competent crosshatch. It had been leveled; the slab up there has a fall of a couple of centimeters toward the drain lines that were never fitted, and someone had shimmed the base with cut tile until the headrest sat true. Kanda had told me the old chairs were better. Heavier. Someone had carried this one up forty-four floors, and by the care in the shimming, more than one person, and they had agreed on where it should sit. Beside it, at a distance I put at twelve centimeters, stood a wooden chair, the armless kind provincial schools buy by the hundred, angled a few degrees toward where the patient's head would be. Twelve centimeters is a talking distance. It is roughly the distance from which, for nine years, a hygienist's stool had been placed beside my own right ear.

I heard the broom before I saw him. A dry rhythmic hiss, unhurried, the temple kind of broom, twigs bound to a long handle, and when I moved the light toward it I caught an old man in trousers and an undershirt sweeping the slab in the dark, moving dust toward the open edge with the long patience of a man who has swept this floor before and will sweep it again, neither of which facts depends on the dust. He did not startle. He glanced at my light, once, and I lowered it to the floor between us without deciding to — some residue of greng jai that survives even at 2:47 in a dead tower — and my phone, in lowering, showed me the time, and the screen behind the time was dark. Sixty-sixth night. I put it in my pocket.

"Ajarn Direk sends a message," I said. "He says he still owes you for a molar."

The broom finished its stroke. The old man considered the dust at the edge, or the river past it.

"He does," he said.

That was all. He resumed. The hiss went along the slab and back, and I stood next to a dental chair on the forty-fourth floor of an unfinished tower at close to three in the morning while an old man swept, and it should have been the strangest room of my life and it was not even the strangest room of my week, and something about that arithmetic loosened a knot I had been carrying since August.

I walked around the chair once. There was no compressor, no plumbing, no line to it at all; the pump was mechanical and the floor under it was clean. I put my hand on the headrest, which was cold.

"Is it connected to anything?" I said.

The broom stopped.

"Not tonight," he said. "You're still asking with the wrong verb."

He said it without turning around, in the tone Kanda uses to tell me to raise a left hand — procedural, kind, admitting no appeal — and then the broom started again, and I understood that the consultation was over, and that there had been a consultation. I did not ask his name. He did not ask mine. Between the garlands and the molar there was clearly a ledger up here older than my arrival, and I have learned this year to stop opening ledgers I am not named in.

I stayed at the edge. Not the rebar edge — one column bay back, hand on a baluster that held up nothing, the wind coming through at body temperature. Below and out to every horizon was Bangkok at three in the morning, and I let myself look at it, and for the first time since the twenty-first of August — for the first time, if I am honest in the way this notebook has been teaching me to be, in some years longer than that — I looked at it without checking it for anything. The river went past the tower's feet, black and businesslike, pushing a barge train upstream at a walking pace. The sister tower's dome stood gold a kilometer down, lit for nobody. Sathorn ran its double string of lights east, and past it the elevated tracks lay pale and empty, waiting for the first train without anxiety, certain of it. So was the rest of the city, mostly without knowing what it was waiting for. Klong Toey port burned sodium-orange along the bend, cranes at rest over the containers; somewhere inside that orange was a converted cold-storage building holding thirty-eight women and a counter that never reaches zero, and somewhere north of it a wok was cooling, a dispensary was shuttered, a call-center tower stood dark with one floor of cleaners moving through it. Ten thousand 7-Elevens printing time and change. Nine hundred temples with their lights banked. Twelve million people, most of them asleep, every one of them a column of dates on the left and numbers on the right, kept by nobody, adding up anyway. The city did not know I was looking. It was not performing. It simply ran — the honest fish, the swept slab — at full detail, at three in the morning, for no observer at all — and I stood on the forty-fourth floor of an interrupted sentence with a cracked lens and a carton of eight eggs, and I loved it. Without irony, without instruments, without a control group. The love asked for nothing back and proved nothing and paid nothing, one unit, ฿0.00, and behind me the broom went on with its accounting, and for a while, standing there, I asked the city no questions at all.
