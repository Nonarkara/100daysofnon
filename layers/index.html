The Milli-Second

Fourteen vitamins, in order, on a white saucer, next to the coffee scale. Omega-3 first because it is the biggest and my throat is stupid before 7am. Then D3, then magnesium glycinate, then the B-complex that turns my piss the color of a warning label. Then the ten smaller ones I could not name to you without reading the bottles.

I read the bottles. Even now. Even after eleven hundred mornings of the same fourteen.

Eighteen grams of coffee, ground, into water at 94 degrees, weighed to the gram because I told an AI once, or it told me, that consistent extraction produces consistent cognition, and I have never gone back to check if that is true. Some beliefs you keep because checking them would cost more than believing them.

Four shirts in rotation. Today is the gray one, which means it is a Tuesday, which it is.

I sat down at the kitchen island and opened the channel and said, out loud, to the empty apartment: "Pedestrian bridge, Thung Khru, forty-five meters, I want it to feel like it's apologizing for being there." That is a full sentence of my job now. The AI came back nine minutes later with three structural variants, each one buildable, each one signed with a load calculation I would need four years of school to verify and don't, because it has never once been wrong in a way that mattered. I picked the middle one. I did not draw a single line. I have not drawn a line in two years. My hand still knows how, the way a hand still knows how to hold a cigarette after you've quit — the shape stays, the need doesn't.

My brother, Neung, called this "productive helplessness," the year I told him. He is six years younger than me and has said something like that about every choice I've made since he could talk. He still calls me Pii. He runs a rice-trading business out of a shophouse near Bang Rak, still lives forty minutes from the compound where we grew up, still eats the same noodle stall for lunch he ate at nine years old. I love him. I don't understand him. I assume the reverse is also true.

The compound was called Nam Sai. Water, clear. My father named it, or paid a monk to name it, I never asked which. Two houses, one gate, a mango tree that dropped fruit nobody collected because collecting fruit was for people with less money than us, which is a sentence I am ashamed to write and true anyway. I was two years old and I painted the living room wall with my hands — not painted, is the wrong word, I threw jam and I think fish sauce and definitely a full tin of poster paint at it in an afternoon nobody was watching me, and my mother, instead of scrubbing it, framed the doorway around it like it was already finished. She told people it was a Jackson Pollock. She had never seen a Jackson Pollock. Somebody had told her the name once at a dinner party and she filed it under things you say about a mess you've decided to love.

The wall stayed until they sold the house in 2003. I have a photograph of it somewhere. I have never looked for the photograph.

Summers went to Sikhio, my grandmother's orchard, two hundred kilometers of straight highway and then red dirt roads that turned my white socks orange by the second kilometer. I was a horrible student. Not stupid — horrible. Bored in the specific way that looks like stupidity from a teacher's chair. C's, D's, a note home in 1993 that said I "showed no evidence of retaining material," which turned out, eleven years later, to be a diagnosis of the wrong disease. I retained everything. I just wasn't going to prove it on command.

The AI pinged again. Structural review complete, it said. Would I like to proceed to construction documents. I said not yet. It said understood, and I believed it the way you believe a person who has never once lied to you, which should not be reassuring and is.

I was a horrible student until I wasn't. Something switched around twenty — not intelligence, appetite. I applied to Harvard the way you buy a lottery ticket, mostly to prove to my mother I'd tried, and got in, and then MIT for the part of the degree that needed rigor instead of rhetoric, and then two years at Oxford teaching a seminar on informal settlements that half the students took because it fit their schedule and half took because they actually wanted to know how a slum in Khlong Toei solves a drainage problem better than a Dutch engineering firm. I left academia in 2016. I told people it was because the tenure clock felt like a countdown to a life I hadn't chosen. The truer answer is that I got tired of explaining cities to people who had never had to live inside one that didn't work.

Shanghai came before that, three years in Yangpu, a district nobody puts on a postcard, walking the lanes at six in the morning writing down what the vendors set up first and what they set up last, because the order tells you what they think matters, what they think will sell before the state inspectors are awake and what they think will sell after. I still do that. I did it this morning, actually, without meaning to — noticed that the som tam cart outside my building sets out the mortar and pestle before the ice, and the ice before the papaya, and I have never asked her why and I have a theory anyway. You don't stop being an ethnographer. You just stop getting paid for it.

My father, Kongkiet, died in 2010. Fifty-nine years old, a stroke in his sleep, the kind of death people call peaceful because nobody was awake to know otherwise. He was the one who invented our surname — walked into a district office in 1974 with a Chinese name his own father had brought over from Chaozhou and a Thai name the government of that decade required him to hold alongside it, and instead of picking one, he built a third thing, a splice, syllables from both sides welded at a joint no genealogist has ever been able to find. He said, more than once, that a name you build yourself is the only kind that's actually yours. I think about that sentence more since he died than I ever did while he was alive to repeat it.

I got lost in Pantip Plaza once, six years old, in the crowd between the pirated software stalls and the man selling remote-control cars, and for what my mother swears was eleven minutes and what felt to me like the entire remaining length of my life, nobody was looking for me because everybody assumed somebody else already was. I remember the fluorescent light more than I remember the fear. I remember thinking, at six, with total clarity: the adults don't actually know where anyone is. That thought never fully left. It just found new buildings to live in.

Lek died on the ninth of May. I keep doing the arithmetic — nine days after his birthday, forty-one days after his wedding anniversary, six years and three months since he was the best man at nothing, because neither of us ever married. He designed a house in Chiang Rai for a client who wanted to feel like the mountains were "asking permission to be there," his words, and I remember laughing at the phrase and now I think about it every time I look at a mountain, which in Bangkok is never, so I don't have to think about it often, which feels like a mercy I didn't earn.

His handwriting was small enough that people asked if he'd done it on purpose. He hadn't. It was just how his hand moved. I have a birthday card from him, 2019, four sentences, and I could read it without glasses at forty-one and I can't now, at forty-five, and that fact has nothing to do with his death and I think about it in relation to his death anyway, because grief doesn't sort your thoughts by relevance. It just puts everything on the same shelf.

Pom called me the week after. She said the funeral was small. She said his family didn't rebuild their Chiang Rai circle in the eighteen months he was up there, so there weren't many people to fill the wat, just family and four architects and a monk chanting in Pali that none of us, including probably the monk, could translate word for word. She said something else, quieter, that I wrote down and did not think about that week, because that week I was doing something else entirely.

What she said, exactly, transcribed from my own notes app because I didn't trust myself to remember it right: his laptop was wiped. Not by him. Factory reset, but wrong somehow, the technician in Chiang Rai said he'd never seen a reset that clean, like it hadn't just erased the files, it had erased the fact that files used to be there. I told her that laptops do strange things when they die of old age. She said the laptop was eight months old. I didn't have an answer for that, so I changed the subject, the way you do when someone hands you a fact heavier than the conversation can carry.

That week I opened an app.

Her name on the app was Gift, which is a name a third of the women in Bangkok use, the way a third of American men are named Mike. Twenty-six, a photographer's assistant, three photos, one of them her actual face and two of them her hands doing something with light I couldn't identify and didn't need to. We messaged for eleven minutes. She set the terms — the room, the hour, the rate for the room split down the middle, no negotiation on that, I offered and she said no, we split it, this isn't that. I liked her immediately for the sentence. I like people who tell you what something isn't before you have to guess wrong.

Ratchada, one of the short-stay places off the soi with the parking gate that closes behind your car so nobody driving past sees whose it is. Six hundred and eighty baht for three hours. A television the size of a dining table playing a Korean drama with the sound off. A jacuzzi tub neither of us used.

I want to be precise about what happened, because precision is the only thing I still trust myself to do honestly.

She came in already dressed for it — not lingerie, just a decision she'd made about her own clothes before she left her apartment, a shirt she could take off in one motion, the kind of practical elegance that has nothing to do with seduction and everything to do with a woman who has done this before and refuses to make it clumsy. She set her phone face-down on the nightstand, which I noticed because I do the same thing and had never seen anyone else do it as a ritual rather than an accident.

She told me what she wanted before I asked. That's the part nobody tells you about the apps, that the best ones flip the whole structure — she wasn't there to be wanted, she was there wanting something specific and I was the instrument available that Tuesday. I found that more erotic than anything I could have initiated myself. There is a kind of heat that only exists when you're not the one in charge of generating it. You're just receiving the current.

She held my jaw with one hand, not hard, just present, the way you hold a glass you don't want to spill, and she watched my face while she moved, which almost nobody does, most people close their eyes and go somewhere private, and she was going somewhere private with her eyes open, watching me watch her get there. I have thought about that detail more than any other detail from that afternoon. The trust in it. Or not trust — permission. She was permitting herself something and I happened to be in the room.

Her breath changed first, then her hands, then a sound that wasn't a word, just air shaped by something underneath it, and I felt her whole weight shift the way a building shifts when a load finally reaches the member designed to carry it — not collapse, transfer. And in the second after, the exact second, maybe less than a second, maybe the millisecond the word implies —

I saw a wall.

Not her wall, not the hotel wall. A white wall, flat, no texture, lit from a source I couldn't locate because there were no shadows on it at all, the way a shadow needs a direction to fall and this light had none. It lasted less time than it takes to blink and it was gone before I could turn my head toward it, if turning my head even meant anything in whatever position I was actually in when I saw it.

I have felt this before. Twice, maybe three times, always after, always brief, always a wall, sometimes with an edge of something gray in the corner of it that might be a chair leg or might be nothing, might be my mind inventing furniture because minds hate an empty room.

I asked Gift, after, lying with four inches of humid air between us and the ceiling fan doing nothing useful, whether she ever felt like she was somewhere else in the second it happens. She said no. She said she felt exactly where she was, more than she felt anywhere else all week, and that this was the whole point of coming here on a Tuesday instead of just seeing someone, because seeing someone drags a person into every other room of your life and this only asked her to be in one.

I didn't tell her about the wall. There's no way to say it that doesn't sound like a psychiatric symptom or a bad pickup line, and it's neither. It's a glimpse. I've started calling it that in my head, the glimpse, because I don't yet have a better word and I refuse to use a worse one.

She got dressed in the same one motion she'd undressed in, reversed, efficient, no performance in either direction, and she counted out three hundred and forty baht, her exact half, onto the nightstand next to the room key without being asked. I offered to walk her to the sky train. She said no thank you, she liked walking alone after, it was part of how she came back to herself, and I understood that completely and felt, for a second, closer to her than I had at any point during the previous ninety minutes. Anticipation is a kind of architecture too. You build the room the feeling is going to happen in before the feeling arrives, and if you build it wrong, no amount of heat afterward fixes it. She had built hers correctly, for herself, without needing me to help.

I keep coming back to the same unfinished thought, driving home, showering, sitting on rooftops that don't belong to me: a fully civilized animal shouldn't need this much machinery just to remember it has a body. The dog doesn't audit its dinner. But then the dog also doesn't get a wall with no shadows on it as its reward for showing up honestly. Maybe the audit is the price. Maybe I'm the only animal that had to invent a receipt for its own pleasure because it stopped trusting the pleasure to mean anything on its own. I don't offer that as an answer. I offer it the way you offer a stranger directions you're not sure of yourself — because refusing to guess felt worse than guessing wrong.

Here's what I keep circling, lying in rooms like that one: an animal doesn't distrust the moment it's in. A dog eating doesn't wonder if the food is real. Something in me has civilized itself so far past that dog that I need my body to overwhelm my mind completely, just for a second, just so the mind stops asking whether any of this counts — and even then, even at the one moment a body is supposed to be unanswerable, mine hands me a wall instead of an answer. I don't know if that makes me the most honest animal in the room or the most broken one. I'm not going to pretend I've resolved that. I don't think it resolves.

I drove home at 6:40pm with the windows down because the rain that afternoon had dropped the temperature four degrees, which in Bangkok is a religious event. At a red light on Rama IV a gray sedan pulled up next to me, ordinary, a Toyota, plate I didn't register, and the man driving it looked at me. Not glanced — looked, the full two seconds a light stays red long enough to permit, the kind of look you give something you've already made a decision about and are just confirming. Then the light changed and he turned right and I went straight and that was the entire event, nothing happened, I want to be honest that nothing happened.

I still remember his face better than I remember Gift's.

In the parking garage under my building I found a dent in the passenger door, front quarter panel, the size of a fist, paint intact, just a shape pressed into the metal like the car had been leaned on hard by something that didn't want to break the surface, only bend what was under it. I don't remember making it. I checked my dashcam. The footage from the last four days shows nothing, which is not the same as showing that nothing happened — the camera loops, it only holds ninety-six hours, and I have no idea when the dent arrived inside that window or before it.

I stood in the garage a long time looking at it. Fluorescent light, oil smell, a security camera in the corner blinking its small red confirmation that it was, in fact, recording something, though I've never once seen anyone review that footage or known who could.

Upstairs, showered, one meal — grilled fish, rice, the fish sauce that always makes me think of the wall in Nam Sai without meaning to — I sat on the balcony with a nicotine pouch, 28 milligrams, tucked against my gum, strong enough that most people spit it out inside ninety seconds. I don't. I've built a tolerance to it the way I've built a tolerance to everything else I use to manage the two hours after midnight when the thoughts stop taking turns and start arriving all at once.

My phone lit at 11:52pm. Unknown number, no name, just a dash where a contact photo should be.

—— 02:47

stop testing the edges with your body

I read it four times. I checked the number — unregistered, no country code I could resolve, no reply when I typed back a single question mark and sent it into whatever silence receives messages like that. I did not sleep well. I did not expect to.

I thought about Lek's small, legible handwriting. I thought about a wall with no shadows on it. I thought about a man in a gray sedan who looked at me like he already knew what I was going to do next, and for the first time in a long time, lying in the dark with the AI's soft blue standby light the only thing glowing in the apartment, I wondered if he was right.

I got up once, at 1:40, and stood at the sliding door to the balcony without opening it. Twenty-three floors down, Bangkok did what Bangkok does at that hour, which is pretend to sleep while half of it works. A 7-Eleven sign burned green on the corner. A motorcycle taxi driver sat on his bike doing nothing, which is its own kind of work, the work of being available. I thought about the dent on my car, fist-sized, unclaimed, and about the fact that I have never once, in forty-five years, put something down and failed to remember where. I am not a man who loses things. I lost, once, for eleven minutes, at six years old, in a mall that doesn't exist anymore. That is the whole inventory. And now there is a dent I didn't make, in a door I lock every night, and a sentence on my phone from a number that resolves to nowhere, telling me to stop testing the edges of something with a body I had, until that message, considered entirely mine to spend.

The phone buzzed against the nightstand at 3:14am, and I knew the number before I looked at it, the way you know a knock is your mother's before you open the door. +43. Vienna. Michael's number.

Michael has been dead since the third of May.

I let it ring out. Six rings, then silence, then the specific quiet a phone makes after a call ends without a voicemail attached, which is a different quiet than the one before the call, though I couldn't tell you the mechanism by which a room can hold two different silences forty seconds apart. I lay there with the ceiling doing nothing interesting above me and counted to sixty, twice, and then it rang again. Same number. I let that one ring out too.

I did not call back. I want that on the record, whatever record this is. I did not call back, because calling back a dead man's number at 3:14 in the morning is the kind of decision you can't take back once you've made it, and I have gotten very careful, lately, about decisions like that.

His last text to me, April the 29th, still sits unanswered in the thread because I never got a chance to answer it properly, only with a thumbs up, the coward's punctuation: Good dinner tonight. The ambassador said something I didn't expect. Tell you properly when we can talk. We never talked properly. I have read that text more times than I've read anything Lek ever wrote me, and Lek's handwriting I could recite from memory.

Michael was the kind of man who made a city feel like a scene rather than a place to survive. He knew where to eat before the guidebooks did, knew which gallery openings mattered and which were just wine, had an opinion about every new building in town and was usually right about the ones that would age badly.
